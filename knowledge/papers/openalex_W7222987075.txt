Marginal Specification and Tail Dependence in Equity 
Markets 

Haozhou He* 
Business school, Monash University, Caulfield East, Australia 
*Corresponding author: Haozhou He. 

Abstract 
Copula conclusions for equity markets depend on both marginal filtering and the temporal pairing of daily 
returns. We analyse S&P 500, FTSE 100, and Nikkei 225 returns from 2010 to 2025 in three pairwise panels. 
A rolling 756 -day training and 63 -day evaluation design compares empirical -CDF, Gaussian-GARCH, and 
Student-t-GARCH margins. The GARCH filters use a standard one-step-ahead recursion, and every candidate 
copula is estimated by pseudo maximum likelihood. The Student -t copula has the highest window -weighted 
density score under all three margins, with scores of 0.0924, 0.0703, and 0.0685. It is the numerical winner in 
eight of nine panel–margin cells, but paired moving-block intervals support a positive advantage in only three 
cells. A lead –lag check materially changes the S&P 500 –Nikkei 225 association, while a joint -downside 
probability score does not select the same family as the density score. The evid ence therefore supports 
specification-aware conditional -dependence analysis, rather than an invariant model ranking or causal 
contagion claim. 
Keywords 
copula models, cross-market dependence, tail dependence, GARCH filtering, temporal alignment 

1. Introduction 
Equity indices can co -move strongly in ordinary periods and in joint downturns. Linear correlation alone 
does not distinguish these forms of association. Copulas separate a dependence distribution from fitted 
univariate margins and can represent symmetric or asymmetric tail behaviour [1]. Financial applications 
consequently combine copulas with volatility filters, dynamic -correlation models, and rolling estimation [2 –
5]. Yet a dependence result can inherit properties of the marginal transformation used to create the pseudo -
observations. 
This issue is important for two reasons. First, volatility clustering and heavy tails change the probability -
integral transform entering a copula fit [6]. A conclusion obtained after one filter may therefore not survive a 
different, plausible filter. Secon d, matching two closing returns by calendar date does not guarantee that the 
two returns reflect the same information set. The Japanese close precedes the US close, and the European close 
also precedes it. Calendar matching can therefore conceal lead–lag dependence, especially for US–Asia pairs.

Four choices are consequently entangled in a typical cross -market exercise: the return -pairing rule, the 
marginal filter, the copula family, and the loss function used to compare fitted models. Each choice is 
defensible in isolation. Their interaction is harder to assess because a change in pseudo-observations can alter 
the fitted parameter, the implied tail probability, and the held -out score at the same time. A density -score 
winner should therefore be interpreted conditional on the pairing and marginal assumptions that produced the 
score. The same caution applies to an apparent difference in tail dependence across regions. 
This paper asks how marginal specification and temporal pairing affect rolling copula evidence for the S&P 
500, FTSE 100, and Nikkei 225. It holds the candidate family set and out -of-sample protocol fixed, while 
comparing empirical-CDF, Gaussian-GARCH, and Student-𝑡-GARCH margins. The primary contribution is 
not a new copula family. It is a reproducible robustness analysis with standard one -step-ahead GARCH 
filtering, common pseudo-maximum-likelihood estimation, uncertainty intervals for score differences, and a 
joint-downside prediction metric. 
The analysis is designed around a simple counterfactual. If a family is genuinely robust to reasonable 
marginal choices, it should retain a comparable score advantage when the pseudo-observations are regenerated 
by another plausible pipeline. If the advant age disappears, changes sign, or is statistically unresolved, the 
appropriate scientific result is sensitivity rather than a forced winner. Similarly, if a lead –lag pairing changes 
the association, the result should be reported as temporal -alignment sensitivity. It should not be transformed 
into an informal causal story about market transmission. 
The results are deliberately qualified. The Student -𝑡 copula is the numerical density -score leader after all 
three margins, but its lead over the closest competitor is not statistically positive in most panel –margin cells. 
A right-market lead–lag shift substantially changes the S&P 500–Nikkei 225 association. The study therefore 
concerns conditional association under stated specifications. It does not identify contagion, directed spillovers, 
portfolio profitability, or systemic risk. Those questions require additional identification or decision designs 
[7–9]. 
2. Related Work 
Copula studies document non-linear and tail association in stock, commodity, foreign -exchange, sectoral, 
and technology-equity markets [10–14]. Copula–GARCH formulations model conditional marginal volatility 
before estimating a separate dependence layer [4]. Conditional copula work also formalises time-varying and 
asymmetric dependence [15]. Other work introduces time variation through GAS or DCC -type dynamics [5, 
16], and flexible vine or regime -switching structures extend the family set [17]. These studies show why tail 
dependence is useful, but they commonly select one marginal specification before comparing dependence 
families. 
The resulting methodological gap is narrower. Marginal transformation affects the pseudo -observations, 
whereas the copula comparison is usually interpreted as if only the dependence family had changed. Heavy -
tailed marginal transformations can alter tail -risk conclusions even with a fixed Gaussian copula [6]. Our 
design therefore fixes the copula set, rolling schedule, and scoring rule, and changes only the marginal filter 
or temporal pairing. This isolates specification sensitivity from a claim of universal model superiority. 
The comparison also requires a common estimation target. A correlation inversion for an elliptical copula 
and a Kendall’s-𝜏 inversion for an Archimedean copula can be useful descriptive estimators, but they do not 
make a family contest identical when evaluation is based on predictive density. We instead fit every family by 
pseudo maximum likelihood and then evaluate the same h eld-out density. This does not remove model 
misspecification, but it ensures that the family label is not confounded with a different parameter-training rule. 
Estimating the Student-𝑡 copula degrees of freedom further prevents the primary comparison from assuming 
the same tail thickness in every rolling block. 
Forecast comparison presents a second distinction. A full copula density rewards accurate probability 
allocation across the entire unit square. A risk manager may instead care about a small lower-left region, such 
as the probability that both standardized returns fall below their fifth percentiles. These are related but non -
identical tasks. Reporting both metrics makes it possible to identify whether an aggregate density result carries 
over to a concrete joint-downside scenario.

This distinction also separates conditional dependence from contagion. Event studies and causal -network 
approaches use structural breaks, shocks, or directed assumptions to study transmission [7, 8]. A rolling 
observational copula fit does not contain thos e elements. We use joint -downside probabilities as a forecast -
evaluation scenario, not as evidence of a causal risk channel. 
3. Method 
3.1 Data and Rolling Design 
We use archived public adjusted closes for the S&P 500, FTSE 100, and Nikkei 225 from 1 January 2010 
to 31 December 2025. Daily returns are 𝑟𝑡 = log(𝑃𝑡/𝑃𝑡−1). Common -date pairing yields 3,946 S&P 500 –
FTSE 100 returns, 3,778 S&P 500–Nikkei 225 returns, and 3,803 FTSE 100–Nikkei 225 returns. The primary 
design fits each model on 756 returns and evaluates the next 63. Origins advance by 63 returns, producing 50, 
47, and 48 blocks, respectively. Raw responses and SHA-256 hashes are retained with the experiment. 
Calendar alignment is not an information -time assumption. For each pair, a temporal robustness check 
shifts the right -hand market by −1, 0, and +1 common trading days before calculating association and the 
Student-𝑡-copula score. Thus, a positive shift pairs the left -market return at 𝑡 with the right-market return at 
the next common trading day. 
The lag convention is a robustness device, not a structural model of price discovery. It retains the same 
return construction and evaluates a limited set of adjacent common trading days. Holidays, exchange-specific 
closing conventions, and intraday news ti ming are not modelled. A large change under a shift nevertheless 
signals that the zero-lag calendar pair should not be treated as an innocuous benchmark.

Three pipelines generate pseudo -observations in (0,1). The empirical -CDF pipeline ranks test returns 
against the training distribution, using a half -rank correction and denominator 𝑛 + 1, where 𝑛 is the training-
sample size. The parametric pipelines use the stationary GARCH(1,1) formulation [2],

Gaussian GARCH uses a Gaussian likelihood and normal probability transform. Student-𝑡 GARCH uses a 
unit-variance Student likelihood and transform. The primary Student -𝑡 margin uses six degrees of freedom, 
while 𝜈 ∈ {4,6,8,10} is examined as a sensitivity check. Parameters are re-estimated in every training block.

The two scores answer different questions. Density scoring evaluates the fitted copula at every held -out 
pseudo-observation. The event score discards the magnitude of non -event observations and focuses on 
calibration of a specified joint downside event. Ne ither score is a portfolio return, value -at-risk, or expected-
shortfall result. In particular, the analysis does not combine indices in different currencies into an investment 
portfolio, so it makes no international allocation claim. 
For each panel –margin cell, the highest mean density -score model is compared with its runner -up using 
paired moving-block resampling of rolling score differences. We report 2,000 replicates, block length four, 
and a 95% percentile interval. Tail-dependence paths are descriptive robustness summaries. Lower dispersion 
is not treated as an intrinsic model improvement because genuine dependence can change over time. 
4. Results 
4.1 Density-score Ranking and Its Uncertainty 
At the 756-day window, the Student-𝑡 copula has the highest window-weighted density score under every 
marginal pipeline (Table 1). The ranking is therefore numerically robust at the aggregate level. However, the 
size and inferential status of the advantage depend on the panel. Student -𝑡 is the numerical winner in eight of 
nine panel –margin cells, whereas its paired moving -block interval is strictly positive in only three. The 
remaining six intervals include zero. Aggregate ranking should thus be read as a useful summary, not as proof 
of uniformly superior density forecasts. 
The positive intervals arise in specific conditions rather than throughout the design. Under empirical -CDF 
margins, the Student - 𝑡 lead is 0.0289 over Gumbel for S&P 500 –FTSE 100, with a 95% interval of 
[0.0106,0.0518], and 0.0186 over Clayton for S&P 500 –Nikkei 225, with [0.0021,0.0415]. Under Student-
𝑡 GARCH, the S&P 500 –FTSE 100 Student -𝑡–Gaussian difference is 0.0063, with [0.0013,0.0116]. These 
cases support a local predictive advantage. They do not justify carrying the same claim to the remaining 
conditions, where the closest-family interval crosses zero.

The distinction is economically intuitive but should not be over -read. The full -density score uses both 
central and tail observations, so it can prefer a flexible symmetric family even when a lower-tail-only event is 
more accurately calibrated by Clayton. Conversely, the event score is based on a rare binary outcome and is 
not a general test of distributional fit. The two metrics together establish a bounded result: model choice 
depends on the predictive target, not only on the marginal filter.

Temporal alignment is consequential. For S&P 500 –Nikkei 225, same-date Pearson correlation is 0.1723, 
but pairing the S&P 500 return with the next Nikkei return raises it to 0.4469. The corresponding Student -𝑡-
copula mean density score rises from 0.0206 to 0.1355. The direction of the shift is consistent with the ordering 
of market closes, but it is not a causal estimate. It demonstrates that same -date matching is an empirical 
convention rather than a neutral information -time choice. Other pairs also change under lead –lag shifts, 
although less sharply. 
The contrast is not limited to a simple correlation statistic. For the S&P 500–FTSE 100 pair, the same-date 
Student-𝑡 score is 0.1341, compared with 0.0011 under a negative shift and 0.0196 under a positive shift. For 
FTSE 100–Nikkei 225, the corresponding values are 0.0471, 0.0010, and 0.0428. These patterns show that the 
inferred dependence is sensitive to the observat ion schedule itself. They also motivate reporting the pair 
definition alongside the copula specification in cross-market applications.

5. Discussion 
The revised analysis changes the interpretation of the earlier aggregate finding. Student -𝑡 remains the 
numerical density-score leader after three marginal pipelines, which is consistent with heavy-tailed joint-return 
behaviour. Yet numerical leadership is not a uniform statistical advantage. Six of nine winner –runner-up 
intervals include zero, and Clayton is preferred for the specific joint -downside score. The appropriate 
conclusion is specification -aware model comparison, not a claim that one copula is best for every financial 
purpose. 
The lead–lag evidence is equally important. A same-date US–Japan return pair combines markets that close 
at different points in the global trading day. The strong change under a positive Nikkei shift shows that calendar 
alignment alone does not solve this problem. Researchers should report the temporal pairing rule and test 
plausible alternatives before attributing a low same-date dependence estimate to a structural difference between 
markets. 
The evidence also reframes the role of tail-dependence dispersion. A smoother rolling tail-dependence path 
is not automatically preferable, because genuine joint risk can vary during stress or policy changes. The 
relevant robustness question is whether marginal choices materially alter the estimated path and the associated 
predictive ranking. In this study they do alter both, although the aggregate Student -𝑡 density ranking remains 
intact. This interpretation preserves the negative or inconclusive comparisons as substantive information rather 
than treating them as failed support for a preferred filter. 
For practical use, the joint -downside exercise provides a more direct bridge from a fitted copula to a 
financial scenario. Its result does not select the same model as the full -density criterion. A practitioner must 
therefore state the decision target before translating a statistical dependence fit into a risk forecast. The present 
data do not include common-currency portfolio construction, transaction costs, or a trading rule. Consequently, 
the scenario result should be understood as probabilistic calibration of co-downside events, not an investment 
recommendation. 
Several boundaries remain. The analysis uses daily closes and three bivariate pairs, so it does not describe 
an entire market network. The candidate set omits asymmetric two -tail families, regime switching, and 
dynamic copulas [17, 18]. The Student-𝑡 marginal sensitivity grid does not replace full marginal goodness-of-
fit testing, and the bootstrap assesses pairwise ranking differences rather than a complete model -confidence

set [19]. Finally, joint downside probabilities are predictive quantities under an observational design. They do 
not identify spillovers, causal contagion, systemic risk, or investment gains. 
6. Conclusion 
This study examined how marginal filtering and temporal alignment shape copula -based cross -market 
dependence evidence. With standard one -step-ahead GARCH filtering and common pseudo -maximum-
likelihood estimation, the Student -𝑡 copula had the largest aggregate density score under empirical -CDF, 
Gaussian-GARCH, and Student-𝑡-GARCH margins. Its advantage was nevertheless statistically unresolved in 
most panel–margin comparisons, and the joint -downside scenario favoured Clayton. The lead –lag analysis 
further showed that S&P 500 –Nikkei 225 dependence changes materially when the pairing respects market 
timing. The evidence supports specification -aware conditional dependence, not a universal ranking or causal 
co-movement claim.
