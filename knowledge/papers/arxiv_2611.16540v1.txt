Abstract
Portfolio outcomes may depend as much on trading restrictions as on how returns are modeled.
We compared historical moving-window returns with conditional scenarios from ARMA–GARCH
models and a Student-t copula for 30 U.S.-listed exchange-traded funds. Matched mean–variance
and conditional value-at-risk portfolios were evaluated under long-only and limited long–short
bounds over 946 sessions. Historical portfolios had higher observed reward–risk ratios in most
comparisons, but no paired difference remained significant after multiple-testing adjustment.
Conditional scenarios did not consistently reduce realized downside risk. We distinguished
changes in target weights from trades required to rebalance holdings after market movements.
Relaxing the target-adjustment limit changed exposures, turnover, and some returns more than
varying scenario count or seed in the examined tests. These findings suggest that assessments of
conditional return inputs should consider portfolio constraints and trading requirements.
Keywords: exchange-traded funds; portfolio optimization; financial risk management; return
scenarios; conditional value at risk; turnover constraints; transaction costs
1 Introduction
Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) allow investors to combine equity, fixed-income, real-asset, and
alternative exposures. The outcomes of an ETF portfolio depend partly on the returns and risks
used to choose the holdings, and partly on the limits placed on changing those holdings. Both
considerations matter when market volatility and dependence vary over time (Markowitz, 1952;
Gastineau, 2010; Cont, 2001; Longin and Solnik, 2001).
A historical approach uses the returns observed in a moving estimation window. A conditional
approach estimates recent return dynamics and generates possible outcomes for the next period.
Conditional scenarios can represent changing volatility and joint losses, but their use also introduces
estimation and simulation uncertainty; more detailed return modeling does not necessarily improve
realized investment results (Michaud, 1989; Best and Grauer, 1991; Chopra and Ziemba, 1993;
DeMiguel et al., 2009). Related JRFM studies consider heavy-tailed commodity ETF portfolios
(Appiah et al., 2026) and the role of tail risk and trading costs in predominantly actively managed

ETFs (Lamptey et al., 2026). The present study examines a broad multi-asset ETF universe under
matched portfolio construction rules, with particular attention to the distinction between desired
target weights and the trades required to maintain them.
We compared historical moving-window inputs with conditional scenarios generated from ARMA–
GARCH marginal models and a multivariate Student-t copula. Both approaches were applied to
the same 30 ETFs using mean–variance and conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) objectives, long-only
and limited long–short bounds, equal-weight starting allocations, and the same baseline target-
adjustment limit. Realized portfolio returns were calculated from subsequent observed ETF returns,
not from the simulated scenarios. Holding the portfolio rules fixed made the two input methods
more directly comparable within the stated design, although a restrictive trading rule could also
limit the changes either method could make to its holdings.
The paper makes three empirical contributions. First, it compares the realized returns, downside
risk, and reward–risk measures of matched historical and conditional portfolios, using paired
bootstrap inference to describe uncertainty in their differences. Second, it distinguishes adjustments
between successive target weights from the rebalancing trades required after market returns cause
holdings to drift. This provides a separate view of portfolio turnover, exposures, and modeled trading
costs. Third, it examines whether the observed comparisons change when the daily adjustment limit,
simulated scenario count, random seed, or estimation-window length is varied. The paper does not
propose a new return model or optimization criterion. Its contribution is the empirical assessment
of how return-input construction interacts with the rules governing portfolio holdings and trading.
The evaluation covers 946 trading sessions from December 2022 to September 2026. Execution
prices, market impact, and short-position financing were not observed, so reported performance
follows a close-to-close return-attribution convention with illustrative proportional costs. Section 2
describes the data, Section 3 presents the portfolio methods, Section 4 reports the empirical results,
and Sections 5 and 6 discuss their implications and limitations.
2 Data and Portfolio Design
The analysis included 30 U.S.-listed ETFs spanning equities, fixed income, income-oriented and
multi-asset funds, real assets, and alternative strategies (Appendix Table A1). This selection
provided different sources of market exposure, although several funds held overlapping underlying
assets. The resulting portfolios were therefore diversified across ETF tickers, not necessarily across
independent economic risks.
The adjusted closing prices of the 30 U.S.-listed ETFs were downloaded from Yahoo Finance on
27 October 2026 using Python’s yfinance interface. The download requested daily observations from
19 December 2018 (inclusive) to 1 October 2026 (exclusive), with auto_adjust=True, actions=True,
threads=False, and progress=False. Under the specified adjustment option, the returned Close
field contains Yahoo Finance’s adjusted closing prices, incorporating its treatment of distributions
and splits. The corporate-action fields were requested but not separately archived in the analysis
workbook; the provider’s adjustment factors were not independently reconstructed.
The retrieved price columns were ordered to match the fixed 30-ETF universe. The common
panel was formed by deleting dates missing a price for any ETF, using dropna(); no interpolation,
forward filling, or other imputation was applied. Examination of the archived workbook found
2
that its Full_Price_Panel and Adjusted_Prices sheets were identical and contained no missing
or nonpositive prices. The panel comprised 1,955 price observations from 19 December 2018 to
30 September 2026 and 1,954 one-session arithmetic returns. Its trading dates were compared with
the New York Stock Exchange session calendar; no missing, extra, or duplicated sessions were
identified. Recalculating every arithmetic return from successive adjusted prices agreed with the
saved return sheet to numerical precision.
As a limited check of corporate-action treatment, issuer and exchange records were examined for
the one-for-three reverse split of SRET effective 20 December 2022, the BYLD distribution dated
2 September 2026, and the IYG distribution dated 15 June 2026. These events fell on dates present
in the archived panel, and the adjusted SRET series showed no mechanical threefold jump across
the split. The checks did not independently validate every historical dividend or split factor: raw
unadjusted closing prices and corporate-action fields were not saved with this workbook. The study
therefore used the provider-adjusted return convention rather than an independently reconstructed
total-return index.
For ETF i, the daily arithmetic return was calculated as
ri,t = Pi,t Pi,t−1 − 1, (1)
where Pi,t denotes the provider-adjusted closing price for ETF i at session t. The return series
therefore reflects the provider’s adjustment convention, rather than an independently reconstructed
distribution-reinvested index.
The baseline estimation window contained W = 1,008 returns and ended initially on 20 Decem-
ber 2022. The first evaluated portfolio return was dated 21 December 2022, and 946 out-of-sample
sessions were evaluated through 30 September 2026. Both optimization methods and all reported
portfolio performance calculations used the same synchronized return panel; the indexed-price
figures used the corresponding adjusted prices. Using a common panel removes differences in under-
lying asset-return samples as an explanation for the compared portfolio histories. The historical
distribution-adjustment factors supplied by the provider were not independently reconstructed.
The U.S. three-month Treasury par yield was the risk-free proxy. At each decision date, the
most recent available yield was converted to an effective daily rate under a 252-session convention
(Equation 26). The aligned rates were used both in estimated excess-return objectives and in realized
excess-return ratios.
The benchmark was an initially equal-weighted buy-and-hold portfolio (BHP), with 1/30 of
capital allocated to each ETF and no subsequent rebalancing. It provides a common reference for
realized wealth, although it does not maintain the target exposures of an optimized strategy. All
wealth paths began at USD 100. The return-correlation matrix and indexed adjusted-price series in
Appendix Figures A1 and A2 describe the ETF universe rather than portfolio investment outcomes.

Computational implementation and numerical validation. The 24 portfolio strategies were
computed in MATLAB R2026a Update 3. Mean–variance portfolios used the Portfolio class and
quadprog with 10−9 optimality and constraint tolerances; the tangent mean–variance selection
used the toolbox’s iterative Sharpe-ratio routine. CVaR optimization used PortfolioCVaR and
TrustRegionCP, with up to 2,000 iterations and absolute and relative gap tolerances of 10−8. The
tangent-CVaR rule selected the highest admissible ratio from 20 requested frontier candidates. Both
input methods used the same bounds, the same cap on changes between target weights, and the
same numerical acceptance tests. Numerical cap overshoots no greater than 10−6 were scaled back
toward the preceding feasible target; the cap itself was never enlarged. A failed final candidate
would have led to retention of the preceding target.
The saved-output audit found that the final portfolio allocations met the specified budget,
position-bound, and target-adjustment checks. The recorded scale-back counts refer to decisions
for which at least one optimization candidate required a small numerical correction; they do not
necessarily count corrected final allocations. Representative tangent-CVaR comparisons using
finer frontier grids and tighter solver settings produced only small differences in selected historical

The evaluation employed a close-to-close return accounting convention. Although each target

4.5 Paired bootstrap inference and block-length sensitivity

4.9 Sensitivity analyses
We examined how portfolio outcomes varied with the permitted change in target weights, the
simulated scenario count and random seed, and the length of the moving estimation window.
Each comparison retained the ETF universe and portfolio objectives and used common evaluation
dates within that experiment. Table 10 summarizes the observed patterns; Appendix J gives the
strategy-level results. These descriptive exercises were not independent tests of statistical superiority.
Allowing larger daily changes in target weights had a more visible effect on the portfolios than
changing the simulation settings. With greater freedom to move toward their selected allocations,
many strategies traded more and held more concentrated exposures. Several minimum-risk strategies
experienced lower realized returns alongside lower measured risk, whereas some tangent strategies
earned higher gross returns. The effect was not uniformly favorable to either return-input method.
Figure 13 shows the accompanying changes in implementation turnover, modeled costs, and gross
exposure for MC95 portfolios; Appendix Table A10 reports the portfolio-level gross and net returns.
The cost estimates retain the same linear trading assumption and exclude initial formation, market
impact, and short-position financing.

similarly limited response in the conditional portfolios, while historical optimization was unaffected
(Appendix Table A12). With a restrictive adjustment rule, changes in estimated optimal weights

5 Discussion
The matched comparison shows why differences in estimated return distributions need not produce
similarly large differences in realized portfolio outcomes. The historical and conditional frontiers
displayed different estimated return–risk opportunities, yet the restrictive adjustment rule often
kept their subsequent holdings close. This finding does not establish that the underlying forecasts
were equally accurate: it shows how a common trading restriction shaped the portfolios that could
be constructed from them.
Historical inputs generally had higher observed reward–risk ratios, especially in several tangent
strategies (Table 1). Differences in downside risk were less consistent. A minimum-risk objective
can favor holdings with lower modeled loss exposure but also less participation in positive market
returns, while a tangent objective seeks a higher expected excess return relative to a risk measure.
The outcomes in Tables 4 and 6 are consistent with these portfolio-objective tradeoffs. They do not
imply that historical returns provided better forecasts in general.
The paired bootstrap results also limit the inference. Some unadjusted intervals excluded zero,
but none of the comparisons remained significant after adjustment for multiple testing. The absence
of adjusted rejections is not evidence that the methods are equivalent. Moreover, the bootstrap used
the realized return series from already-optimized portfolios rather than repeating the full estimation
and portfolio-selection exercise within each resample. Its uncertainty statements therefore apply to
the observed strategy histories under the stated resampling design.
Implementation adds a separate consideration. The baseline rule limited changes between
successive target weights, but market movements continually altered the proportions actually
held. Maintaining the targets could therefore require appreciable rebalancing even while target
adjustments were small (Table 5 and Figure 11). When the adjustment allowance was relaxed,
portfolio concentration, short exposure, turnover, and performance responded differently across
objectives. The return-input comparison consequently depended on the investment rule used to
translate estimated preferences into holdings.
The sensitivity results support this interpretation but do not isolate a single causal mechanism.
Changing scenario counts or seeds had relatively little effect under the baseline cap, whereas the
effect of a looser cap was more substantial for several strategies; changing the estimation window
produced mixed outcomes. These comparisons concern the tested settings and sample, and should
not be extended to other trading restrictions or models without further evaluation.
Several limitations matter for investment use. The Yahoo Finance price adjustments were
30
not independently reconstructed. Reported returns used next-session, close-to-close attribution
rather than observed executable trades, and transaction costs were modeled without market impact,
variable spreads, or borrowing and financing costs. The conditional copula permitted joint-tail
dependence but was not tested for predictive loss calibration. Numerical checks verified feasibility
and stability at representative decisions, not global optimality across the full path. The single ETF
universe and evaluation period further limit generalization.
6 Conclusions
This paper compared historical moving-window returns with conditional scenario inputs for multi-
asset ETF optimization under matched portfolio objectives and investment rules. Historical portfolios
generally had higher realized reward–risk ratios over the evaluation period, but none of the paired
differences remained significant after adjustment for multiple testing. Conditional scenarios did not
consistently improve realized downside-risk measures. These findings describe outcomes under the
specified data and trading conventions, rather than a general ranking of forecasting methods.
The portfolio implementation results offer the main practical interpretation. A tight constraint on
changes between target allocations limited the effects of different return inputs on realized holdings,
while maintaining those allocations still required trades as market prices changed. Relaxing that
constraint altered trading activity, exposures, and some realized returns. For evaluating portfolio
risk management, the choice of return inputs should therefore be considered together with the limits
and costs of implementing the resulting allocations.
