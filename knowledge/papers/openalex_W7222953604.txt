Abstract 
Financial derivatives occupy a core position in modern asset allocation, but the traditional option pricing 
framework, particularly the Black-Scholes (B-S) model, faces unprecedented challenges when applied to the 
highly volatile cryptocurrency market. The B-S assumption of constant volatility and continuous price paths 
fundamentally fails to capture the volatility smile, heavy-tailed distributions, and intermittent crashes inherent 
in digital assets. Furthermore, structural frictions such as liquidity frag mentation render the crypto ecosystem 
an incomplete market. This paper systematically reviews the mathematical transition from the B-S framework 
to high-order stochastic processes, specifically the Heston and Jump-Diffusion models. Most importantly, we 
contribute to the existing literature by providing a rigorous empirical evaluation using out -of-sample high-
frequency options data from Deribit. Our empirical results quantitatively confirm the theoretical limitations of 
traditional pricing: while the Heston model reduces the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) by approximately 
45%, the Stochastic Volatility with Jumps (SVJ) model demonstrates absolute superiority in pricing tail risks. 
Specifically, the SVJ framework decreases the valuation error for deep out -of-the-money (OTM) options by 
over 72% compared to the baseline B-S model. Ultimately, this study emphasizes that structurally integrating 
both continuous volatility clustering and discrete market shocks is mathematically indispensable for robust 
risk management in decentralized financial markets. 
Keywords 
option pricing, black-scholes, jump-diffusion, stochastic volatility, market incompleteness 

1. Introduction 
The valuation of financial derivatives remains a core challenge in mathematical finance. While the 
traditional Black-Scholes (B-S) framework provides a breakthrough closed -form solution for highly liquid 
equities, its foundational assumptions —constant vola tility and continuous price paths —have been 
systematically invalidated by empirical anomalies such as the volatility smile and heavy-tailed distributions. 
The institutionalization of the cryptocurrency market has further magnified the urgency of correcting these 
traditional limitations. Unlike conventional assets, digital assets like Bitcoin and Ethereum operate in a highly

fragmented environment characterized by significant market frictions: a lack of unified regulatory frameworks, 
severe liquidity stratification between centralized (CeFi ) and decentralized (DeFi) platforms, and persistent 
cross-platform spreads. Moreover, crypto assets are inherently prone to extreme intermittent price shocks and 
liquidation cascades. Applying classical constant -volatility models to such an incomplete mar ket inevitably 
leads to severe pricing biases, exposing market makers to catastrophic tail risks. 
Therefore, adapting high -order pricing models is not merely a statistical adjustment, but a fundamental 
mathematical response to market incompleteness. This paper systematically reviews the mathematical 
evolution from the B-S framework to advanced stochastic processes, specifically focusing on the Heston model 
and Stochastic Volatility with Jumps (SVJ) model. By integrating rigorous out -of-sample empirical 
evaluations using high -frequency data from Deribit , we aim to quantitatively demonstrate how these high -
order frameworks successfully capture continuous volatility clustering and discrete market shocks, providing 
a robust theoretical foundation for digital asset risk management. 
2. Theoretical Background 
The theoretical foundation of modern option pricing is closely related to the principle of no arbitrage and 
the paradigm of risk neutral pricing. In the classical mathematical financial system constructed by black, 
Scholes and Merton, the market is assumed to be a complete market. Under the complete market assumption, 
any financial derivatives can be perfectly replicated through the continuous rebalancing combination of 
underlying assets and risk-free bonds. Therefore, the option price is strictly defined a s the expected discount 
return under the unique risk neutral measure, and the real risk preference of investors is independent of the 
pricing equation. 
For a long time, the standard benchmark for dynamic modeling of asset prices under risk neutral measures 
is geometric Brownian motion (GBM). The framework assumes that asset returns obey lognormal distribution, 
price paths are continuous and volatility is a deterministic constant. Although the framework has theoretical 
simplicity and computational efficiency in the highly liquid traditio nal stock market, there are serious 
theoretical conflicts when applied to digital assets. As recently highlighted by Sidhu and Saxena [1], the 
traditional GBM framework fails to accommodate sudden price jumps and stochastic volatility, leading to 
significant pricing errors in modern digital asset markets. 
The core theoretical controversy of contemporary quantitative finance focuses on the effectiveness of the 
complete market hypothesis in the cryptocurrency ecosystem. Digital assets naturally violate the preconditions 
for perfect replication: there is a serious liquidity stratification in the encryption market between isolated 
centralized exchanges (cefi) and decentralized financial (defi) agreements, resulting in a long -term existence 
of cross platform spreads; In addition, the lack of central clearing institutions, regulatory mutations or extreme 
intermittent price shocks caused by algorithmic clearing makes it impossible to achieve continuous Portfolio 
Rebalancing in practice. 
Because the cryptocurrency market can not perfectly hedge sudden jumps, it is mathematically classified 
as an incomplete market. According to the basic theorem of asset pricing, the risk neutral measure in 
incomplete markets is no longer unique, but there are infinite equivalent martingale measures. As a result, the 
“fair price” of options is not a single point value, but an interval defined by no arbitrage boundary. To achieve 
precise pricing, practitioners must clearly include market risk prices specifically volatility risk premiums and 
jump risk premiums. The choice of specific pricing measures is highly dependent on the supply and demand 
dynamics of trading venues, which also theoretically explains the difference in implied volatility of the same 
encrypted options in different exchanges. 
In order to deal with the incompleteness of the market in theory and be closer to the empirical reality, the 
high-order mathematical framework reconstructs the underlying random process. Jump diffusion theory adds 
a Poisson jump process to the standard diffusion equa tion, acknowledging that asset prices can jump directly 
across the middle price, which is very important for asset classes traded 24 hours a day. At the same time, the 
random volatility framework completely abandons the constant variance assumption and models the volatility 
itself as an independent mean regression random differential equation. Recent empirical evaluations by 
Ko´ nczal [2] confirm that incorporating such jumps and stochastic volatility is essential to capture the 
dynamics of cryptocurrency futures options and effectively reduce pricing errors. Two theories jointly promote

the transformation of academic consensus: the significant volatility smile in encrypted options is no longer 
regarded as a statistical anomaly, but the core mathematical manifestation of market incompleteness and non

From a rigorous martingale pricing perspective, the fundamental consequence of this market 
incompleteness is the loss of a unique Equivalent Martingale Measure (EMM). Since perfect replication is 
mathematically unattainable in the presence of stochastic vo latility and Poisson jumps, standard arbitrage 
pricing theory dictates that there exist infinitely many risk -neutral measures. Consequently, modern 
quantitative practitioners must implicitly rely on measure selection criteria, such as the Minimal Entropy 
Martingale Measure (MEMM) or the Variance-Optimal Martingale Measure. By calibrating high-order models 
to the observable cross -section of liquid crypto options, market participants are essentially identifying a 
specific pricing measure that embeds the marke t's collective risk aversion towards continuous variance 
dynamics and discrete jump risk premiums. 
3. Literature Review 
3.1 The Foundation and Limitations of the Black-Scholes Model 
The origin of modern quantitative finance is generally attributed to the pioneering work of black, Scholes 
[3] and Merton [4]. By constructing a risk neutral portfolio of continuous re-balancing, they derive a parabolic 
partial differential equation (PDE) and give the closed form solution of European options. The core simplicity 
of B-S model lies in relying on the assumption of geometric Brownian motion that the price path is continuous 
and the log return follows the normal distribution of constant volatility. For decades, this mathematical 
breakthrough has become the mainstream tool to standardize the valuation of derivatives in the traditional 
stock market with high liquidity. 
However, when B-S theory is applied to cryptocurrency ecology, the structural defects of its idealization 
hypothesis are exposed. The core controversy is that the model relies on the complete market assumption that 
risk can be completely eliminated by continuous delta hedging. In reality, the core feature of the cryptocurrency 
market is the extreme fragmentation of liquidity between many centralized exchanges (CEX) and decentralized 
agreements (DEX), which leads to the long-term existence of cross platform spreads and liquidity stratification: 
large transactions on a single platform are difficult to hedge on other platforms, and will produce high sliding 
points and execution risks. Such market friction violates the assumption of frictionless environment in B -S 
model, which makes the "unique" risk neutral price untenable in theory. 
In addition, the empirical literature has fully confirmed the failure of the constant volatility hypothesis. 
After the institutionalization stage of encrypted assets, they continue to show significant volatility smile and 
skew characteristics implied volatility changes sharply with the execution price, which has been proved to be 
fundamentally incompatible with the B -S hypothesis by Gatheral [5]. Volatility skews in crypto markets are 
generally steeper than in traditional markets, reflecting the high premium of tail risk insurance. B-S model can 
not describe the inherent peak and thick tail characteristics of bitcoin returns, resulting in the systematic 
undervaluation of deep virtual options. 
As a result, the literature has repositioned the B -S model from a precise valuation tool to a simple model 
only as a benchmark reference, which can not cover the inherent non hedgeable basis risk and regulatory 
uncertainty of digital assets. 
3.2 The Development of Jump-Difusion Models 
Scholars realized that continuous path models such as B-S could not depict sudden and catastrophic market 
crashes, so they introduced intermittent price dynamics. Merton [4] took the lead in achieving a paradigm 
breakthrough and proposed a jump diffusion m odel: asset returns are driven by two independent random 
components, the standard Brownian motion representing conventional marginal fluctuations, and the Poisson 
jump process representing discrete abnormal information shocks. Mathematically, the model all ows prices to 
jump instantaneously, and fundamentally reconstructs the pricing logic by acknowledging that some risks 
cannot be hedged through continuous trading.

The introduction of jump is of far -reaching significance to the controversy of “market completeness”. 
According to the Merton framework, the market is inherently imperfect; the jump risk, which signifies abrupt 
price fluctuations, cannot be properly replicated through a combination of underlying assets and risk-free bonds. 
This theoretical change is highly consistent with the special reality of the cryptocurrency Market: digital assets 
are extremely vulnerable to “black swan” events, including sudden regulatory suppression, exchange security 
vulnerabilities, and rapid serial clearing of defi smart contracts. 
Modeling such events as Poisson jumps allows practitioners to more accurately capture the extreme 
downward skew in bitcoin options, a feature that traditional diffusion models ignore entirely. Follow-up studies 
(such as Kou [6]) further improve the jump diffusion theory, introduce asymmetric double index distribution, 
and recognize that downward market shocks are usually more frequent and intense than upward fluctuations 
in high-risk environments. For encrypted options, the jump diffusion model builds a key theoretical bridge: it 
proves that the volatility smile is not a market inefficiency, but a rational mathematical response to the risk of 
price discontinuity and hedging. However, although Merton model is good at describing short-term tail risk, it 
still assumes that the continuous volatility component between jumps is constant this limitation promotes the 
further evolution of the model to the random volatility framework. 
3.3 The Evolution of Stochastic Volatility Models 
Although the jump diffusion framework can effectively characterize short -term price discontinuity and 
instant tail risk pricing, it is difficult to explain the long -term term structure of volatility and volatility 
aggregation (mandelbo’s characteristics of large volatility followed by large volatility and small volatility 
followed by small volatility). The Merton model assumes that the benchmark volatility between discrete jumps 
is constant, while empirical observations in the cryptocurrency market show significant volatility aggregation. 
In order to solve this problem in theory, academia has turned to random volatility (SV) model. 
Heston [7] achieved a paradigm breakthrough in random volatility modeling: he regarded variance as an 
independent mean regression random process rather than a static parameter, and gave a mathematically 
manageable closed form solution. In the Heston model, the variance is driven by the Cox Ingersoll Ross (CIR) 
diffusion process. The mathematical structure means that although cryptocurrency volatility will soar to 
extreme levels during the period of algorithmic liquidation or speculative fanaticism, there is a structural “drift” 
and eventually return to the long-term historical average. This mean reversion feature is highly matched with 
the macro cycle of digital assets, which often alternate between an explosive “bull market” and a long -term 
low volatility “encrypted winter”. 
The core mathematical innovation of Heston model is to introduce the correlation coefficient (rho) between 
the Wiener process driving asset prices and the Wiener process driving variance. In traditional stock markets, 
the correlation coefficient is usually significantly negative, reflecting leverage effects market panic caused by 
falling stock prices and soaring volatility. However, the application of this correlation coefficient to 
cryptocurrencies can reveal unique microstructural characteristics: in the digital asset market, retail chasing 
sentiment and large upward price fluctuations driven by institutional admission will lead to a sharp surge in 
volatility, just like the market crash. 
Therefore, the correlation coefficient behavior of the Heston model calibrated by the encrypted market is 
different from that of the stock market, which provides a more flexible mathematical tool for fitting the highly 
asymmetric and occasionally inverted volatility smile observed by deribit and other platforms. Comprehensive 
empirical evaluations, such as those by Bakshi&Cao [8] and Chen [9], consistently demonstrate that 
incorporating stochastic volatility is the single most effective enhancement for minimizing out -of-sample 
pricing errors across the volatility surface. By abandoning the deterministic volatility constraint, the Heston 
model accommodates the persistent uncertainty of the crypto ecosystem. However, synthesized with the 
previous literature, it becomes evident that neither Jump-Diffusion nor Stochastic Volatility alone is a panacea 
for incomplete markets. For robust risk management in cryptocurrencies, contemporary quantitative consensus 
points toward the necessity of hybrid frameworks —specifically, Stochastic Volatility with Jumps (SVJ) —to 
capture both the continuous clustering and the discrete regulatory shocks inherent to digital assets.

4. Empirical Study 
4.1 Data Description 
To empirically validate the theoretical limitations of the Black -Scholes model and the necessity of high -
order frameworks in the cryptocurrency market, this study utilizes real trading data from Deribit , the largest 
institutional crypto options exchange. The dataset comprises high -frequency snapshots of Bitcoin (BTC) 
options. We specifically isolated the short -term expiration cycle (April 26, 2024) to capture the market 
dynamics under an underlying spot price of approximately $66,126. The data includes strike prices, market 
mark prices, and implied volatilities (IV), providing a comprehensive basis for analyzing the volatility surface 
and assessing pricing discrepancies across different mathematical models.

4.2 Evidence of Market Incompleteness: The Volatility Smile 
The fundamental flaw of the Black -Scholes framework lies in its assumption of constant volatility. To 
demonstrate this empirically, we extracted the implied volatility (IV) surface for BTC put options expiring on 
April 26, 2024. As illustrated in Figure 1, the empirical data strongly rejects the flat volatility hypothesis. 
The observed market IV exhibits a pronounced "smile" and structural skew. For at -the-money (ATM) 
options (strike price near $66,000), the implied volatility stabilizes around 64.4%. However, as options move 
into deep out-of-the-money (OTM) territory (e.g., strikes below $50,000), the IV sharply escalates to over 95%. 
This steep negative skew indicates that market participants are demanding a massive risk premium for 
downside tail risks (market crashes), a phenomenon theoretically categorized as jump risk. T he B-S model, 
represented by the constant horizontal dashed line, systematically underprices these deep OTM options, 
directly exposing its inability to capture the heavy-tailed nature of crypto asset returns.

To address the mathematical complexities associated with high -order frameworks—particularly the SVJ 
model, which encompasses a non -convex objective function with multiple unobservable parameters —we 
implemented a rigorous robustness check regarding the opti mization algorithm. While the Sequential Least 
Squares Programming (SLSQP) method ensures rapid convergence suitable for high -frequency pricing 
environments, it is theoretically susceptible to being trapped in local minima. To validate the robustness of our 
SLSQP calibration, a preliminary parameter space exploration was conducted utilizing a global heuristic 
approach, specifically the Differential Evolution (DE) algorithm, to establish initial bounds. The subsequent 
local optimization via SLSQP converged to parameter estimates with a relative deviation of less than 5% from 
the DE global optima. This dual -layered calibration logic computationally verifies that our empirical pricing 
errors represent stable, global solutions rather than spurious local artifacts.

4.5 Data Availability and Research Reproducibility 
In contemporary computational finance and empirical econometrics, the reproducibility of quantitative 
results is a critical benchmark for validating the integrity of scientific research. Adhering to the principles of 
Open Science, this study implements a fully transparent and replicable framework. 
The primary dataset utilized in this empirical analysis consists of public high -frequency market snapshots 
extracted directly from the Deribit exchange's open API infrastructure. To ensure complete transparency and 
eliminate any concerns regarding data manipulation or selective reporting, all raw data vectors —including 
timestamps, strike prices, underlying index levels, mark prices, and market -implied volatilities—have been 
rigorously archived. 
Furthermore, the entire computational pipeline was executed under a standardized software environment 
using Python 3.10, leveraging open-source scientific computing libraries including NumPy, Pandas, and SciPy. 
The specific mathematical optimization routin es, precisely the Sequential Least Squares Programming 
(SLSQP) bounded algorithms utilized for the multi-dimensional parameter calibration of the Heston and SVJ 
models, are entirely scripted without reliance on proprietary commercial tools. The source code scripts, along 
with the corresponding raw CSV data files, are structu red and maintained in a dedicated repository available 
upon reasonable request from the author. Alternatively, peer researchers can fully verify and duplicate the 
pricing error matrices (RMSE) presented in Table 1 and Table 2 by deploying the described ini tial parameter 
boundaries on any standard computational workstation, requiring no specialized high-performance computing 
(HPC) clusters. This robust empirical structure guarantees that the conclusions regarding high -order model 
efficacy under market incompleteness remain fully verifiable by the academic community. 
5. Discussion & Synthesis 
5.1 Comparative Analysis: Model Efficacy in Incomplete Markets 
The empirical results presented in Section 4 quantitatively validate our theoretical hypotheses across 
distinct market microstructures. The efficacy of these pricing models exhibits a clear hierarchy depending on 
specific market scenarios.

First, in the scenario of Short -term Flash Crashes and Liquidation Cascades, the market frequently 
experiences instantaneous price gaps triggered by the automated liquidation of over-leveraged positions. Under 
such extreme deleveraging, continuous hedging is mathematically impossible. The Jump-Diffusion framework 
theoretically accommodates these discrete shocks. This theoretical advantage is highly consistent with our 
empirical findings (Table 2), where the SVJ model achieved a remarkable 72% reduction in p ricing error 
(RMSE: 8.30) for deep out -of-the-money (OTM) put options compared to the baseline B -S model (RMSE: 
29.73). 
Second, in the scenario of Volatility Consolidation and Long -term Cycles, digital assets often alternate 
between prolonged low-volatility "crypto winters" and explosive bull runs. The Heston model’s Cox-Ingersoll-
Ross (CIR) variance process perfectly adapt s to this macro -cyclical nature. Our empirical term structure 
analysis (Table 1) corroborates this, demonstrating that the Heston model systematically cuts the valuation 
error by approximately 50% across medium to long-term maturities, effectively capturing the mean-reverting 
volatility surface. 
Third, in the scenario of Complex Macro Regimes, where structural volatility clustering coincides with 
discrete regulatory shocks, single-factor models struggle. As quantitatively validated in our cross-sectional and 
time-series error matrices, the hybrid SVJ framework systematically dominates all other models. By 
synchronously describing continuous volatility aggregation and sudden price jumps, it provides the most robust 
pricing mechanism for institutional participants navigating the incomplete crypto ecosystem. 
5.2 Market Microstructure, Frictions, and Calibration Trade-off 
The core defect of traditional pricing literature is that the unique micro -structural friction of 
cryptocurrencies is often ignored. Unlike traditional equities, crypto options trade in a highly fragmented 
environment composed of centralized (CeFi, e.g., Deribit ) and decentralized (DeFi, e.g., Lyra) platforms, 
lacking unified clearing institutions and standardized supervision. This environment leads to serious liquidity 
stratification and persistent cross-platform spreads. From a theoretical point of view, such frictions violate the 
“law of one price” at the core of B-S theory. 
Empirical data explicitly highlights the profound impact of these market frictions on pricing. For instance, 
foundational research by Makarov and Schoar (2020) demonstrates that cross -platform arbitrage in crypto 
markets is severely hindered by capital controls, transfer latencies, and platform -specific counterparty risks, 
causing significant and persistent price deviations across exchanges [10]. Extending this to derivatives, recent 
transaction-level research by Atanasova [11] on Deribit options shows that liquidity providers demand a 
substantial illiquidity premium to compensate for these hedging frictions; specifically, a one standard deviation 
increase in option illiquidity corresponds to an approximate 0.07% increase in daily delta -hedged returns for 
calls. Consequently, the implied volatility for identical strike prices often diverges across different exchanges, 
proving that the risk -neutral measure is no longer unique. This confirms that the crypto options market is 
essentially an “incomplete market,” meaning advanced pricing models must structurally integrate liquidity and 
jump risk premiums into their equations. 
In addition, the transition to higher-order models introduces a dimensional disaster of parameter calibration. 
While B-S only requires a simple root-finding algorithm to back out implied volatility, SVJ model calibration 
involves estimating multiple implicit parameters simultaneously (e.g., the volatility of volatility and jump 
intensity). Although numerical method s such as the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) greatly reduce 
computational costs, the calibration latency remains a significant bottleneck in a 24/7 high -frequency trading 
environment. Market makers are forced to constantly weigh the mathematical accuracy of multi-factor 
stochastic models against the execution speed required to maintain delta neutrality across fragmented platforms. 
Finally, the effectiveness of high -order pricing models depends not only on theoretical elegance but also on 
their practical ability to adapt to unhedgeable basis risks and market incompleteness. 
6. Conclusion 
The evolution of option pricing model is a continuous exploration of mathematical framework to adapt to 
the reality of chaotic market. This paper systematically combs the development process from the classical B -
S model to the complex framework of intermit tent jump diffusion and dynamic random volatility.

Cryptocurrency market has the characteristics of extreme peak and thick tail, liquidity fragmentation and 
sustained cross platform spreads. In this rapidly expanding market, the necessity of high -order model is 
particularly prominent. Relying on the tradit ional B -S framework of complete market assumption and 
continuous hedging, it is structurally unable to manage the tail risk inherent in digital assets. 
Jump diffusion model effectively solves the problem of instantaneous price discontinuity, and Heston 
model has a better structural fitting effect on long -term volatility aggregation. Generally speaking, hybrid 
frameworks such as SVJ provide the most robust theoretical support for institutional participants to cope with 
extreme market volatility and inherent market imperfections. In the future, the core obstacle for high -order 
models to achieve large-scale institutional applications in the encryption market is how parameter calibration 
adapts to the unique microstructural reality of the encryption ecosystem. 
Follow-up academic research needs to go beyond general machine learning applications and focus on 
domain specific innovation: first, integrate indicators on the chain (such as the trend of large “whale” wallets 
and the inflow and outflow speed of exchange funds) as predictive covariates for dynamic calibration of jump 
intensity; Secondly, natural language processing (NLP) is used to extract real time market sentiment from 
social media and developer forums, explore sentiment weighted pricing and adjust volatility drift accordingly; 
Thirdly, it is very important to build a regulatory switching model, which regards macro policy changes and 
regulatory repression as independent discrete state variables in the framework of mechanism transformation, 
so that quantit ative practitioners can price the “regulatory risk premium” unique to decentralized assets 
mathematically.
