Abstract
This paper evaluates whether market-wide ModernFinBERT news variables improve volatility
forecasts for an ex-post selected panel of eleven U.S.-listed stocks using source data from 2000–
2022. Atℎ= 1,5,22, the target is the annualized square root of an average of future trailing 252-
session Yang–Zhang variance estimates. Sentiment, directionality, posterior entropy, intensity,
and news volume are compared with persistence, VIX, and AAII through matched HAR-X
models and additional regularized, tree-based, naive, and stacking specifications.
Hyperparameters are selected once on the first 252 complete observations. Five chronological
blocks support expanding and rolling comparisons. The primary expanding family contains 792
BH-adjusted tests, with joint-window and dependence sensitivities. Naive has the lowest mean
QLIKE at each horizon. HAR-VIX is the most frequent expanding cell winner, but not the
model with the lowest mean loss. HAR-Text improves on HAR in two of 33 expanding cells;
neither favorable QLIKE rejection survives longer HAC. High-state comparisons are relatively
less adverse to text but do not establish generally positive gains.
Stacking contributes 66 tests (33 cells×two losses) to this fixed family. Because its near-
constant forecasts generate very small p-values in many adverse comparisons, those tests can
affect the BH rank thresholds applied to the remaining models. They are retained only to
preserve the ex-ante model universe; no substantive evidence claim is based on Stacking perfor-
mance.
ModernFinBERT is a fixed ex-post measurement instrument with an undocumented training-
data cutoff. The existing 2,700-text agreement audit is not expert ground truth and is not
regenerated here. SHAP explains fitted forecasts, not causal effects or incremental accuracy.
Conclusions are conditional on the long-window target, initial-only selection, sparse refits, cali-
bration, and numerical safeguards.

1 Introduction
Forecasting financial-market volatility is central to derivatives valuation, portfolio allocation,
risk management, margin setting, and systemic-risk monitoring. In contrast to expected re-
turns, volatility displays persistence, clustering, long-memory-like behavior, leverage effects,
and pronounced state dependence, which make it comparatively more forecastable and eco-
nomically relevant (Andersen et al., 2001, 2003; Bollerslev, 1986; Christoffersen, 2012; Engle,
1982; McNeil et al., 2015). The development of realized-volatility measures further strength-
ened this forecasting literature by providing observable proxies for latent integrated variance
(Andersen et al., 2001; Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard, 2002). Within this class of models,
the heterogeneous autoregressive model of realized volatility (HAR-RV) has become a particu-
larly demanding benchmark because it captures persistence across daily, weekly, and monthly
horizons in a parsimonious specification (Andersen et al., 2007; Bollerslev et al., 2016; Corsi,
2009).
The strength of HAR-type benchmarks raises a substantive question for information-rich fore-
casting systems: do additional predictors contain genuinely incremental information, or do they
mainly increase model complexity without improving out-of-sample accuracy? This question
is especially important for textual data. Financial news is economically plausible as a source
of volatility information because news arrivals, uncertainty, disagreement, and heterogeneous
interpretation can affect trading activity and risk perceptions (Bekaert & Harvey, 2000; French
et al., 1987; O. Kim & Verrecchia, 1994; Ross, 1989). Textual measures may therefore capture
dimensions of the information environment that are not fully represented by lagged realized
volatility.
The preceding empirical phases sharpen this motivation. Article 2 (Section 5) shows that VIX-
based market uncertainty is more consistently related to subsequent trading dynamics than the
examined optimism indicators are to abnormal returns. Article 3 (Section 8) finds that text-
derived emotions do not provide robust short-horizon directional signals, while market-state
and drawdown information is more useful for exposure management. These findings motivate a
distinct question for the present study: whether market-wide text adds more reliably to forecasts
of risk and volatility than it does to directional return prediction.
The empirical literature provides reasons both for optimism and for caution. Media pessimism,
news-based uncertainty, and disagreement have been associated with returns, trading activity,
and volatility (Audrino et al., 2020; García, 2013; Tetlock, 2007). At the same time, evidence
on machine-learning volatility forecasts is model- and design-dependent. Neural-network and
broader machine-learning approaches can improve realized-volatility forecasts in some settings,
but their gains are not uniform across models, horizons, and evaluation designs (Bucci, 2020).
Separately, nonlinear HAR extensions and explicit treatment of realized-volatility measurement
error can improve on the canonical HAR benchmark (Buccheri & Corsi, 2021). These results
motivate direct out-of-sample comparison rather than a presumption that a modern language
215
model will improve forecasts relative to a strong persistence benchmark.
This paper therefore asks a deliberately narrow question:does market-wide financial news pro-
cessed with a modern transformer provide incremental out-of-sample information for forecasting
individual-stock realized volatility?The emphasis is on the predictive value of the textual signal
rather than on the predictive capacity of machine learning in general. To make that distinction
explicit, the transformer-based news variables are evaluated alongside two simpler market-wide
information channels: VIX-based implied volatility and AAII survey sentiment. VIX summa-
rizes forward-looking option-market expectations (Busch et al., 2011; Christensen & Prabhala,
1998; Whaley, 2000), whereas AAII provides a direct survey-based measure of investor sentiment
(M. Baker & Wurgler, 2007; Brown & Cliff, 2004). Comparing these channels helps determine
whether any observed gains are specific to transformer-derived text or instead arise from more
conventional information sources.
1.1 Research hypotheses
The empirical analysis evaluates four hypotheses. The hypotheses distinguish incremental tex-
tual information from the predictive value of conventional market-wide indicators, model flexi-
bility, and forecast combination.
H4.1: Incremental textual forecasting value.Model specifications that augment
volatility-persistence predictors with ModernFinBERT-derived news variables should achieve
lower out-of-sample forecast losses than the corresponding available non-text benchmarks.
H4.2: Comparative information-channel performance.If transformer-derived news
contains economically distinct information, its forecasting contribution should be at least as
recurrent as the contribution of survey-based AAII sentiment and VIX-based market-implied
volatility across assets and forecast horizons.
H4.3: Stability across forecasting environments.Any forecasting gain associated with
textual information should remain visible across securities, one-, five-, and twenty-two-day hori-
zons, and rolling and expanding walk-forward estimation schemes rather than being confined
to an isolated ticker–horizon–model configuration.
H4.4: State-dependent textual value.If textual sentiment, directionality, and posterior
uncertainty become particularly informative during changes in market risk, text-augmented
specifications should exhibit relatively stronger forecast performance in the ex-post high-
volatility regime than in the low-volatility regime.
The hypotheses are assessed using out-of-sample forecast losses and formal forecast comparisons;
the interpretive role and limitations of SHAP are defined in Section 3.12.
The textual signal is market-wide rather than firm-specific. Daily New York Times financial
news is processed with ModernFinBERT to construct directional sentiment, directionality, pos-
terior entropy, sentiment intensity, and news-volume variables. The same daily information-flow
216
measures are then used to forecast Yang–Zhang realized volatility for a panel of large U.S. equi-
ties. This design imposes a demanding test. A broad news signal must add information beyond
each stock’s own volatility persistence and must remain useful despite being common across
firms with different sectoral exposures and information environments.
The forecasting exercise compares the baseline HAR model with matched HAR-X specifica-
tions, Elastic Net, Random Forest, XGBoost, a naive benchmark, and forecast-combination
models over one-day, five-day, and twenty-two-day horizons. Rolling and expanding walk-
forward schemes are considered because the usefulness of historical information may depend
on the trade-off between parameter stability and adaptation to structural change (Pesaran &
Timmermann, 2007). Forecast performance is evaluated primarily with QLIKE. Squared-error
losses enter the secondary DM comparisons and winner counts, while RMSE and MAE are re-
tained as archived diagnostics. Formal forecast-comparison procedures are used to distinguish
economically small numerical differences from more persistent forecasting gains (Clark & West,
2007; Diebold & Mariano, 1995; Giacomini & White, 2006; Patton, 2011). All estimated fore-
casting specifications are evaluated under the same horizon-specific, fully purged walk-forward
protocol. Both model selection and final out-of-sample evaluation preserve chronological or-
dering and impose a gap corresponding to the forecast horizon. Statistical conclusions are
additionally based on multiplicity-adjusted forecast-comparison procedures rather than on raw
minimum-loss rankings alone.
We also examine whether forecast performance and predictor attribution vary across low- and
high-volatility states. Volatility dynamics differ substantially between tranquil and stressed pe-
riods, and the relevance of news, sentiment, and nonlinear interactions may be state-dependent
(Ang & Bekaert, 2002; Guidolin, 2011; Hamilton, 1989; Marcucci, 2005). The regime analysis is
used as an ex-post diagnostic rather than as a real-time forecasting input. It therefore describes
where forecast errors and feature attributions differ, without treating full-sample regime labels
as implementable trading signals.
This paper contributes to the volatility-forecasting literature in five respects.
First, it evaluates the out-of-sample forecasting performance of market-wide transformer-based
news signals relative to a strong HAR benchmark and matched linear HAR-X specifications for
the VIX, AAII, text, and full information blocks. These linear comparisons isolate the incre-
mental contribution of each information channel without simultaneously changing the functional
form of the benchmark. The text-augmented and full information sets are also evaluated with
tree-based models. VIX-only and AAII-only extensions are compared within the matched linear
family; no RF-VIX specification is added.
Second, itseparatesthreeconceptuallydifferentinformationchannels: transformer-derivednews
variables, AAII survey sentiment, and VIX-based market-implied volatility. This separation
prevents forecasting gains generated by survey data, option-market information, or forecast
combinations from being attributed to textual sentiment.
217
Third, itevaluatestheseinformationchannelswithinacommonmulti-stock, multi-horizonwalk-
forward framework. The comparison covers one-day, five-day, and twenty-two-day Yang–Zhang
volatility targets, multiple model classes, and both rolling and expanding estimation schemes.
This permits an assessment of whether any incremental value is stable across assets, horizons,
and estimation environments rather than confined to an isolated configuration.
Fourth, it complements aggregate forecast losses with ex-post regime-stratified diagnostics. This
analysis examines whether the relative performance of HAR, text-based models, survey-based
models, VIX specifications, and forecast combinations differs between tranquil and stressed
volatility states, while maintaining a clear distinction between descriptive regime analysis and
implementable real-time forecasting.
Fifth, it combines predictive evaluation with SHAP-based model interpretation (Lundberg &
Lee, 2017). The analysis distinguishes between two questions that are often conflated: whether
a variable receives substantial attribution inside a nonlinear model, and whether adding that
information produces a robust out-of-sample forecasting gain.
Taken together, the design separates within-model attribution from incremental forecasting per-
formance and makes the dependence on the long-window target and initial parameter-selection
rule explicit. The empirical assessment below concerns that fully stated procedure. Neither
model sophistication nor feature importance alone establishes the forecasting value of an infor-
mation channel.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the equity, VIX, AAII,
and New York Times data and the construction of the transformer-based textual variables.
Section 3 presents the forecasting targets, feature sets, models, walk-forward evaluation design,
forecast-comparison procedures, regime diagnostics, and SHAP analysis. Section 4 reports
the full-sample and regime-stratified forecasting evidence and interprets the feature-attribution
results. Section 5 concludes.
2 Dataset
2.1 Data Sources, Dataset Construction, and Corpus Diagnostics
Our empirical design requires a dataset capable of supporting a multi-horizon volatility fore-
casting task in which stock-specific realized volatility is predicted using three complementary
information channels: historical volatility dynamics, market-wide risk expectations, and tex-
tual news-based sentiment. Existing volatility datasets typically provide rich market variables
but limited textual information, while existing financial text datasets often lack direct align-
ment with daily asset-level forecasting targets. This motivates the construction of a daily panel
dataset that links equity prices, implied volatility, investor sentiment, and large-scale news
sentiment over a common sample period.
The final dataset is constructed at the daily frequency for AAPL, ABBV, ABT, AMZN, JNJ,
JPM, LLY, MA, UNH, WMT, and XOM. Each equity is evaluated at forecast horizonsℎ= 1,

ℎ= 5, andℎ= 22, yielding a complete empirical panel of11×3 = 33observed ticker–horizon
cells. No ticker–horizon cell is imputed.
The equity universe is a purposively selected ex-post panel of 11 currently large U.S.-listed
firms. It is not a point-in-time reconstruction of the historical large-cap universe. Accordingly,
the empirical conclusions are restricted to the firms included in this panel and should not be
generalized to all firms that qualified as large U.S. equities at different points during 2000–2022.
Thesource-dataperiodspans1January2000through31December2022. Theeffectivemodeling
sample begins only after the construction of the 252-session Yang–Zhang initialization and
subsequent lag construction. Parameter selection and stacking calibration further precede the
common out-of-sample period. Consequently, effective model samples generally begin in 2001
for the long-history securities and later for ABBV and MA. The distinction between the source-
data period and the effective estimation period is maintained throughout the analysis.
Daily open, high, low, close, adjusted close, and volume data are obtained from Yahoo Finance.
The sample begins in January 2000 and ends in December 2022, subject to security-level data
availability.
To capture market-wide uncertainty, we include the CBOE Volatility Index (VIX), which serves
as a forward-looking proxy for aggregate option-implied volatility. In addition, we incorporate
survey-based investor sentiment from the American Association of Individual Investors (AAII),
including bullish, bearish, and neutral sentiment shares, as well as the bull–bear spread. All
market-wide variables are transformed into lagged and smoothed predictors over one-day, five-
day, and twenty-two-day windows, matching the forecasting horizons considered in the study.
The textual component is based on a large-scale New York Times article corpus obtained from
the Kaggle dataset “NYT Articles 2.1M 2000–Present”. The raw corpus contains article-level
metadata, including publication timestamp, source URL, headline, abstract, snippet, news desk,
section, subsection, and lead paragraph.
To ensure data quality and financial relevance, the textual corpus is processed in one ordered
sequence before the forecasting features are formed. First, we retain observations with a valid
article URL, a parseable publication timestamp, and non-empty textual content in at least
one of the headline, abstract, snippet, or lead-paragraph fields; when available, articles are
additionally required to contain at least 80 words and to belong to standard editorial formats
(articles or blog posts). Second, we apply a finance-relevance screen based jointly on editorial
metadata and textual content: an article is retained if it originates from a business-oriented
desk or section (e.g.,Business,DealBook,Finance, orMarkets) or if its textual fields contain
terms from the predefined financial lexicon covering accounting, macroeconomic, and capital-
market terminology. Third, headline, abstract, lead paragraph, and snippet are normalized and
concatenated with explicit delimiter tokens; boilerplate and formatting artifacts are removed
and the sequence is truncated to the fixed model input length. Fourth, duplicate URLs and
exact duplicate texts are checked separately and malformed or empty records are excluded.
Fifth, a language diagnostic is performed on a sample of documents to assess non-English
219
contamination. Sixth, each retained article is mapped to an eligible U.S. equity trading session
using the timestamp rule defined once in Section 3.4; article-level measures are then aggregated
at that assigned-session level. Finally, the daily textual predictors are lagged and transformed
into the prespecified rolling features using only information available before the forecast origin.
The textual variables are constructed as market-wide information-flow measures rather than
firm-specific news signals. The same daily textual signal is merged into each stock-level forecast-
ing dataset. This design is intentional: the objective is to measure whether broad financial-news
sentiment, directionality, posterior uncertainty, and news intensity contain incremental informa-
tion for individual equity volatility beyond stock-specific volatility persistence and market-wide
implied volatility. The resulting document-level corpus is therefore aggregated into market-wide
predictors; the forecasting design does not construct firm-specific news features.
The sentiment classifier produces posterior probabilities for three classes: bullish, bearish, and
neutral. These probabilities are used to construct five textual information measures. Direc-
tional sentiment is defined as the difference between bullish and bearish posterior probabilities.
Directionality is defined as the posterior mass assigned to either directional class, irrespective
of sign. Posterior uncertainty is measured separately using the normalized Shannon entropy of
the three-class probability distribution. Sentiment intensity is the absolute value of directional
sentiment, while news volume is the number of valid articles assigned to a trading session.

2.2 Blinded agreement audit of ModernFinBERT classifications
A blinded agreement audit was conducted on a simple random sample without replacement
comprising𝑁= 2,700records. The purpose of this exercise was not to establish absolute
classification accuracy against expert-annotated ground truth. Instead, it assessed the extent
to which ModernFinBERT classifications agreed with an independently applied, automated
semantic reference procedure that was blinded to the model outputs. Each record received
exactly one reference label: bearish, neutral, or bullish.
The validation procedure was blinded to the ModernFinBERT outputs. During label assign-
ment, only the content of the text field was visible. The model-generated bearish, neutral, and
bullish probabilities and the model-assigned sentiment label were masked from the reference-
label procedure.
The reference labels were generated using ChatGPT 5.6 Sol, accessed in August 2026. Because
the interface did not expose a reproducibility seed or decoding parameters, exact deterministic
reproduction of individual reference labels is not claimed. The annotation procedure received
only the textual content of each sampled record. The ModernFinBERT probabilities, Mod-
ernFinBERT class label, publication date, subsequent market outcomes, and all remaining
metadata were excluded from the annotation input.
The following instruction was used to assign the blinded validation labels:
Assign exactly one label to the following financial text: bearish, neutral, or bullish.
Use only the information contained in the text. Do not use probability scores, existing
sentiment labels, dates, subsequent market outcomes, or any other metadata.
Apply the following definitions:
Bearish:deterioration in financial performance or economic conditions, losses, liquidity
problems, declining activity, adverse legal or regulatory decisions, increasing risk, or other
predominantly negative economic implications.
Bullish:improvement in financial performance or economic conditions, growth, favorable
transactions, stronger demand, value creation, or other predominantly positive economic
implications.
Neutral:descriptive content, materially mixed implications, or insufficiently clear direc-
tional economic implications.

3.3 Textual sentiment aggregation
We use ModernFinBERT as an external pre-trained financial sentiment classifier and do not
fine-tune it within the volatility forecasting pipeline. The model is based on ModernBERT and
is designed for financial sentiment classification across heterogeneous financial text domains,
including news, social media, crypto-related text, stock-market commentary, and macroeco-
nomic content. We therefore treat its outputs as externally generated, model-based sentiment
measurements rather than as labels estimated within the present forecasting exercise. They
are not regarded as historically point-in-time measurements or as semantically validated labels
for the New York Times corpus. This limitation is upstream of the downstream forecasting
design. Chronological splits, lagged predictors, horizon-specific purging, and training-window-
only preprocessing prevent the forecasting models from using future observations contained in
the analytical panel. They cannot, however, rule out the possibility that the fixed ModernFin-
BERT weights encode linguistic or economic information originating after a historical forecast
date or from documents overlapping the 2000–2022 evaluation corpus.
Consequently, the text-based results should be interpreted as an ex-post measurement experi-
ment: they assess whether features produced today by a fixed language model are associated
with subsequent historical volatility outcomes. They do not establish the performance of a text-
classification system that was historically available and trained only on information preceding
each forecast origin. Their semantic validity is assessed only through the limited agreement au-
dit described above and should not be regarded as established against expert-annotated ground
truth.

an outcome already realized within that session.
