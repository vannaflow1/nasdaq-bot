Abstract
This study develops and evaluates a dynamic downside-minim izing portfolio
through ﬁve stages. Starting with a 4-asset universe of equity, bonds, gold, and
a volatility instrument, the portfolio is expanded to 13 and the n 21 assets while
incorporating mean-semivariance optimization, Asian arithme tic-average puts,
American puts valued with Longstaﬀ–Schwartz Least-Squares Mo nte Carlo, cov-
ered calls, momentum tilts, and regime-aware rebalancing. The ﬁ nal stage targets
a 15% expected return. Portfolio performance is evaluated using corre lated Geo-
metric Brownian Motion with 20,000 Monte Carlo paths over a one- year horizon.
The four-asset semivariance-optimized portfolio has a Sortino rat io of 2.04 and
a maximum simulated loss of −23.7%, compared with 1.20 and −45.9% for
SPY. Adding Asian and American puts increases the Sortino ratio t o 2.26 and
reduces maximum simulated loss to −8.8%. The 13-asset buy-and-hold port-
folio reaches a mean Sortino ratio of 5.81. Among the rebalancing s trategies
tested, calendar rebalancing produced the highest mean Sortin o ratio of 9.62.
The 21-asset portfolio with a 15% return target has a Sortino ratio of 4.06 and
maximum simulated loss of −28.8%; the full option overlay reduces the latter
to −9.1%. These results characterize the tradeoﬀs among diversiﬁcation, down-
side protection, return targets, and adaptive rebalancing withi n the simulated
framework.

1 Introduction
The critical balance in a portfolio is between two conﬂicting goals: max imize expected
return and minimize risk on the downside. The Markowitz mean-vari ance framework
Markowitz (1952) tackles this dilemma by assuming that upside and downside volatil -
ity are symmetrical, which is odd in light of the observed behavior of investors and
odd in light of a concave utility function and expected-utility th eory Kahneman and
Tversky (1979). Sortino and Price Sortino and Price (1994) suggest that downside
semivariance is a better measure of risk, as it is zero when returns exceed a minimum
acceptable return τ but is only penalized when returns fall below that threshold.
The full development of one such portfolio, featuring an overlay of exot ic options
and progressively expanded to attain a higher return level through dow nside semi-
variance minimization, is followed. To provide insight into each de sign decision, we
document the decision as it was made, its mathematical justiﬁcation, an d the simula-
tion results that validated or challenged it. This lineage-based approach is motivated
by two considerations: ﬁrst, portfolio construction is an iterative p rocess in which each
step constrains the next; second, this approach keeps a clean account of what is proved
versus assumed in the model.
A summary of the development of the portfolio:
• Stage 1 — 4-asset baseline: Equity + bonds + gold + vol-instrument,
buy and hold, no options. Sets up the diversiﬁcation baseline and semi variance
optimization framework.
• Stage 2 — Asian and American put overlay: Overlays an exotic option
program on top of the 4-asset base. Demonstrates analytically that, under the
stated payoﬀ and exercise assumptions, the American put overlay est ablishes
a lower bound on terminal portfolio value after accounting for the option p re-
mium, and introduces the path-averaging variance-reduction proper ty of the
Asian option Kemna and Vorst (1990).
• Stage 3 — 13-asset optimized portfolio: Adds 10 large-cap proxies to the
equity sleeve. The larger asset set provides a greater number of ret urn sources
that are not highly correlated, resulting in a signiﬁcant increase in t he optimizer’s
Sortino ratio at the same return level.
• Stage 4 — Enhanced EV strategies: Four strategies are tested against
the buy-and-hold base: covered calls (premium income); momentum f actor tilts
(21-day trigger, dynamic trailing-return weights); calendar rebalanci ng (21-
day trigger, ﬁxed optimizer weights — identical trigger to momentum t ilt,
diﬀering only in target weights); and regime-aware rebalancing (Frobe nius-
norm-triggered, ﬁxed optimizer weights). The 13-asset buy-and-hold b ase is the
benchmark for each comparison.
• Stage 5 — 21-asset 15% return target: Expands to growth asset classes
(SmallCap, EM, TechGrowth, Biotech, MicroCap, REIT, Commodities, HiYield)
to gain access to higher expected returns, drawing on documented s ize and factor
premia Fama and French (1993). The numerical frontier analysis indicates that
a 15% expected return carries a minimum reported semiDD of 3.70% under the
model constraints, illustrating the risk-return tradeoﬀ implie d by the model.

2.2 Downside Semivariance and the Sortino Ratio
The downside semivariance of a portfolio with return R(w) and minimum acceptable
return τ is (following the downside-risk framework of Sortino and Price Sortino and
Price (1994)):
σ2 d(w) = E [ min(R(w) − τ, 0)2] (3)
Downside semivariance quantiﬁes shortfall below the target. The Sor tino ratio
Sortino and Price (1994) is used to quantify the quality of a given portfolio, trading
oﬀ low downside deviation against expected return:
Sortino = E[R] − τ σd(w) (4)
where E[R] is the expected portfolio return, τ is the minimum acceptable return
(MAR), and σd(w) is the downside deviation (the square root of the downside
semivariance).
A note on notation: τ in Eq. 3–4 (the MAR used to compute downside deviation
and the Sortino ratio) and the “target return” in the optimizer’s constr aint (Eq. 5,
Sec. 4.2 Eq. 10) are two distinct quantities, both of which this paper u nfortunately
denotes with language that can look interchangeable. In every reported re sult in this
paper, τ = 0% is the value actually used for the Sortino/semivariance calculation —
this can be conﬁrmed by back-solving τ = E[R] − Sortino × σd from the mean return,
downside deviation, and Sortino ratio reported in any row of Tables 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, or
13, all of which are consistent with τ ≈ 0% to within rounding. The “target return”
(6.76% in Stages 1–4; 7–15% in the Stage 5 frontier of Table 10) is a separate return-
ﬂoor constraint on the optimization itself (Eq. 5) and does not enter the Sortino ratio’s
denominator or numerator threshold.

3.3 Covered Calls — Black–Scholes
The payoﬀ of a covered call (long the portfolio, short a call at strike Kcc) is:
min(VT , Kcc) + Cprem · erT − 1 (9)
By put–call parity Black and Scholes (1973); Merton (1973), this strategy (i)
increases the mean return by the call premium, (ii) truncates the right tail at Kcc,
and (iii) provides no direct downside protection. Because the pr emium is received
regardless of the terminal portfolio value, it can modestly improve d ownside outcomes
while the short call limits gains above Kcc. The Sortino ratio improves the more that
premium income outweighs the tail-truncation cost.

4.4.4 Strategy D: Regime-Aware Rebalancing
This strategy monitors the rolling realized correlation matrix every 21 days and
rebalances only when the Frobenius norm of change exceeds a threshold θ:
∥Σt − Σt−lag∥F > θ ⇒ rebalance (14)
Across 1,000 independent simulation trials, regime-aware rebalancing p roduced a
mean Sortino ratio of 8.58, averaging 6.7 ”+-” 1.2 rebalances per year (range 3–10).
This outperforms buy-and-hold (mean 5.81) in 100% of trials and the momentum t ilt
(mean 4.29) in 100% of trials, but is outperformed by calendar rebalancing (me an 9.62,
Section 4.4.3) in 91.9% of trials. The portfolio avoids rebalancing while the correlati on
regime is unchanged, reducing unnecessary tracking error and transac tion costs, but
responds promptly when correlations shift (e.g., a simulated ﬂight- to-quality event).
This is a rule-based reaction to observed changes in correlation struc ture, not a forecast

the Frobenius norm trigger responds to sampling noise rather than genui ne regime

framework. Regime-aware rebalancing may oﬀer advantages in real markets w here
correlations genuinely shift across economic regimes, but that hypothe sis is not testable
within the present GBM design.

4.5.1 The Return Ceiling Problem
Comparing Stage 3 and Stage 5 reveals a critical diagnostic: the highest assu med µ
in the 13-asset universe (12% for LgCap E) sets a hard ceiling on achievable p ortfolio
return. Because the set of feasible long-only portfolios is a convex comb ination of
individual asset returns (Theorem (vi), Sec. 10.1: E[R] ≤ maxi µi), no such portfolio
can exceed this ceiling — and the ceiling itself is set by LgCap E’s simulated realized
mean across the 20,000 paths, not its 12% input assumption, which is why it lan ds near
12.75% rather than exactly 12% (the same simulated-vs-assumed sampling var iation
documented for equity in Section 4.2, where the 9% assumption realize d as 9.53%; the
same ≈ 6% relative uplift applied to 12% lands at ≈ 12.7%). Reaching 15% requires
assets with µ > 12% — a structural feature of the feasible set, not a modeling choice.

5 Discussion
The following subsections distinguish results that follow from th e model assumptions
from claims that are not established by the present analysis.
5.1 Results Conditional on Model Assumptions
(i) Optimizer dominance: σ2 d(w∗) ≤ σ2 d(wbench) for any feasible benchmark
wbench. A true global minimizer would satisfy this inequality because the b ench-
mark is feasible; the numerical procedure used here provides ev idence toward that
minimum but does not by itself prove global optimality.
(ii) Hedged portfolio ﬂoor: K − pT path-by-path. Proof: max( K − VT , 0) − pT ≥
K − VT − pT when VT < K .
(iii) Asian option variance eﬀect: averaging can reduce the variance of the underly-
ing average when correlations are below one Kemna and Vorst (1990); the relative
option price remains model-dependent.
(iv) American ≥ European: Pamer ≥ Peuro Longstaﬀ and Schwartz (2001). Proof:
every European exercise strategy is a feasible American exercise st rategy.
(v) Covered call eﬀect: adds the call premium to the portfolio return and caps the
portfolio payoﬀ at Kcc, thereby truncating the right tail. It provides no direct
downside protection, although the premium can modestly improve do wnside
outcomes; see Black and Scholes (1973); Merton (1973).
(vi) E[R] ≤ maxi µi for any long-only portfolio. Proof: the feasible return set is the
convex hull of individual asset returns.
(vii) Eﬃcient frontier lower bound: for return target t, σ2 d(w∗(t)) is the minimum
achievable semivariance, identiﬁed by the numerical optimization p rocedure using
SLSQP with multiple random restarts; this is a numerical result r ather than a for-
mal proof of global optimality. Rebalancing strategy ranking under GBM:
across 1,000 independent simulation trials, the strategies ranked by me an Sortino
ratio as: calendar rebalancing with ﬁxed optimizer weights (9.62) >regime-aware
rebalancing (8.58) >buy-and-hold base (5.81) >momentum tilt with dynamic
weights (4.29). Calendar rebalancing outperformed regime-aware in 91.9% of t ri-
als; the momentum tilt underperformed buy-and-hold in 98.6% of trials . Because
GBM generates stationary correlations, the Frobenius norm trigger respon ds only
to sampling noise, explaining calendar rebalancing’s advantage over regi me-aware.
The momentum tilt’s underperformance relative to calendar rebalanc ing and buy-
and-hold isolates the cost of dynamic versus ﬁxed target weights in a set ting with
no return autocorrelation.
5.2 Not Proven
(i) F orward-looking alpha: whether any version of this portfolio would beat a real
ETF going forward. All µ, σ, and correlation inputs are historical estimates, not
predictions.
22
(ii) F actor premium persistence: the small-cap, EM, and growth premia (16–22%)
are not guaranteed to persist Fama and French (1993); these premia have shown
multi-year periods of underperformance despite a positive long-ru n average.
(iii) Momentum in real markets: under the GBM speciﬁcation used here, the
momentum strategy did not improve performance because the model cont ains
no return autocorrelation; whether momentum adds value in real market s is not
explored here.
(iv) GBM adequacy: real markets exhibit volatility clustering, fat tails, and jumps
(cf. stochastic-volatility modeling in Heston Heston (1993)); GBM understates
tail risk relative to the true downside distribution.
(v) Option executability: Asian options are non-trivial, OTC instruments with
minimum trade sizes; all prices here are frictionless fair-value M onte Carlo prices.
6 Conclusion
This study developed and evaluated a dynamic downside-minimizin g portfolio through
a ﬁve-stage progression that combined mean-semivariance optimization, d iversiﬁca-
tion, exotic option overlays, covered calls, factor tilts, and regime -aware rebalancing.
The simulation results demonstrate that portfolio downside risk can be substantially
reduced through systematic portfolio construction while maintainin g explicit control
over the desired return target. Expanding the portfolio from four to thirteen assets
produced a substantial improvement in downside-adjusted perf ormance, increasing the
Sortino ratio from 2.04 to 5.85 while reducing downside deviation from 3. 31% to 1.16%.
The addition of Asian and American put overlays further demonstrated th e value of
explicit tail-risk protection, reducing the maximum simulated l oss of the four-asset
portfolio from −23.7% to −8.8%. Among the rebalancing strategies evaluated, calen-
dar rebalancing (every 21 days, ﬁxed optimizer weights) produced t he highest mean
Sortino ratio of 9.62 across 1,000 independent simulation trials, outperfor ming regime-
aware rebalancing (mean 8.58) in 91.9% of trials and the momentum tilt (mean 4.29)
in 100% of trials. The momentum tilt, which uses the same 21-day trigger as c alen-
dar rebalancing but with dynamic trailing-return-based weights, un derperformed even
buy-and-hold (mean 5.81) in 98.6% of trials, isolating the cost of dynamic vers us ﬁxed
target weights in a setting with no return autocorrelation. Within a GBM framework
where correlations are stationary by construction, calendar rebalancing to ﬁxed opti-
mizer weights is the most robust strategy. Regime-aware rebalancing an d momentum
tilts may oﬀer advantages in real markets where correlation structure an d return auto-
correlation genuinely exist, but those hypotheses require validati on on real market data
beyond the scope of this study. Finally, the 21-asset eﬃcient-fronti er analysis showed
that achieving higher expected returns necessarily required acc epting greater down-
side risk within the model, with the 15% target associated with a min imum semiDD
of 3.70%. The principal added value of this work is therefore not a single opt imal
portfolio, but a systematic framework that links portfolio diversiﬁc ation, downside-
risk optimization, option-based protection, and adaptive rebalancing wi thin a uniﬁed
design process. The framework provides a quantitative basis for und erstanding how
23
diﬀerent portfolio mechanisms aﬀect downside risk and return obje ctives, while also
making explicit the tradeoﬀs involved in pursuing higher returns .
