A Comparative Analysis of Three Quantitative Pricing
Frameworks for SSE 50ETF Options: BS Analytical Model,
CRR Binomial Tree Model and Monte Carlo Simulation
Xiaoyu Lin
International Business School Suzhou, Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, Suzhou, China
3095546042@qq.com
Abstract. This study compares the effectiveness and feasibility of the Black-Scholes (BS)
analytical model, the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein (CRR) binomial-tree model and Monte Carlo
simulation of SSE 50 ETF European call contracts. With the help of the no-arbitrage
condition, risk-neutral valuation and geometric Brownian motion are used to create these
three models in Python with the same benchmark parameters and later compare their pricing
accuracy, convergence rate and algorithmic efficiency, and their applicability to various
applications. The numerical data shows that the three methods give almost the same option
price: 0.0846 to the BS model, 0.0847 the 200-step CRR model, and 0.0843 the Monte
Carlo simulation of 100, 000 paths. The BS approach is by nature simple and easy to use,
but has some limitations. As the number of iterations increases, the CRR framework is
inclined to the BS value and thus can be used in American style options. High-dimensional
and path-dependent derivatives are best modeled using Monte Carlo simulation, whose
accuracy depends on the number of simulated paths and the traditional model is not able to
price American contracts directly. These results explain the tradeoff between accuracy,
flexibility and computational cost in option pricing model selection.
Keywords: SSE 50 ETF options, Black–Scholes model, CRR binomial-tree model, Monte
Carlo simulation, option pricing
1. Introduction
As the Chinese derivatives sector advanced over recent times, the SSE 50ETF options were
implemented to assist traders in hedging exposures. Accurate pricing of options serves as the
foundation for portfolio selection and risk control choices. Given that market disruptions and
analytical flaws exist, the costs might deviate from the genuine market value.
Three prominent option valuation techniques are frequently utilized: the Black-Scholes (BS)
theoretical framework, the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein (CRR) discrete tree approach, and Monte Carlo
simulation. While overseas investigation has evaluated the efficacy across mature markets, limited
research has utilized SSE 50ETF futures information from China's developing sectors for a
comprehensive horizontal assessment.

2. Literature review
Option valuation constitutes a fundamental field within financial engineering. Following the
introduction of classical option pricing frameworks, Chinese researchers have performed theoretical
derivation and empirical evaluations regarding the Chinese equity market using three primary
valuation instruments: the Black-Scholes analytical framework, the CRR binomial tree method, and
Monte Carlo simulation, offering benchmarks for the pricing activities of SSE 50ETF options.
2.1. Foreign research status
2.1.1. Research on the Black-Scholes analytical model
Black and Scholes initially established a closed-form pricing framework for European non-dividend-
paying stock options [1]. Within the framework of frictionless markets, geometric Brownian motion
of the underlying asset, constant volatility, arbitrage-free replicating strategies were derived to use in
the computation of option values and, thereby, to form the foundation of the modern option
valuation. Merton also extended the Black-Scholes model and also included continuous dividend
payments as well as varying risk-free rates [2]. He strengthened the theoretical framework of Black-
Scholes-Merton and confirmed the rationality of risk-neutral valuation.
Continued studies have shown the shortcomings of Black-Scholes framework [3]. Real-world
financial markets have characteristics of fluctuation pattern, price spikes and transaction cost, and
non-uniform volatility levels. Several scholars have extended the Black-Scholes to develop jump-
diffusion and stochastic volatility models. However, due to its simplified nature of computation and
ease of calculation, the original Black-Scholes paradigm has remained in use to approximate
European options.
2.1.2. Research on the CRR binomial tree model
The CRR binomial tree option valuation framework was proposed by Cox, Ross and Rubinstein [4].
The model represents the price of assets on frequent intervals in a grid, and assumes that every time
period has two possible outcomes of the primary securities: upward or downward. The process is
reversed to obtain option values in a risk-neutral environment. The method is an effective way of
managing the early-exercise possibility of the American derivatives, and it also addresses the
weakness of the Black-Scholes model, which is limited to the European contracts. The longer the
time intervals, the closer the pricing outputs of the binomial tree algorithm are to the value obtained
via the Black-Scholes closed-form solution, and hence there has been a bridge between finite-
precision algorithms and continuous mathematical methods.
Subsequently, a series of studies have sought to hasten the convergence of binomial models and
integrate models of exotic instruments, such as barrier options [5]. Previous studies have further
recognized the following challenges. An increased time interval can increase the computational load
in a quadratic manner, thus this method is inefficient with high-dimensional derivative instruments.

2.1.3. Research on the Monte Carlo simulation
The first application of Monte Carlo simulation to option pricing theory was the adoption of Boyle
method of averaging discounted payoffs across a large number of simulated paths in the risk-neutral
distribution of market factors [6]. It is particularly suitable to path-dependent derivatives whose
value is determined by the overall price path, and not only by the end value. This was followed by
an analysis by Jasra and Del Moral of the applications of SMC in this context which have proven
useful in computing arithmetic Asian securities, and in the calculation of sensitivities in the context
of stochastic volatility [7]. The primary merit of Monte Carlo is its versatility, yet it possesses a cost
– it is extremely "heavy" in terms of processing and the quantity of paths that are simulated
constitutes a crucial variable in the precision of the algorithm.
2.1.4. Comparative research on the three types of models
Previous international investigations have performed a direct comparison among these three
frameworks [8-10]. Several practical researches demonstrate that these three methodologies are
remarkably similar regarding outcomes for simple European contracts. A binomial tree for CRR can
be employed to value American securities. Monte Carlo simulations are highly potent for path-
dependent derivatives. The Black-Scholes paradigm is suitable for deriving swift analytical
outcomes of European contracts but cannot be utilized for intricate elements like American-style
rights and path-dependent characteristics. Various investigations have identified that model
discrepancies stem from abstract assumptions, fragmented time intervals and the magnitude of
simulation trials. Precision and execution expense frequently clash in real-world applications.

3.2. No-arbitrage pricing principle
Arbitrage-free valuation represents the fundamental concept of derivative valuation. Arbitrage refers
to a risk-free gain with no initial outlay. Within an arbitrage-free market, securities possessing
identical risk and reward ought to have the identical price; otherwise, by modifying the discrepancy
between these values, one might find an arbitrage chance.
Both the Black-Scholes framework and the CRR binomial model adhere to replication portfolio
principles; a portfolio of underlying securities plus risk-free instruments is established to match the
option reward structure. At present, this replication cost corresponds exactly to the theoretical option
value. According to the no-arbitrage criterion, option prices remain unaffected by risk levels and
therefore can be calculated via risk-neutral valuation.

4.6. Comparative analysis of models
4.6.1. Pricing performance
When given the required time intervals of the CRR binomial tree and enough simulation paths of
Monte Carlo simulations, these three models produce strikingly the same values of SSE 50 ETF
European call options. Black-Scholes closed-form formula is the optimal theoretical benchmark in
risk-neutral pricing models.
CRR binomial tree is based on discrete backward. The few time intervals create large temporal
discrepancies, and the estimations of the time intervals introduce discretization errors resulting in it
not converging to Black-Scholes values. As the number of steps is greater, the number of steps
decreases and the CRR results eventually converge to the BS price. However, the lack of precision
arithmetic still has small numerical errors.
Statistical variations will have an effect on Monte Carlo results. According to the law of large
numbers, increasing the number of simulated paths decreases estimation dispersion and makes
estimated prices converge towards the Black Scholes target. However, such improvements in
sampling are not able to eliminate underlying inconsistencies that occur due to core assumptions
such as constant volatility.
Prices of non European items differ in efficiency. Black Scholes model does not manage early
exercise payouts of Americans. CRR tree has the inherent ability to support American contracts by
comparing continuation value with immediate payoff at each level. Monte Carlo Monte Carlo does
not include facilities to represent the American instruments and requires other methods, e.g. the least
squares Monte Carlo. In the case of path dependent exotic securities, Monte Carlo is better than
binomial models which face the problem of dimensionality.
4.6.2. Computational efficiency
This Black Scholes model gives the quickest valuation since it is a closed form mathematical
representation that does not require any iteration or stochastic processes. Whenever inputs are
entered the option values can be computed in real time. Its main disadvantage however is that it only
takes the European contracts and cannot estimate the exotic derivatives without coming up with
specific formulae.
The computation of the CRR binomial tree grows quadratically with time intervals. An increase
in the number of steps increases the lattice points, which increase memory requirements and
execution time. Though it works well with American early exercise, it is inefficient with high-
dimensional derivatives. In the case of path-dependent contracts, some extra path data are required
at every node, which increases computational requirements. In Python execution, CRR is executed
quickly and with few steps, but reduces significantly in speed when accuracy is crucial.
Monte Carlo simulation is ideal with path-dependent and high-dimensional derivatives. Its
implementation costs are mainly associated with the path volume, but not with the overall risk
factors. But American options cannot be directly valued using traditional Monte Carlo. Increased

5. Conclusions
The primary subject of investigation within this work involves SSE 50 ETF options: the Black
Scholes fundamental framework, the Cox Ross Rubinstein binomial tree technique, and Monte
Carlo simulation. In accordance with these experiments, the outcome of the three frameworks is
consistent in case of European options when all parameters are set to an ideal outcome. When model
validity conditions hold, the results of high-order step CRR binomial-tree computations as well as
huge-sample Monte-Carlo computations are astonishingly similar to the fundamental benchmarks of
Black Scholes, demonstrating conceptual consistency between no arbitrage based approaches to
valuing options and the binomial-tree.
The Black-Scholes framework is relatively straightforward to apply and provides almost instant
execution speed, rendering it extremely useful for rapid market evaluation and sensitivity
assessments for European-style SSE 50 ETF options. However, it is based on a set of ideal market
conditions: a steady volatility, a steady risk free interest rate, smooth markets and the trading of
assets constantly. In real Chinese ETF option markets, implied volatility swings between different
strike prices and different durations forming volatility smile patterns; risk free yield changes across
different macroeconomic periods; bid ask spreads, trading costs as well as position limits all present
real financial impediments. Such practical market failure deviations of theoretical bases are bound to

result in systematic valuation errors between theoretical Black Scholes valuation and actual SSE 50
ETF option marketplace valuation. Moreover, its closed form equations are unable to deal with the
American early exercise and most of the complex derivative cashflows which restricts its
functioning range.
The CRR binomial tree methodology presents a straightforward and transparent architecture.
Reverse induction from maturity effectively illustrates the risk-neutral pricing rationale, while it
inherently supports American-style early-exercise assessments by comparing the continuation value
against the immediate-exercise outcome at every lattice point. It is more accurate in its computations
with respect to increasing step count; otherwise exceptionally large time scales can create substantial
numerical discretization errors. Nonetheless, the quadratic growth of node density creates feasibility
constraints. In very high-dimensional multi-asset derivatives or other instruments with path
dependence, the binomial-tree approach suffers the curse of dimensionality and has prohibitive
computational and storage costs. Therefore, the CRR technique is most suitable for low-dimensional
European or American single-underlying securities like equity and ETF options.
Monte Carlo analysis is especially useful in high-dimensional, path-dependent derivatives
because its performance is mostly determined by the volume of the simulation path, rather than risk
factor dimensions, enabling it to have unique benefits when pricing Asian derivatives, barrier
contracts and other instruments whose payoffs are determined by full historical price patterns.
However, to obtain high accuracy and reduce sampling variations requires a large number of sample
paths, which greatly augment the cost of computation. Moreover, traditional vanilla Monte Carlo is
not able to price American options or would have to be adjusted through least-squares regression or
other optimal-stopping methods, introducing more implementation complexities and extra
processing overheads.
Pragmatically, model selection should strike a balance between the price precision requirements,
exact target contract features, and available computational resources. The Black Scholes analytical
model remains the most cost-conscious of the options to the rapid valuation of basic SSE 50 ETF
options. The CRR binomial tree is a reliable numerical alternative provided the elements of
American options are required and the principal asset is small. Monte Carlo simulation is the most
preferred method in cases where the security dimension is high or path dependent irrespective of the
comprehensive algorithmic intensity.
The current study has some limitations. First, using the theoretically computed constant
parameters instead of the rolling market-implied volatility may limit the ability of the models to
reflect the changing market dynamics. Second, the exploration relies largely on numerical
experiments and is not empirically verified using actual SSE 50 ETF option market quotes. Third,
model parameterization does not consider transaction costs, liquidity differences and volatility-smile
effect observed in the ETF option market in China. Future studies may combine market implied
volatility indices, apply actual high-frequency trading data, and market-friction corrections to
evaluate the true usefulness of these three pricing models in the Chinese ETF option market in more
depth. Besides, further studies may also compare refined model versions like stochastic volatility
models and least-squares Monte Carlo computing of American options, to explore development
avenues of pricing in the context of realistic market constraints.
