Cross-product Modelling in Continuous Intraday Electricity
Markets via Hawkes Processes
Ayoub Jhabli∗†, Tarek AlSkaif †, Kwabena E. Bennin †, Bedir Tekinerdogan †, and Joost M.E. Pennings §¶
†Information Technology Group, Wageningen University, The Netherlands
§Marketing and Consumer Behaviour, Wageningen University, The Netherlands
¶School of Business and Economics, Maastricht University, The Netherlands
Abstract—As renewable energy integration accelerates, intra-
day continuous (IDC) electricity markets have become essential
for short-term balancing. However, the simultaneous trading of
overlapping contracts with varying granularities creates com-
plex, hidden liquidity dependencies that are critical to market
stability but remain poorly understood. This paper proposes a
multivariate State-Dependent Hawkes Process (SDHP) to model
cross-product liquidity dynamics between Quarter-Hourly and
Hourly IDC contracts. To determine how cross-product effects
vary under different market conditions, we classify the trading
sessions into two distinct regimes: ’Normal’ and ’Stressed’, based
on a composite metric of the bid-ask spread, mid-price volatility
and queue imbalance. The model is calibrated on the first month
of each quarter of 2024 using level 3 limit order book (LOB)
data from the Dutch EPEX SPOT IDC market. We find that
order flow is overwhelmingly self-driven, with roughly 95% of
orders arriving in reaction to earlier orders in both regimes.
Cross-excitation runs mainly from the Quarter-Hourly to the
Hourly contract, but this leadership weakens under stress: the
ratio of cumulative cross-excitation falls from 2.8 to 1.5, so the two
contracts influence each other almost equally. Stress also makes
the Quarter-Hourly to Hourly channel much faster, cutting its
mean lag from 6.05 to 0.93 seconds. The strong seasonal variation
found in calm periods largely disappears under stress, where all
four months converge to the same structure.
I. INTRODUCTION
With rising shares of wind and solar generation, the intraday
continuous (IDC) market has become the main venue for
correcting imbalances between the day-ahead auction and real-
time balancing [1]. Participants use it to adjust positions after
the day-ahead result is known, typically in response to updated
wind and solar PV power forecasts [2], plant outages or revised
demand [3]. Unlike the day-ahead auction, which clears all
orders at a single price, IDC markets match orders one by
one as they arrive. A trade occurs whenever a buy and a sell
order meet, so a single delivery period generates a sequence
of prices rather than one, which exchanges summarise through
volume-weighted indices such as EPEX’s IDFull, ID3 and ID1
[4], [5], [6], [7].
At European level, continuous intraday trading is coordi-
nated through the Single Intraday Coupling (SIDC) project,
implemented on the M7 Cross-Border Intraday (XBID) plat-
form [8], [9]. XBID connects the Nominated Electricity Mar-
ket Operators through a central Shared Order Book (SOB),
where market participants across Europe are able to trade

EPEX SPOT, one of the largest European electricity ex-

continuous trading: Hourly, Half-hourly and Quarter-hourly
individual contracts going 24/7 all year round [11]. Trading
takes place through a limit order book (LOB), which makes the
market structurally similar to an electronic financial market,
though with important differences arising from the physical
delivery [12].
Liquidity determines how cheap trading can be. In a liquid
book a participant can correct an imbalance quickly at a
price close to the prevailing mid; in an illiquid one the same
correction moves the price against them or cannot be executed
at all. Liquidity in power markets has therefore been studied
through the traded volume, bid-ask spread, market depth and
resiliency [13]. Empirical work on the German market shows
that bid-ask spreads widen with risk and narrow with trading
activity and competition close to delivery [14]. Liquidity is
also uneven across granularities: Quarter-hourly products are
consistently thinner than Hourly products, which is why much
of the forecasting and execution literature restricts attention to
the Hourly contracts.
The observation that contract dynamics change as delivery
approaches is not specific to electricity. Samuelson [15] argued
that the volatility of a futures contract rises as it nears maturity,
because information about the terminal value arrives faster and
matters more. In electricity this mechanism is present as well.
Weather forecasts sharpen as delivery approaches, imbalances
become known, and participants must close their positions
before gate closure [16]. Empirical studies confirm the pattern
in IDC power markets: volatility, trade frequency and order
book activity all increase towards gate closure [17], [18]. The
intensity of orders can therefore not be treated as constant
through a session.
Point processes provide a natural way to model the intensity
of orders, because they describe events in continuous time
rather than on a fixed grid. Hawkes process, introduced to
model earthquake aftershocks [19], is the standard choice
when events cluster. Its defining feature is self-excitation:
each event excites further events for a period afterwards, with
the increase decaying over time. The framework has been

extended in several directions. Multivariate specifications treat
several event types jointly and estimate the full matrix of
self- and cross-excitation [20], [21]. Marked Hawkes processes
attach a variable to each event, such as a trade size or a jump
magnitude, allowing the strength of excitation to depend on it
[22]. Regime-switching and Markov-modulated specifications
let the parameters vary with a latent state, capturing alterna-
tion between quiet and bursty periods [23]. State-dependent
Hawkes processes (SDHP) instead couple the process to an
observable state, so that excitation depends on the current
condition of the market and the state itself evolves with the
events [24].
In financial markets these models have been applied ex-
tensively to LOBs [25]. They have been used to estimate
transaction-level volatility and to explain volatility clustering
[20], to model the joint arrival of market orders, limit orders
and cancellations [26], to specify multitype marked Hawkes
processes nonparametrically with state-dependent baselines
driven by order book imbalance [27], and to quantify how
much activity is generated endogenously by the market itself
rather than by external factors [28], [29]. That last strand is
directly relevant here: branching-ratio estimates close to one
imply that most observed activity is a reaction to previous
activity, a property associated with long memory in order flow
and with fragility, since shocks are damped only weakly [30].
A parallel literature models the order book through its queues
rather than its event intensities, with state-dependent arrival
rates that respond to the configuration of the book [31], [32].
Applications to electricity markets are more recent but
growing. Hawkes models have been used to describe market
order arrivals in the German IDC market, where self-excitation
is found to be strong but short-lived and the estimated pa-
rameters vary systematically across delivery hours [33]. They
have been combined with transient price impact models to
derive optimal execution strategies for participants trading
large volumes, reproducing the intraday activity profile and
the Samuelson effect [34]. They have also been extended with
exogenous covariates, showing that trading activity responds
not only to its own past but to vRES forecast errors and
to activated balancing volumes [35]. Related point-process
work models the transaction arrival process directly [36], and
Hawkes specifications have also been used to detect manip-
ulative order flow [37]. Alongside this, a separate literature
studies intraday liquidity and order book structure without
point processes, through spreads, depth and execution cost
[14], [38], [39].
The same contract can be traded in a liquid book with tight
spreads for much of its session and in a thin, volatile book
shortly afterwards, and conditions differ across delivery hours
and seasons. If the way order flow propagates depends on these
changing conditions, then a model with constant parameters
estimates an average across regimes that may describe neither.
This is a problem for cross-product analysis in particular.
Whether activity in the quarter-hourly book drives the hourly
book, or the reverse, is a statement about the structure of
transmission, and there is no reason to expect that structure
to be the same when the book is deep and when it is
nearly empty. A constant-parameter model cannot answer this
question, because it imposes the same structure across different
conditions. Allowing the baseline intensity and the excitation
kernel in Hawkes models to depend on an observable measure
of liquidity stress separates the two cases: exogenous order
arrivals and the market’s reaction to its own past are then
estimated within each regime, and the difference between
regimes becomes the object of study rather than an assumption.
That conditioning on liquidity stress matters has already been
shown for this market. In earlier work on Dutch intraday
hourly products, regimes defined by a liquidity stress index
built from the bid-ask spread, order book volume and mid-
price volatility fitted the order flow considerably better than
a single-regime benchmark, and order flow was found to be
strongly self-exciting and near-critical [40].
In the financial literature, the predominant methodology
for measuring liquidity spillovers relies on the generalized
vector autoregressive (V AR) framework and variance decom-
position introduced by Diebold and Yilmaz [41]. This macro-
econometric approach has been widely adapted to quantify
directional spillovers across various domains, such as assessing
global risk in foreign exchange markets [42] and analyzing
the simultaneous dry-up of liquidity between ETFs and their
underlying constituent stocks [43]. However, these variance
decomposition methods operate on temporally aggregated,
lower-frequency data, making them inadequate for model-
ing event-based data. Multivariate Hawkes processes have
emerged as an alternative by capturing the mutually exciting
nature of market shocks [44], [45]. Yet, these existing point-
process applications in this context still fall short of captur-
ing continuous market microstructure dynamics; they either
integrate Hawkes intensities with aggregated DCC-GARCH
frameworks for macro-level contagion [45], or they restrict the
event space to extreme, threshold-triggered illiquidity drops
rather than modeling the continuous arrival of high-frequency
events [44].
Three research gaps follow. First, cross-product spillovers
in IDC electricity markets are largely understudied. Most
empirical work studies a single granularity, usually the hourly
contract. How much activity in one transmits to the other, in
which direction, and how quickly, is not established.
Second, the effect of liquidity conditions on excitation
across products is not understood. The endogeneity literature
reports branching ratios close to one in financial markets, and
[40] studies how stress reshapes excitation within a contract,
how endogenous electricity markets are but it is unknown
whether stress changes how much activity spills between
different contracts, or in which direction.
Third, kernel choice is usually asserted rather than tested, so
the number of timescales in the market’s memory is imposed
and not empirically established.
The SDHP model, the LSI-based regimes, the regime-
dependent baseline and excitation, and the spectral-radius
penalty follow [40]. This paper makes the following contri-
butions.
•Kernel comparison:We replace the single-exponential
kernel of [40] with three exponential-mixture kernels and
a power-law kernel, and select the best performing one
for economic interpretation.
•Estimation at scale:We extend the analytical gradients
of [40] to both kernel families, which makes it feasible
to estimate the model separately for the first month of
each quarter, covering 2,915 delivery contracts.
•Cross-product spillovers:We measure spillovers between

II. EUROPEANINTRADAYCONTINUOUSPOWERMARKETS
In the IDC market, market participants can trade several
products until shortly before delivery. In 2024, trading for
intrazonal contracts remained open until 5 minutes before
delivery, while XBID contracts closed 1 hour before delivery.
For both intrazonal and XBID markets, several granulari-
ties exist: hourly, half-hourly, and quarter-hourly. All these
contracts open for trading at 14:00 CET on the day before
delivery and can be traded simultaneously. Given the multitude
of concurrent contracts available per delivery period, not all
of them attract sufficient liquidity to support healthy trading
dynamics. Figure 1 shows the total traded volume per contract
type over time in the Netherlands. It reveals the rapid adoption
and growing popularity of XBID products, particularly the
hourly XBID contract. In fact, in the Netherlands in 2024, the
hourly XBID was the most heavily traded contract in terms
of the total number of trades, submitted orders, and trading
volume, followed by the quarter-hourly XBID products. This
suggests that market participants remain dependent on the
classical hourly market structure while increasingly utilizing
quarter-hourly contracts for finer balancing. This dynamic
motivates our choice to study the relationship between these
two specific products, which constituted 86% of the total
traded volume in the Netherlands in 2024.
A. Clustering of Orders
Point processes are a class of stochastic processes where one
realization is defined by a single point in time. The simplest
example of a point process is the Poisson point process, in
which events occur randomly and independently from each
other over time at a constant rate. In a Poisson process, the
occurrence of one event has no influence on the likelihood of
future events, meaning the process has no memory and does
not generate clustering beyond what would arise by chance.
In contrast, a Hawkes process is a self-exciting point process
in which past events increase the probability of future events
occurring shortly afterward. This generates temporal clustering
and dependence, as each event can trigger subsequent events,
leading to bursts of activity followed by periods of calm.
Figure 2 shows this phenomenon for IDC electricity
markets. Rather than being evenly dispersed over time, order
arrivals tend to occur in bursts, with periods of intense activity
followed by relatively quiet intervals. The color scale repre-
sents the density of order arrivals. The figure reveals that high-
density regions are concentrated in the minutes immediately
preceding delivery, indicating an intensification of trading
activity as maturity approaches. This pattern is consistent
with the Samuelson effect, which states that volatility and
trading intensity tend to increase as a contract approaches its
delivery time. This temporal clustering is inconsistent with
the independence assumption of the Poisson process but is
naturally explained by a self-exciting mechanism in which
the arrival of an order increases the likelihood of subsequent
orders.
III. MODEL ANDTRAINING
This section provides the full SDHP model. We start by
briefly introducing the data and the training setup. We then
define the model mathematically. Next, we present the likeli-
hood, its gradients, the stability of the model and the spillover.
A. Data and Training Setup
We estimate SDHP models following [24] and extending
their framework in two key directions. First, we allow the
baseline intensities to be state-dependent, enabling the model
to capture changes in autonomous order flow across different
market regimes. Second, we replace the single exponential
decay with a multi-exponential kernel to simultaneously cap-
ture the disparate timescales of fast algorithmic reactions and
slower, macro-level trading flow.
For our empirical application, we define a single trading
session as the order flow of one specific Hourly XBID
contract alongside its four corresponding Quarter-Hourly (Q)
constituent contracts spanning the same delivery hour. We
focus on the final five hours of trading for every chosen
session. However, because XBID contracts close exactly one
hour prior to their specific delivery start time, the Gate Closure
Times (GCT) of the Hourly and Quarter-Hourly contracts
are staggered. To ensure the multivariate Hawkes process is
calibrated strictly on synchronous LOB dynamics, we truncate
the data to the overlapping active window (illustrated as the
grey period in Figure 3), during which all five contracts
are simultaneously open for trading. This truncation is a
methodological necessity: including non-overlapping periods
where certain contracts are closed would introduce artificial
intervals of zero activity. The model would falsely interpret
these structural market closures as a genuine lack of liquidity
or cross-product interaction, severely biasing the estimation of
both the baseline intensities and the cross-excitation kernels.
Therefore, we treat each of these precisely defined, fully
synchronous sessions as an independent realization.
We fit each delivery contract independently. Our calibration
uses contracts from the first month of each quarter of 2024
(January, April, July, and October), which spans seasonal
variation in the Dutch IDC market.

The choice of the75 th percentile as the threshold is
motivated by two key methodological considerations. First,
calibrating a SDHP requires a statistically significant number
of events in both regimes to robustly estimate the state-specific
baseline intensities and cross-excitation kernels; selecting a
higher threshold (e.g., the95 th percentile) would result in
data sparsity and noisy parameter estimates for the stressed
state. Second, this threshold ensures that the ”Stressed” regime
represents a genuine, recurring structural state of the market,
rather than simply capturing isolated microstructural anoma-
lies or extreme outliers. Finally, this definition ensures that
the stressed state corresponds to periods of persistently high
volatility and wide bid–ask spreads, while the queue imbalance
provides complementary information about the asymmetry of
available liquidity between the bid and ask sides of the order

not covertly encode time of day.
To confirm that a single fixed threshold transports across
sessions, we compare the global75th percentile against its per-
session counterparts and assess its out-of-sample calibration;
results are summarized in Table I. The per-session thresholds
cluster tightly around the global value, and the small median
pairwise Wasserstein distance between session distributions
indicates that sessions are similar though not identical. We also
conducted a leave-one-session-out calibration: for each session
we apply the threshold estimated on all remaining sessions and
record the realized fraction of stressed events. This fraction is
centered on the25%target and stays within[0.18,0.32]across
every session. No session collapses to a single regime—even
the calmest retains18%stressed events—so the state variable
exercises both regimes in every session and the state-dependent
kernels remain identifiable throughout.
We therefore adopt the global thresholdq glob0.75 = 0.264.
The LSI was introduced in our earlier work [40], where
we assessed its robustness to the choice of components and
threshold levels, as well as the contribution of each component
to regime transitions. In that specification, the LSI combined
the bid–ask spread, the mid-price volatility and the total depth
of the LOB. The robustness checks showed that the sign with
which depth entered the index had little effect on the resulting
classification, indicating that the component carried limited
information about stress. We therefore replace it here with the
absolute queue imbalance (Eq. 2), a depth-based quantity with
an unambiguous directional interpretation: values close to one
indicate a one-sided, fragile book, while values close to zero
indicate balanced liquidity on both sides.

Equation (11) can be verified directly. It is an instance of

form (11) necessarily exists; the Gamma weight identifies the
corresponding mixture. The same representation holds for any
completely monotone kernel.

two unrelated functional forms, the choice between them
comes back to an empirical question:how many distinct
timescales (i.e., exponential components) are required to de-
scribe the market’s memory?

I. Limitations of the Power-Law Approximation
The truncation introduced in Section III-E1 warrants explicit
discussion.
The exponential kernels are evaluatedexactly: the recursion
(14) is algebraically equivalent to the untruncated intensity.
The power-law likelihood, by contrast, is evaluated on a
kernel truncated at∆ max, and therefore approximates different
quantity. Two consequences follow.
First, the log-likelihood attained by the power-law specifi-
cation is an approximation whose bias decreases with∆ max
9
but does not vanish. Any comparison of likelihood-based
quantities across the two families must be read in that light,
since one is computed exactly and the other is not.
Second, the branching integral (16) for the power-law
integrates the kernel over[0,∞), whereas the likelihood
constrains the kernel only on[0,∆ max]. The tail mass beyond
the truncation horizon is therefore extrapolated rather than
estimated, andρ x inherits that extrapolation—an effect that
grows asη (x)e′→e ↓1, precisely where the tail is heaviest. To
quantify it, we report alongsideρ x the truncated branching
radius obtained by integratingκonly to∆ max; a material gap
between the two indicates that the stability estimate is driven
by unobserved tail mass.
These limitations follow from the absence of a recursive
representation for the power-law kernel that makes it com-
putationally heavy, and they are the price of relaxing the
assumption of a finite number of timescales.
IV. RESULTS
A. Kernel Comparison and Model Selection
We estimate the model separately for each contract, using
the first month of each quarter of 2024: January, April, July
and October of the Dutch XBID IDC market from EPEX
SPOT. Each contract is fitted with four excitation kernels: the
one-, two- and three-component exponential mixtures (1-Exp,
2-Exp, 3-Exp) and the power-law kernel. This yields 2,915
contract-level fits.
This subsection compares the four kernels and selects the
specification used in the remainder of the paper. The com-
parison uses five criteria, reported in the following order. We
begin with two formal tests on the time-rescaled residuals:
the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test of the marginal distribution
and the Ljung–Box test of residual independence. We then
report the Wasserstein distance between the empirical residual
distribution and its theoretical counterpart, which measures
how far a model is from correct specification. We then turn to
two likelihood-based criteria, the negative log-likelihood per
event and the pairwise probability that one kernel attains a
higher likelihood than another on the same contract. Finally,
we report the share of estimations that converged, since a
specification that fits well but converges rarely is not usable.
All criteria are computed per contract and then aggregated,
first by month and then across the four months.
