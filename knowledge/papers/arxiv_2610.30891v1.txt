Abstract
We propose a market-making game played among N≥ 2 strategic market makers and a
single non-strategic representative trader. In a continuous quote space, standard finite-game
arguments do not apply, while non-convex and discontinuous payoffs rule out classical
continuous existence results and complicate learning. Nonetheless, we characterize pure-
strategy Nash equilibria (PSNEs) and show that at least one PSNE always exists under
minimal assumptions on the underlying asset value distribution, and that the equilibrium
bid–ask spread widens strictly with asset value volatility. To obtain a tractable learning
framework, we discretize prices and, under an additional regularity condition, show that
the time-averaged play of no-internal-regret learners approaches the continuous Nash price
envelope as the tick size vanishes. Finally, we empirically validate our theoretical results
through numerical simulations.
Keywords:Market making, Online learning, Equilibrium selection, Market microstructure
1. Introduction
Classical models of price competition, such as the Bertrand oligopoly model (Bertrand,
1883), elegantly explain why competition may drive prices toward marginal cost. However,
applying this one-sided logic directly to financial market making overlooks a fundamental
structural feature of liquidity provision: market makers operate simultaneously on both the
buy and sell sides of the market, and their quotes actively shape the direction of incoming
order flow.
This two-sided interaction is particularly prominent in financial markets characterized by
inelastic trading demand, such as commodity markets (Bogmans et al., 2024), where overall
trading volume does not respond to price changes and market participants simply choose
whether to buy or sell. In conventional Bertrand competition, a firm’s price reduction allows
it to gain market share at the cost of a lower margin; in contrast, liquidity providers in
inelastic markets post bid and ask quotes that establish a marketmid-price, which acts as
a threshold determining whether buying or selling is advantageous relative to the asset’s
fundamental value, ultimately dictating trade direction and, indirectly, execution priority.
To study this mechanism, and how makers learn in it, we model liquidity provision
as a static game played among N≥ 2 strategic market makers and a single non-strategic
representative trader. The former simultaneously post bid–ask quotes for a single asset with
uncertain fundamental value, while the latter, upon observing the posted quotes, submits
either a buy or a sell order according to a fixed decision rule that compares the realized
fundamental value of the asset with the market mid-price induced by the quoted prices.

• Nash Equilibrium Existence (Section 3):We characterize pure-strategy Nash equi-

market makers, serving as an explicit reference model for competitive liquidity provision.
• Impact of V olatility on Competitive Pricing (Section 3.1):We investigate how
fundamental market uncertainty shapes equilibrium quotes. For location-scale families

that spread widenings can arise purely from competitive risk adjustments, rather than
algorithmic collusion (Calvano et al., 2020; Harrington, 2018) or a higher share of informed
traders (Glosten and Milgrom, 1985).

additional regularity condition, we prove that all grid correlated equilibria uniformly
concentrate on the continuous Nash price envelope as ∆ → 0. Consequently, the time-
averaged play of adaptive market makers using no-internal-regret learning algorithms is
guaranteed to asymptotically approach the continuous competitive benchmark.
Finally, theoretical predictions and learning dynamics are empirically validated through
numerical simulations in Section 5.
1.1. T echnical Challenges
Analyzing this market-making game presents distinct theoretical and algorithmic challenges:
• Two-Sided Coupling:Updating a quote shifts the implied mid-price, directly coupling
execution risk across both sides of the book. As a result, the game cannot be reduced to
two independent Bertrand competitions. Indeed, since strategies consist of quote pairs,
market makers can execute cross-side deviations (e.g., aggressively lowering the ask while
retreating on the bid), thereby expanding the space of potentially profitable strategies in
ways that break standard single-side equilibrium arguments.
• Continuous vs. Discrete Tension:Payoffs in this game are inherently non-convex
and discontinuous. In continuous quote spaces, this rules out standard PSNE existence
theorems and complicates learning dynamics. Discretizing prices resolves these hurdles,
but it introduces grid-dependent equilibria that obscure the model’s true structure and
parameter dependencies.

1.2. Related W ork
This work connects to market microstructure, game theory, and learning in games; we review
the most closely related literature.
Price Competition.Among classical oligopoly models (Cournot, 1838; Bertrand, 1883),
Bertrand competition is the closest benchmark to our setting: a marginal price improvement
causes a discontinuous jump in demand, which complicates equilibrium existence. Pure-
strategy equilibria can be recovered under additional assumptions, such as quasiconcave
payoffs (Dasgupta and Maskin, 1986; Reny, 1999) or suitable cost and demand conditions,
possibly with multiple equilibria (Dastidar, 1995). Neither applies here: in particular,
quasiconcavity fails for bid–ask pairs, since averaging two profitable one-sided deviations can
leave a maker inactive on both sides. Our existence proof instead exploits the monotonicity
of the midpoint operator via Tarski’s theorem (Tarski, 1955), as in games with strategic
complementarities, where extremal equilibria likewise bracket the equilibrium set (Milgrom
and Roberts, 1990; Vives, 1990), although our game is not supermodular.
Market Microstructure.Seminal models study how informed order flow (Glosten and
Milgrom, 1985; Kyle, 1985; Easley and O’Hara, 1987) and inventory risk (Ho and Stoll,
1981) shape spreads, typically assuming that dealers break even. Our zero-profit quotes
mirror the upper-tail pricing of Glosten (1994), but arise as a Nash outcome among finitely
many strategic makers, as in models of competing dealers (Dennert, 1993; Biais et al., 2000;
Back and Baruch, 2013). Unlike these works, which rely on noise traders to avoid no-trade
outcomes (Milgrom and Stokey, 1982), we consider a fully informed trader with inelastic
demand, so that quotes determine the direction of trade rather than its occurrence. Spreads
are also well known to widen with volatility (Copeland and Galai, 1983; Ho and Stoll, 1981);
we show that, within location-scale families, they scale exactly linearly. Finally, our grid
game relates to the tick-size literature (Anshuman and Kalay, 1998; Cartea et al., 2022),
whose small-tick limit we analyze.
Learning in Games and Market Making.Our guarantees build on online learn-
ing (Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006; Orabona, 2023) and on the convergence of no-internal-
regret dynamics to correlated equilibria (Foster and Vohra, 1997; Hart and Mas-Colell,
2000; Blum and Mansour, 2007). Unlike Stoltz and Lugosi (2007), who learn over compact
strategy sets with continuous payoffs, we approximate our discontinuous game by grid
games. In non-strategic environments, online learning has been used to learn market-making
strategies (Abernethy and Kale, 2013; Cesa-Bianchi et al., 2025; Maran and Restelli, 2026),
via reductions to first-price auctions (Achddou et al., 2021; Cesa-Bianchi et al., 2024; Han
et al., 2024) and dynamic pricing (Cesa-Bianchi et al., 2019; Tullii et al., 2025), while
agent-based studies train makers via reinforcement learning (Spooner et al., 2018; Ganesh
et al., 2019); here, we characterize the equilibria that interacting learners approach.
Algorithmic Collusion.Learning agents can sustain supra-competitive prices (Calvano
et al., 2020; Klein, 2021; Banchio and Skrzypacz, 2022; Hansen et al., 2021), also in dealer
markets (Cont and Xiong, 2024; Colliard et al., 2022), raising regulatory concerns (Harrington,
2018); no-swap-regret learning rules out some of these outcomes (Arunachaleswaran et al.,
2025), motivating our choice of learners. Hartline et al. (2024) detect collusion from prices

3. Equilibrium Concept
In this section, we analyze the equilibrium properties of the proposed market-making game.
Our main goal is to prove that at least one pure-strategy Nash equilibrium always exists.
Establishing existence is nontrivial in our setting, as the strategy space is continuous and the
payoff functions are non-convex and discontinuous due to Bertrand-style price competition
and tie-breaking in order allocation, precluding a direct application of standard equilibrium
existence results.
Definition 1 (Pure-Strategy Nash Equilibrium). A strategy profilesis apure-
strategy Nash equilibrium(PSNE) if, for every market maker i∈ N and every feasible
deviationesi := (ebi,eai)∈ Si, we have:
ui(esi,s −i)≤u i(si,s −i).
In our two-sided market setting, a deviation esi allows market maker i to unilaterally
alter its ask quote, its bid quote or both, simultaneously. As previously mentioned, such
a deviation can affect not only the execution price and the maker’s share of the existing

To establish existence, we first derive the necessary conditions that a Nash equilibrium
profile must satisfy.
Observation 2. In any PSNE, the expected profit from each side of the book must be
zero independently. To see this, first observe that a strategy yielding negative expected
payoff cannot be optimal, since quoting the boundary prices, i.e., si = (0, 1), guarantees
a non-negative payoff for every realization of V . Hence, the expected profit must be non-
negative. On the other hand, strictly positive expected profits cannot be sustained: if a side
of the book generated strictly positive expected profit, a competing maker could undercut the
corresponding quote by an arbitrarily small amount and capture all of the resulting order
flow at a slightly less favorable price. Finally, because makers can adjust their quotes on
the two sides independently, positive profits on one side cannot compensate for losses on the
other: a maker could withdraw from the loss-making side while retaining the profitable side.
Observation 3. As in standard Bertrand competition, any PSNE must have at least two
market makers quoting the best ask and bid, i.e., nA(a), nB(b) ≥ 2. The usual undercutting
argument must account for the fact that quotes also affect the mid-price. If, for instance,
nA(a) = 1, the sole ask maker can execute a joint deviation by raising its ask by a small
ε >0, while improving its bid by a much smaller amount. By shifting the mid-price upward,
this deviation increases the margin on buy orders and converts marginal buy orders into sell
orders captured by its new bid, yielding a strictly positive expected profit.

concepts, such as mixed-strategy Nash equilibria (MSNEs) and correlated equilibria (CEs),
is a natural and interesting next step. Randomization and correlation can readily be layered
onto our results without generating new economic outcomes:
• Mixed Strategies:Passive makers, those quoting strictly non-competitively ( ai > a(m∗)
and bi < b(m∗)), can randomize freely, because they remain non-executing, so the prevailing
best quotes remain bound to the zero-profit competitive threshold.
• Correlated Strategies:A public correlation device can synchronize agents on one of the
(possibly multiple) PSNEs of Theorem 12, again leaving the zero-profit structure intact.
What we cannot show in general is the converse: that these are theonlyroutes to an
MSNE or CE. The obstacle lies specifically in randomization: against a genuinely mixing
opponent, a deviation’s profitability becomes an expectation over its randomized quotes,
whose sign is hard to control without further structure on F . Indeed, under the regularity
of Section 4 and uniqueness of the PSNE mid-price, we establish an asymptotic version of
this characterization, but achieving it without such assumptions remains a nontrivial open
problem.

4. Learning
We ask whether adaptive makers can recover the competitive price structure without
knowing their payoff functions. Continuous-action bandit guarantees typically require

6. Conclusion and Future Work
We modeled market making as a game in which makers post bid–ask quotes and an inelastic
trader, who knows the asset value, always trades at the best quotes, buying whenever
the value exceeds the market mid-price and selling otherwise. In this game, pure-strategy
Nash equilibria always exist, and all equilibrium mid-prices lie between two extremal ones.
These equilibria also pin down how competitive spreads depend on risk: within location-
scale families, they grow linearly with the volatility of the asset. Finally, we proved that,
under an additional regularity condition, no-internal-regret learners recover this competitive
benchmark, with time-averaged market prices that lie close to the Nash price envelope on a
fine price grid.
This benchmark clarifies what competitive pricing looks like in adaptive markets. In
particular, when the asset becomes more volatile, the spreads of all makers widen together,
with no coordination among them. Since algorithmic-collusion tests are designed to flag
exactly this kind of simultaneous widening across makers, they should control for volatility,
which is nontrivial in markets with incomplete information, where volatility itself must be
estimated.
Future work includes a finer analysis of learning: we only showed that time-averaged
market prices approach the Nash price envelope, leaving open whether learners concentrate

in Section 4, to characterize mixed-strategy and correlated equilibria in the general model,
and to extend the model to inventory, latency, heterogeneous order flow and time-varying
fundamentals.
