Abstract
Estimating extreme risk in reinforcement learning becomes increasingly difficult as the
confidence level increases, since only a small number of observations contribute to empirical
estimates of Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). In this paper, we extend an Extreme Value
Theory (EVT) approach for CVaR optimization to high-dimensional neural-network policies.
Using a peaks-over-threshold representation and method-of-moments estimation, we obtain
a differentiable tail-risk objective and show that, conditional on the selected threshold, its
finite-sample gradient can be computed directly through automatic differentiation. We first
validate the approach in a controlled experiment with a known optimal solution and then
investigate its performance in a deep-hedging problem with rare extreme market movements.
Our results show similar hedging behavior for EVT- and empirical-CVaR-trained policies
under normal conditions, while EVT-trained policies exhibit lower hedging losses under
the extreme-scenario evaluation across the training sample sizes considered. These results
suggest that EVT can provide a practical alternative for training neural policies when the
observations relevant to extreme risk are scarce.
Keywords:Extreme Value Theory; Reinforcement Learning; Policy Gradient; Tail Risk.

1 Introduction
Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a flexible framework for sequential decision problems
in which actions are selected to optimize an objective over a multi-period horizon. In risk
management applications, however, optimizing average performance may not adequately account
for rare but potentially severe losses. Risk-aware RL addresses this problem by considering
objectives such as the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), which explicitly focuses on the tail of
the loss distribution. The estimation of such objectives becomes increasingly difficult at high
confidence levels, since only a small fraction of the observations generated during training directly
characterize the extreme tail.
Extreme Value Theory (EVT) provides a natural framework to address this scarcity of tail obser-
vations. Under the peaks-over-threshold (POT) approach, observations exceeding a sufficiently
high threshold are modeled using a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD), allowing threshold
exceedances to be used to extrapolate the behavior of the extreme tail and estimate extreme risk
measures; see, e.g., Troop et al. (2021). Building on this idea, Davar et al. (2025) introduces an
EVT-based policy-gradient methodology in which POT estimates of CVaR are incorporated into
the optimization of risk-aware RL policies. Their numerical results show the potential benefits of
the approach when observations in the extreme tail are scarce, particularly for heavy-tailed loss
distributions.
The methodology of Davar et al. (2025), however, relies on finite differences to approximate
the gradient of the EVT-based objective with respect to the policy parameters. This requires
repeatedly evaluating the objective after modifying individual policy parameters, making the
procedure increasingly computationally restrictive as the number of policy parameters grows.
This limitation concerns primarily the dimension of the policy parameterization rather than the
dimension of the underlying sequential decision problem. Extending the POT framework to
neural-network policies would therefore allow EVT-based tail-risk optimization to be combined
with the flexibility offered by deep RL.
In this paper, we investigate this extension by constructing an EVT-based loss that can be
2
differentiated with respect to the parameters of a neural-network policy. The proposed approach
estimates the tail of the loss distribution using the POT methodology and propagates the resulting
tail-risk objective through the policy using automatic differentiation. We complement the proposed
methodology with a theoretical analysis of the differentiability and statistical properties of the
EVT-based objective. In particular, the analysis distinguishes the local differentiability of the
finite-sample EVT objective from the statistical consistency of the corresponding tail-risk estimator
under explicit assumptions on the excess distribution.
We study the methodology numerically in two stages. We first consider a controlled experiment
in which the optimal policy is analytically tractable. This setting allows us to compare the
numerical behavior of the empirical and EVT-based optimization procedures against a known
solution and to examine how their performance changes as the amount of information available
for estimation increases. We then consider neural-network policies, using deep hedging as the
principal application. Deep hedging provides a natural setting for this analysis since the terminal
hedging error defines a loss distribution whose adverse tail is directly relevant for risk management,
while the policy itself can be represented by a neural network Buehler et al. (2019).
Our analysis therefore extends EVT-based policy optimization from low-dimensional policy
parameterizations relying on finite-difference gradients to neural-network policies trained through
automatic differentiation. The theoretical analysis investigates the conditions supporting this
extension, while the numerical experiments examine its behavior first in a controlled environment
and subsequently in a deep-hedging setting. In the latter, we investigate whether explicitly
modeling the tail during training provides an advantage over empirical CVaR optimization as
losses become more extreme and tail observations increasingly scarce.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the risk-aware rein-
forcement learning problem, the EVT framework for tail-risk estimation, and the EVT-based
policy optimization approach on which our methodology builds. Section 3 presents the proposed
extension to neural-network policies and the corresponding theoretical results. Section 4 presents
the numerical study, beginning with a controlled experiment and subsequently considering the
3
deep-hedging application. Finally, Section 5 concludes.

theory framework used to estimate losses in the far tail of the distribution. We then describe the
peaks-over-threshold policy gradient approach introduced by Davar et al. (2025), on which our
methodology builds. The presentation is restricted to the mathematical objects required for the
methodological extension developed in the following section.

relatively few observations reach the target confidence levelα.

optimization.
2.3 EVT-Based Policy Optimization
The connection between EVT-based tail estimation and reinforcement learning was introduced
by Davar et al. (2025) through the peaks-over-threshold policy gradient (POTPG) algorithm.
The main idea is to replace the direct empirical estimate of CVaR by the POT estimator in
Equation 2.9 during policy optimization.
At a given parameter valueθ, independent trajectories are generated underπθ, producing a sample
Xθ,1, . . . , Xθ,n of policy losses. A thresholdu is selected, the GPD parameters are estimated from
the corresponding exceedances, and the resulting POT approximation is used to estimate the
objective in Equation 2.3. The corresponding sample optimization problem can therefore be
represented as
bθ ⋆n,α ∈arg minθ∈Θ bJ EVTn,α (θ;u).(2.10)
In this way, policy learning does not rely exclusively on the relatively small number of observations
beyond qα,θ. Instead, observations exceedingu are used to model the upper tail and extrapolate
toward the region associated with the target confidence level.
To optimize Equation 2.10, Davar et al. (2025) estimate the gradient coordinatewise using forward

where ei denotes the ith canonical basis vector. The resulting gradient estimate can then be
incorporated into a stochastic optimization procedure to update the policy parameters.
An important aspect of POTPG is that the threshold selected under the current policy is kept
fixed when evaluating the coordinatewise perturbations in Equation 2.11. Hence, each perturbed
policy changes the estimated tail distribution while the threshold used to define the exceedance
region remains unchanged within the corresponding gradient evaluation.
This construction provides a direct connection between EVT-based tail estimation and risk-aware
policy learning. However, Equation 2.11 requires an additional evaluation of the EVT objective
for every coordinate ofθ. Therefore, as the dimensionp of the policy parameterization increases,
so does the number of objective evaluations required to construct a single gradient. This becomes
particularly restrictive when πθ is represented by a neural network with a large number of
parameters.
The following section builds on the same POT representation of the risk objective, but reformulates
its optimization to exploit the differentiable structure of neural-network-parameterized policies.
3 EVT-Based Optimization of Neural-Network Policies
The EVT-based policy optimization framework introduced in subsection 2.3 relies on the finite-
difference approximation in Equation 2.11. While suitable for low-dimensional policy parameteri-
zations, its computational cost increases with the number of policy parameters, limiting its direct
application to neural-network policies.
We address this limitation by formulating the POT-MOM estimator in Equation 2.9 as a
differentiable empirical objective. This allows its gradient with respect to all policy parameters
to be computed jointly using automatic differentiation, without modifying the underlying EVT
approximation. In addition, when the threshold-selection procedure does not identify a threshold

for which the GPD hypothesis is not rejected, the method falls back to empirical CVaR.

network consists of four hidden layers with56neurons each and a single output representing the
position in the underlying asset, following the standard architecture considered for asymmetric
penalty measures in François et al. (2024b). The output layer incorporates the dynamic activation
function proposed in François et al. (2024a), with leverage constraintB = 100. This constraint
prevents the agent from adopting doubling strategies, whereby increasingly large positions are
taken following losses in an attempt to recover previous hedging errors, which can lead to unrealistic
policies and distort the assessment of tail-risk performance. The network is trained using the
ADAM optimizer with an initial learning rate of0.0005and dropout regularization with probability
p = 0.5. The empirical-CVaR and EVT-based policies use the same state representation, network
architecture, leverage constraint, optimizer, learning rate, dropout specification, training paths,
and epoch budget, such that the tail-risk objective is the experimental factor being changed.
Both policies are trained to minimize CVaR at the confidence levelα= 0.999.

4.3 Neural-Network Experiments
We first investigate whether replacing empirical CVaR with the EVT-based objective affects
the training behavior of neural-network policies when extreme observations are not especially
prominent. To account for the stochasticity of neural-network optimization, we train otherwise
identical feedforward policies over four independent runs and compare their validation CVaR
throughout50training epochs. The resulting trajectories are presented in Figure 2.
A clear difference appears during the initial stages of training. The EVT-based objective exhibits a
faster reduction in mean validation CVaR, while the empirical approach decreases more gradually.
This difference is particularly pronounced during approximately the first15epochs. The dispersion
across independent runs is also considerably smaller for the EVT-based approach during this period,
whereas the empirical-CVaR policies exhibit greater variability in their validation trajectories.
After approximately20epochs, however, the mean validation CVaR of both approaches becomes
similar and remains close throughout the remainder of training. This suggests that the EVT-based
objective induces more stable optimization behavior during the early training stage, which is
consistent with the policies reaching the region of their eventual validation performance with
fewer training iterations.
The experiment therefore indicates that the main difference between the two objectives in
this setting concerns the dynamics and stability of the optimization rather than the validation
risk eventually attained. This behavior is consistent with the statistical motivation of the
proposed estimator: when relatively few observations determine the empirical tail-risk objective,
variation in those observations can propagate directly into the optimization step, whereas the
POT approximation uses the exceedance distribution to estimate the relevant tail quantity.
Once training progresses and both policies reach a similar region of the parameter space, the
difference between their validation performance becomes substantially smaller. This raises a
more demanding question for the proposed methodology: whether the difference between the two
objectives becomes more consequential when policy performance is evaluated specifically in the
extreme tail of the loss distribution.

4.5 Sensitivity to Training Sample Size
The previous results show that the difference between empirical-CVaR and EVT-based opti-
mization becomes more pronounced when the learned policies are exposed to extreme scenarios.
21
This raises a related question: whether this behavior depends on the amount of data available
during training. This is particularly relevant at the catastrophic confidence levelα = 0.999, where
only a small fraction of the simulated losses directly determines the empirical tail-risk objective.
We therefore examine whether reducing the number of training paths materially changes the
out-of-sample behavior of the resulting policies.
We train the empirical-CVaR and EVT-based policies using50,000,100 ,000,200 ,000, and400 ,000
simulated paths while maintaining the remaining training specifications unchanged. Each trained
policy is subsequently evaluated on 100,000 out-of-sample paths from the general distribution
and on the 3,000-path conditional extreme-scenario sample defined in subsection 4.4. Figure 4
reports the resulting distributions of out-of-sample hedging errors.
Under the general distribution, shown in Panel A, the hedging-error distributions remain relatively
stable as the number of training paths changes. The empirical-CVaR and EVT-based policies also
exhibit broadly comparable out-of-sample behavior at each training-sample size. In particular,
reducing the training sample from400,000to50 ,000paths does not produce a clear deterioration
in the central part of either distribution. Thus, within the range of sample sizes considered here,
the general out-of-sample performance appears relatively insensitive to the amount of training
data.
The heavy-tail evaluation in Panel B provides a different perspective. Across all four training-
sample sizes, the EVT-trained policies exhibit hedging-error distributions shifted toward smaller
values relative to their empirical-CVaR counterparts. Importantly, this separation remains visible
when the number of training paths is reduced. At the same time, the distributions do not
exhibit a clear monotonic improvement as the training sample increases from50,000to400 ,000.
The experiment therefore does not support the stronger claim that increasing the number of
training trajectories systematically improves either policy over this range. Rather, it suggests
that the difference observed under the conditional extreme-scenario evaluation persists across the
training-sample sizes considered.

5 Conclusion
This study extends the EVT-based risk-aware reinforcement learning framework considered in
the baseline methodology to policies parameterized by neural networks. The main limitation of
the original approach in this setting arises from its reliance on finite-difference gradients, whose
computational cost increases with the number of policy parameters. We address this limitation
by expressing the POT-CVaR estimator as a differentiable empirical objective and computing its
gradient jointly with respect to the neural-network parameters through automatic differentiation.
The resulting framework preserves the EVT treatment of extreme losses while making it applicable
23
to substantially richer policy parameterizations.
The theoretical results establish the properties supporting this construction. In particular,
for a fixed threshold and under the stated local regularity conditions, the finite-sample POT-
MOM objective is locally differentiable and automatic differentiation recovers its exact gradient.
Moreover, as the number of threshold exceedances increases, the POT-MOM estimator converges
in probability to its corresponding population POT-CVaR functional under the assumptions
established in Section 3.3. These results characterize the differentiability and statistical behavior
of the EVT component used within policy optimization, while keeping distinct the approximation
of the true CVaR and the optimization of the neural-network parameters.
The numerical experiments complement these results from two perspectives. First, the controlled
experiment provides an analytically tractable benchmark and shows that the proposed gradient-
based procedure approaches the known optimal policy parameters. Second, the deep-hedging
experiment illustrates the behavior of the methodology in a substantially more complex neural-
network optimization problem. Under the general out-of-sample market distribution, the EVT
and empirical-CVaR approaches ultimately provide comparable hedging performance, although
the EVT-based objective exhibits a faster initial reduction and lower between-run variability in
validation CVaR during training. The distinction becomes more pronounced under the conditional
extreme-scenario evaluation: the EVT-trained policies exhibit substantially smaller hedging losses,
while performance under the general market distribution remains broadly comparable.
Together, these results suggest that explicitly modeling the loss tail can be particularly useful
when neural-network policies are trained to manage rare but severe outcomes. The benefit is
therefore not that EVT uniformly improves policy performance, but rather that it uses the
exceedance distribution to model the tail in regimes where empirical CVaR relies on relatively
few observations beyond the target quantile. Extending the analysis to a broader assessment
of alternative tail models and risk measures would help determine how generally these benefits
persist.

A Details for the EVT-based training approach
The EVT-based training approach consists of three main steps. First, the threshold above which
the loss distribution is approximated by a GPD is selected using a sequence of Anderson–Darling
goodness-of-fit tests combined with the ForwardStop procedure. Second, conditional on the
selected threshold, the CVaR training objective is estimated using the POT-MOM estimator when
sufficient exceedances are available, with empirical CVaR used as a fallback otherwise. Third, the
resulting objective is differentiated with respect to the neural-network parameters using automatic
differentiation. Threshold selection is performed outside the differentiation step, and the selected
threshold is treated as fixed while the active finite-sample objective is differentiated.
A.1 Threshold selection
Let {Xi}ni=1 denote the sample of losses generated under the current policy. We consider a grid
of candidate percentiles P = {p1, . . . , pK}. For each candidate percentile, the corresponding
threshold is obtained from the empirical distribution, a GPD is fitted to the resulting excesses using
the method of moments, and an Anderson–Darling goodness-of-fit statistic and its corresponding
p-value are computed. Candidate thresholds associated with a shape parameter above the
prescribed cutoff are discarded. The ForwardStop procedure is then applied to the sequence of
