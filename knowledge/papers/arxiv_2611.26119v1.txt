Feasible sub-replicating portfolios for basket options
via the block rearrangement algorithm
Mohamad Hassan Abou Daya∗,
Carole Bernard† and Steven Vanduffel‡
November 20, 2026
Abstract
We construct sub-replicating portfolios of vanilla options for a basket call when the
marginal distributions of the underlying assets are known and their dependence is not. To
do so, we reduce the n-asset problem to n two-asset problems of the type solved by Hobson,
Laurence, and Wang (2005a), and use the Block Rearrangement Algorithm1 (BRA) to
approximate the minimising dependence among the assets. The cash component of the
portfolio is computed exactly, so that its price is a valid lower bound, and reassembling the
BRA output with the original marginals gives an upper bound computed by quadrature;
together they bracket the minimum basket-option price. In log-normal examples with three
and six assets the bracket has a relative width of at most 0.1%, and the corresponding sub-
replicating portfolios are sparse, using only two strikes per asset, while the resulting bounds
are tighter than those obtained from a general-purpose multi-marginal optimal-transport
algorithm with strikes on a grid. We also give the lower bound and an optimal portfolio in
closed form in three special cases: a deep in-the-money basket, a basket with one dominant
asset, and jointly mixable marginals.
Keywords: Basket options, arbitrage-free bounds, model-free dependence, sub-replicating port-
folios, Block Rearrangement Algorithm.
JEL classification: G13, C63.

To date, most progress on the lower bound of basket options has been made via semi-
infinite linear programming. d’Aspremont and El Ghaoui (2006) obtain the lower bound in
closed form when a single option price per asset is known, and a linear-programming lower
bound, not shown to be sharp, when the forward prices are known as well. Pe˜na, Vera,
and Zuluaga (2010) give tractable linear programmes for the lower bound when the basket
contains two assets, or when a forward price and a single call price per asset are known for
any number of assets; Pe˜na, Saynac, Vera, and Zuluaga (2010) and Cho, Kim, and Lee (2016)
compute lower bounds numerically in the general case, by Dantzig–Wolfe decomposition and
by discretising the semi-infinite programme respectively. Neufeld, Papapantoleon, and Xiang
(2023) develop cutting-plane algorithms, in the spirit of the discretisation method of Daum
and Werner (2011) but for a more general class of multi-asset payoffs, to compute bounds on
basket options and other multi-asset derivatives, and report an experiment with 60 assets and
400 traded instruments. All of these methods take as input finitely many option prices rather
than the full marginal distributions, i.e. a continuum of option prices, which is one source of
difficulty in pinning down the lower bound. Another source is that the dependence structure
attaining the lower bound is not known in closed form for more than two assets. When two
assets are present the optimal dependence structure is the lower Fr´echet copula (Dhaene and
Goovaerts 1996); Hobson, Laurence, and Wang (2005a) exploit this structure to construct
sheep-track portfolios (STP) which optimally sub-replicate the basket option in the two-asset
case. Their result is the starting point for the algorithm developed below, which uses STP to
sub-replicate basket options containing any number of assets.
The work closest to ours is the multi-marginal optimal-transport algorithm of Neufeld and
Xiang (2022). They replace each marginal constraint by finitely many moment constraints
on a grid, solve the resulting linear semi-infinite programme, and recover a coupling of the
original marginals by an operation that they call reassembly. Applied to a basket call, this
yields lower and upper bounds on the minimum price, with a gap that vanishes as the grid
is refined, together with a sub-replicating portfolio of vanilla options whose strikes lie on the
grid. Our construction instead selects a small number of strikes per asset from the structure
of the two-asset problem, yielding sub-replicating portfolios that provide lower bounds on the
minimum basket-option price, with closed-form solutions in the special cases of Section 3. To
obtain an upper bound on the minimum price, we apply their reassembly procedure to the
output of the Block Rearrangement Algorithm (BRA), thereby constructing a coupling with
the original marginals (Section 2.3). A key distinction is that the pair decomposition approach
we use identifies suitable strike locations directly and does not rely on a prescribed grid. This
makes it possible to construct much sparser portfolios without sacrificing accuracy. Sections 5.2
and 5.3 compare the two methods on our examples and on marginals taken from their paper.
With two strikes per asset our lower bounds are higher than theirs with 32 to 256 grid strikes
per asset and our upper bounds are considerably tighter. Moreover, adding grid strikes to ours
does not improve our lower bound. This shows that the location of the strikes, and not their
number, is crucial for obtaining tight bounds. Their algorithm, however, handles general cost
functions and many more assets than our construction does. The two approaches are therefore

complementary.
In a different direction, optimal-transport methods have been used to derive bounds on
multi-asset derivatives. Bartl, Kupper, Lux, Papapantoleon, and Eckstein (2022) formulate
the dual transport problem when additional information on the dependence is available, and
Ansari, L¨utkebohmert, Neufeld, and Sester (2024) extract such information from the market
prices of traded multi-asset options. Others have used neural networks and stochastic-gradient-
descent algorithms to compute bounds of multi-asset derivatives in the presence of additional
information (De Gennaro Aquino and Bernard 2020, Eckstein, Kupper, and Pohl 2020, Eck-
stein, Guo, Lim, and Ob l´oj 2021, Dragazi, Liu, and Papapantoleon 2024). In general, bounds
on multi-asset derivatives are tighter when additional information on the dependence structure
is present.
We make four main contributions. First, we use the BRA to approximate the lower bound of
the basket call option price and present a simple algorithm to construct feasible sub-replicating
portfolios for basket options. By exploiting a decomposition into two-asset problems, our
method selects the strikes in a structured manner (rather than from a predetermined grid),
yielding sparse portfolios. Second, we show that the cash component of the sub-replicating
portfolio can be computed exactly by exploiting the piecewise-linear structure of the payoff
function, so that no Monte-Carlo approximation of the infimum is needed; this exact com-
putation also turns the framework from a feasibility argument into a verifiable optimality
argument, in the sense that whenever the expected cost of the hedging portfolio coincides with
the basket value under the BRA dependence structure, weak duality implies that both the
dependence structure and the portfolio are optimal for the marginals used in the computation
(Corollary 2.6). Third, we remove the restriction to the discretised marginals on which the
rearrangement algorithms operate: the price of the portfolio under the original marginals and
the basket value of the BRA output reassembled with the original marginals, in the sense of
Neufeld and Xiang (2022), bracket the true lower bound (Corollary 2.12), and in our examples
the bracket has a relative width of at most 0.1%. Fourth, we provide closed-form expressions
for the lower bound, together with optimal sub-replicating portfolios of vanilla options, in three
special cases; the case of one dominant asset is characterised by a verifiable condition on the
marginals alone. Our results contribute to the open question of characterising lower bounds of
basket option prices in arbitrary dimensions.
The key idea behind our method is to exploit the two-asset sub-replication results of Hob-
son, Laurence, and Wang (2005a) to construct feasible portfolios for baskets with any number
of assets. For a basket of n assets, each asset Xi is paired with the aggregate Yi = P j̸=i Xj
of the remaining assets, reducing the problem to n instances of the two-asset sub-replication
problem solved by Hobson, Laurence, and Wang (2005a). From each two-asset pair we extract
strikes and construct the corresponding piecewise-linear payoff functions fi(xi), and a cash
component λ is added to enforce pointwise feasibility. While the pair decomposition applies
in principle to any dimension, two practical difficulties arise. First, in dimension two the de-
pendence structure minimising the sum in convex order, and hence every basket call price,
is the lower Fr´echet copula (the antimonotone dependence structure), but in dimension three
and above no such closed-form structure exists (Bernard, Jiang, and Wang 2014); we therefore
rely on the BRA to approximate it numerically. Second, computational cost and accuracy are
sensitive to the number of assets, as we discuss in Section 5.

the feasible solution. Section 3 presents the optimal sub-replicating portfolio in three special

2 Problem formulation and feasible solution
Let (Ω, F , Q) be a probability space supporting non-negative random variables X1, . . . , Xn
representing the time-T prices of n underlying assets, and denote by CXi (k) := EQ[(Xi − k)+]
the call price function on Xi at strike k ⩾ 0. Let K be the basket call strike. Throughout
the paper we work under a risk-neutral measure Q, assume zero interest rates, no dividends,
and unit weights in the basket; the extension to non-unit weights and constant interest rates
follows by a trivial scaling argument (Laurence and Wang 2005). We use the same maturity
for all options and suppress it from the notation.
We make the following standing assumption.
Assumption 2.1. For each i = 1, . . . , n, the marginal law of Xi under Q admits a density on
(0, ∞) with full support and finite mean. The call price function CXi (k) is therefore strictly
positive for every k ⩾ 0, strictly decreasing, strictly convex on [0, ∞), and continuously dif-
ferentiable in k, with derivative ∂kCXi (k) = −Q(Xi > k) = −Q(Xi ⩾ k) (the two coincide
because {Xi = k} has measure zero under the density).

of marginals under the BRA-approximated dependence structure ˆµ. By construction ˆµ is

2.2 Algorithm implementation
The most important step in constructing the feasible portfolio is to identify the depen-
dence structure of the marginals that minimises the basket option price. We use the BRA
(BRA hereafter; Bernard and McLeish 2016, Boudt, Jakobsons, and Vanduffel 2018), a block-
wise extension of the rearrangement algorithm of Puccetti and R¨uschendorf (2012). Bernard,
Bondarenko, and Vanduffel (2018) provide numerical evidence that, among the dependence
structures compatible with given marginals and a given distribution of the sum, the one re-
turned by BRA has almost maximum entropy; in this sense BRA returns a typical dependence
structure at the lower bound of the sum, not an exceptional one. More concretely, BRA seeks
the dependence structure for which the sum of the marginals has the lowest variance, that is,
the joint law under which the sum is as close to constant as the marginals allow. Minimising
the variance is a proxy: the quantity of interest is E[(P i Xi − K)+], and for n ⩾ 3 the two
objectives need not be minimised by the same dependence structure, since the sum has no
convex-order minimum in general (Bernard, Jiang, and Wang 2014). This is why we also run
the algorithms with the K-aware objective of Remark 2.9 and select their output by the hedging
value HV . We refer to Bernard, Bondarenko, and Vanduffel (2018) for a detailed exposition;
the improved variant of Bernard, Chen, R¨uschendorf, and Vanduffel (2026), which selects the
block sizes dynamically, can be substituted without changing the construction below.

at least 50,000 atoms. Accurate strikes are essential for the cash component to be accurate;
since the lower bound of the basket option is typically small, any inaccuracy in the strikes
propagates directly to the portfolio value.
Second, run BRA at least ten times, since it occasionally converges to a poor local minimum,
and let each run converge: with random block partitions, a stall rule of a few iterations
without improvement stops BRA long before convergence, and we use a window W of a few
hundred iterations. Importantly, we recommend selecting the BRA output that maximises
HV = Pn i=1 E[fi(Xi)] + λ rather than the output with the lowest variance of the sum. Two
BRA outputs with similar variance can produce very different strike configurations, and the
configuration with slightly higher variance may yield a tighter sub-replicating portfolio. Since λ
is computed exactly via Proposition 2.3, evaluating HV for each BRA output incurs negligible
additional cost.
Third, the Rearrangement Algorithm (RA) of Puccetti and R¨uschendorf (2012) can com-
plement BRA: running both algorithms with multiple random initialisations and selecting the
output with the highest HV across all runs further tightens the sub-replication gap.
Fourth, once candidate strikes {K j i } have been identified from the zero-crossings of ϕi, the
hedging portfolio value HV viewed as a function of the strike values (with the number of strikes
per asset and their sign pattern fixed) is continuous and piecewise-linear: the cash component
λ is the minimum of a piecewise-linear function of (x, {K j i }) over a finite vertex grid, and each
E[fi(Xi)] is a linear combination of expected call payoffs that are themselves piecewise-linear in
their strike on the discretised marginal. Consequently, a derivative-free local search initialised
at the zero-crossings of the ϕi closes two sources of discrepancy: the finite-grid discretisation of
the zero-crossing detection, and the misalignment between the objective used inside BRA and
the actual HV surface. We alternate the simplex method of Nelder and Mead (1965) with the
direction-set method of Powell (1964) until neither improves HV . On this surface the simplex
method alone stalls at kinks once there are more than a handful of free strikes (in the six-asset
example of Section 5 it stops up to 3 × 10−5 short of the value reached by the alternation), and
the line searches of Powell’s method move along the kinks that stop it. The search may still
end at a kink that is not a local maximum; this is harmless, because every strike configuration
yields a valid sub-replicator and HV never decreases along the search. The refinement therefore
cannot overstate the bound: each candidate strike set yields a pointwise sub-replicator whose
price is a valid lower bound for the given marginals, and the search merely selects the largest
of these valid lower bounds. Note that HV depends on the dependence structure only through
the starting point of the search, since both E[fi(Xi)] and λ are determined by the strikes and
the marginals; the dependence structure enters through BV , the target of the certificate. In
our numerical experiments this strike-refinement step typically closes the sub-replication gap
to the reported precision for the dependence structure returned by BRA; see Section 5.
Remark 2.9 (Alternative objectives inside RA/BRA). Although we use the variance of the
row-sum as the default objective for RA and BRA (as in Bernard, Bondarenko, and Vanduffel
(2018)), each rearrangement step replaces the coupling of two block sums U and V by their
antimonotone coupling, which minimises E[φ(U + V )] over all couplings of U and V for every
convex φ (Tchen 1980, R¨uschendorf 1983). Each step is therefore non-increasing in E[φ(P i Xi)]
for any convex φ, whatever objective is used to monitor convergence. In particular, taking
φ(z) = (z − K)+ yields a K-aware objective that directly targets the basket value BV at
the strike of interest. Running BRA with both the variance proxy and the K-aware objective
and retaining the run that maximises HV adds diversity to the set of candidate dependence

and the refined portfolio is retained only if it is worth more than both. The comparison has to
be made after the refinement and not before: at deep out-of-the-money strikes the value of the
bound is of the order of the grid error of the zero-crossings, so that the unrefined strikes can
be worth less than the strike-free portfolios although the refined ones attain the basket value
(this is the case at K = 115 and K = 120 in the three-asset example of Section 5). The value
returned by Algorithm 1 is therefore never below (mX − K)+.

4 On the optimality of the construction
The construction of Section 2 is unconditionally feasible (Theorem 2.4), and Corollary 2.6
provides a verifiable sufficient condition for optimality whenever the hedging-portfolio value
coincides with the basket value under the BRA dependence structure, for the marginals used
in the computation. The three special regimes of Section 3 give the value of the lower bound in
closed form. A natural follow-up question is whether this coincidence, and hence the optimality
18
of the construction, can be guaranteed a priori for general marginals in dimension n ⩾ 3. The
short answer is negative, and two distinct obstructions explain why.
The first obstruction concerns the origin of the strikes. The dual of (1) over integrable
functions of the individual assets has, by the theorem of Kellerer (1984), value equal to the
lower bound; the portfolio of Theorem 2.4 belongs to the subclass of static positions in vanilla
calls on X1, . . . , Xn together with cash. Its strikes, however, are inherited from n separate
two-asset problems: for each pair (Xi, Yi) the Hobson–Laurence–Wang triplet (λi, νi, νyi ) is
optimal in a market in which calls on the aggregate Yi are traded, and the construction keeps
the Xi-leg νi of each triplet, while the role of the discarded Yi-legs is taken over by the other
fj and by the single constant λ. Nothing guarantees that strikes obtained in this way, even
after the strike-refinement step of Section 2.2, coincide with a dual optimiser of the n-asset
problem: the dual optimum may require a different number of strikes per asset, or may not
be attained by finitely many strikes at all. In dimension two this issue does not arise, because
the pair decomposition is the problem itself.
The second obstruction is that, even setting the first aside, the worst-case dependence
structure µ∗ attaining inf µ∈Π Eµ[(P i Xi − K)+] has no closed-form description in dimension
three or above (Section 2.2), and characterising it remains an open problem in dependence
modelling. BRA provides a numerical approximation but, as a heuristic, comes with no a
priori guarantee of converging to a global minimiser; multiple restarts, the K-aware objective
of Remark 2.9 and the HV -maximising selection rule of Section 2.2 mitigate this in practice,
yet cannot deliver a theoretical guarantee.
The three special regimes of Section 3 are precisely those in which one or both obstructions
disappear. In Case 1 (Ai = ∅ for some i) the lower bound is attained by a dependence
structure under which the basket payoff is linear, so the linear portfolio x1 + x2 + x3 − K is
dual optimal and BRA uncertainty plays no role. In Case 2 (one dominant asset) the basket
along the co-monotone/antimonotone coupling µc crosses the strike once, from above; the
worst-case dependence structure is then µc itself, and the vanilla portfolio (12), which has the
pair-decomposition form with a single strike on each dominated asset and a single call on the
dominant one, is dual optimal, so both obstructions disappear. In Case 3 (joint mixability) the
basket collapses to a deterministic constant, trivialising the lower bound. Outside these three
regimes neither obstruction is known to vanish, and we are not aware of a general analytical
optimality result for n ⩾ 3 baskets in the literature.
The two obstructions are theoretical and, to the best of our knowledge, cannot be removed
without solving the open problem of characterising basket-option lower bounds in dimensions
⩾ 3. Corollary 2.6 provides a complementary form of progress by reducing the question
of optimality to a single numerical comparison between the hedging-portfolio value and the
basket value under the BRA dependence structure, with both quantities computed exactly
(free of Monte-Carlo noise) once Proposition 2.3 is invoked, for the discretised marginals used
in the computation. Whenever this equality is observed, as it is to the reported precision at
every strike of the three-asset example of Section 5, weak duality shows that at that strike
the pair-decomposition strikes are dual optimal and the BRA output is primal optimal for
the discretised problem, even though such an outcome cannot be anticipated analytically.
Corollary 2.12 extends this to the original marginals and dispenses with the equality: the
difference between BV ex and HV ex bounds the sub-optimality of the portfolio and of the
reassembled dependence structure, whatever its origin. The contribution of the construction
is therefore best described as verifiably optimal rather than provably optimal: it returns a
19
feasible sub-replicator, a dependence structure of the original marginals, and a certified bound
on the distance of each from the optimum, which is at most 0.1% of the value of the bound in
the examples of Section 5.
