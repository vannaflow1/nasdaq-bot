Abstract
We develop an arithmetic multi-factor stochastic volatility model for commodity futures and
options. The arithmetic specification yields a low-dimensional Markovian representation of the
futures curve and semi-closed-form pricing formulas for vanilla and basket options, including
calendar spread options. These properties make the model suitable for large-scale empirical
estimation.
We estimate the model using an unscented Kalman filter on a panel of WTI crude oil futures,
vanilla options, and calendar spread options spanning 2013–2024. We find that three stochastic
volatility factors are needed to capture both the level and slope of the volatility term structure,
and that hedges based on these factors remove almost all of the daily risk of a portfolio of
vanilla options. Calendar spread options provide information about the dependence structure
of futures prices that is not captured by futures and vanilla options alone. A model estimated
without calendar spread options systematically overprices them by overstating the decorrelation
between adjacent futures contracts. Including these options in the estimation brings the model’s
decorrelation close to the observed decorrelation, which removes the overpricing and improves
their hedges, at only a small loss of accuracy in the pricing and hedging of vanilla options.
Finally, we show that the arithmetic model fits futures and vanilla options better than its
geometric counterpart and hedges the vanilla options slightly better.

model the term structure of commodity futures prices. The HJM framework’s ability to incorporate
a wide range of volatility structures, such as stochastic volatility, seasonality and jumps, while
maintaining consistency with no-arbitrage conditions makes the approach suitable for modeling
commodity futures markets, such as oil, where prices exhibit both short-term volatility and long-
term trends; see Trolle and Schwartz (2009) and Chiarella et al. (2013) for extensive empirical

In this paper we will instead focus on so-calledarithmeticmodels, i.e., models without explicit
level dependence in the volatility, the Bachelier model being the simplest example. Arithmetic
models and their advantages are well known, and have been studied in many constructions before,
but mostly in the context of modeling electricity spot prices and futures. See for example Lucia and
Schwartz (2002), Benth et al. (2019), Latini et al. (2019), Piccirilli et al. (2021), Piccirilli (2018) and
Benth et al. (2015). Instead, our paper focuses on building an arithmetic model for the WTI futures
market. We also take a simpler approach than most of the electricity models listed above which are
heavily focused on modeling seasonality and jumps in prices. Jumps in WTI futures prices appear
to be relatively rare and, when present, largely confined to the very short end of the curve, with
a limited contribution to overall variance, see ˇZikeˇ s and Barun ´ ık (2016). Similarly, seasonality in
crude oil is generally found to be weak (see, e.g., Borovkova and Geman, 2006). This motivates the
use of a simpler driving process, and we construct a model driven by a continuous affine process
with stochastic volatility in the sense of Duffie et al. (2003), on the state spaceR k+ ×R n. Here,kis
the number of stochastic volatility factors andnis the number of factors driving the futures curve.

geometric approach admits semi-closed-form pricing for vanilla options, pricing options involving
multiple futures contracts generally requires approximations or more computationally demanding
numerical methods. On the other hand, arithmetic models produce semi-closed-form solutions for a

wide variety of options.
Beyond the practical advantages of arithmetic models, there are also some desirable empirical
advantages. Firstly, it is well documented (Bouchouev, 2023) that the Bachelier volatility smile
implied from options written on WTI futures is roughly symmetric. This implies a symmetric distri-
bution of the futures pricelevel. This feature is produced by arithmetic models in a straightforward
manner, but is significantly more complicated to do in a geometric model where symmetry in the
distribution of thelog-priceis the natural starting point. Furthermore, arithmetic models naturally
produce negative prices of futures. This was previously seen as a deficiency, but since the negative
price event observed in front-month WTI futures on 20 April 2020, this is now a realistic, but also
necessary, feature to price options with negative strikes. 1
The existence of semi-closed-form solutions for a wider class of options allows for a tractable
inclusion of calendar spread options in addition to vanilla options and futures in the data panel. A
calendar spread option allows the purchaser the option to roll the position of an existing futures
position at a predetermined price, and thus is often referred to as avirtual storageoption. In
particular, calendar spread options on WTI futures have seen a substantial increase in liquidity in
the last 15 years, due to the increasingly storage-driven nature of the WTI futures market. We
refer to Bouchouev (2023), chapter 13, for an overview of the increasing importance and underlying
economics of calendar spread options.
While there exists a considerable literature on pricing and hedging spread options, in particular
calendar spreads (see for example Schneider and Tavin, 2018, for an overview), the empirical liter-
ature on calendar spread options is more modestly sized. Schneider and Tavin (2018) focus on the
fact that calendar spread options provide additional information about the joint dynamics of futures
contracts at different maturities and, in particular, their dependence structure. They demonstrate
the importance of this information by jointly calibrating a multi-factor stochastic volatility model
for crude oil futures to vanilla and calendar spread options, using the latter to investigate the term
structure of dependence and the so-called Samuelson correlation effect. Their empirical analysis is
based on calibration to three individual trading days. More recently, Frau et al. (2025) develop a
general geometric framework for pricing calendar spread options in energy markets under stochastic
volatility and jumps. Their focus, however, is on calibration, which they do for a single day of data.
Other articles with an empirical focus include Seok et al. (2018) and Schmitz et al. (2013), both
considering option pricing of calendar spread options on agricultural grain commodities.
Our empirical analysis takes a different approach than what has been done in the existing liter-
ature. Since the arithmetic modeling framework provides a semi-closed-form solution for calendar
spread options, we incorporate these prices directly into the estimation alongside futures and vanilla
options.
We estimate the proposed model jointly under the risk-neutral and real-world measures using the
unscented Kalman filter in conjunction with the prediction error decomposition on an extensive data
panel on WTI futures spanning January 2013 to February 2024, with a total of 36,530 futures prices,
235,277 vanilla option prices and 70,830 calendar spread option prices observed over 2,810 business
days. The options are selected to ensure a representative sample of the daily observed volatility
surface. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale empirical investigation to include time-series
of calendar spread option prices in the dynamic estimation of an arbitrage-free commodity futures
term structure model. Furthermore, it is the first large-scale empirical study of the performance of
arithmetic term structure models for crude oil markets.
Our results show that three stochastic volatility factors are needed to reproduce both the level
and slope of the volatility term structure. We also show that calendar spread options carry informa-

We also investigate the model’s hedging performance. Hedges built on the three factors remove
almost all of the daily risk of a portfolio of vanilla options, but provide only incremental improvements
when hedging calendar spread options. Finally, we compare the pricing and hedging performance of
the arithmetic model with that of its geometric counterpart. The arithmetic model provides both a
better fit to futures and vanilla option prices and modestly improved hedging performance.
The paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, the model framework is presented and a general
option pricing formula is derived. In Section 3, we present the data and the estimation method.
Section 4 presents the estimates and the pricing and hedging results. Finally, Section 5 concludes.

3.1.4 Option-implied volatility , skewness and decorrelation
We summarize the option prices by three option-implied quantities: the model-free volatilityM2 and
skewnessM 3 of the vanilla options, and the implied decorrelation of the calendar spread options.
For each contract and date, we computeM 2 andM 3 from the full cross-section of out-of-the-money
vanilla options. The spanning formula requires option prices at every strike, but options are listed
only at discrete strikes and stop short of the wings. We therefore fit a smooth risk-neutral density to
each day’s quotes and compute the moments from it. Beyond±2 at-the-money standard deviations,
we replace the density on each side by an exponential tail with the same mass and first moment, so
thatM 2 andM 3 are moments of the whole distribution. 13

in the volatility. The skewness is positive on most dates and increases with maturity, although its
sign at the short end varies across regimes, being predominantly negative during the 2020 plunge
