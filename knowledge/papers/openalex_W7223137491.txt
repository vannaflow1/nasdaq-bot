Multi-dimensional risk profiling of BIST-100 stocks: A comparative analysis of 
the MEREC-AROMAN MCDM framework and the classical CAPM beta 

Ali Osman Öztop Asst. Prof., Muğla Sıtkı Koçman University, aliosmanoztop@mu.edu.tr, 0000-0002-7568-5927 

Corresponding author/Sorumlu yazar: Ali Osman Öztop 
Abstract 
Our study creates a multi-dimensional risk profile for Borsa Istanbul 100 (BIST-100) stocks combining MEREC and AROMAN from multi-criteria 
decision-making (MCDM) techniques. For this purpose, 7 risk dimensions were calculated using daily price and volume data of 91 BIST -100 
stocks for the period between January 1, 2015 - April 30, 2026: market beta, downside beta, VaR, CVaR, max drawdown, lower -tail 
dependence, Amihud illiquidity. In the results obtained, the MEREC -AROMAN ranking was compared with the classical CAPM beta ranking. 
Accordingly, although the two rankings show a moderate Spearman correlation (+0. 28), there is a divergence stemming theoretically from 
liquidity and tail-risk channels. Furthermore, in out -of-sample tests conducted during the post -2023 monetary policy normalization period, 
the MEREC-AROMAN ranking predicts realized volatility (Mann-Whitney p = 0.033; Holm-adjusted p = 0.065), whereas the beta ranking works 
in the opposite direction. In robustness tests, the robustness of findings was confirmed with sectoral fixed effects regressi on, behavioral 
regime analysis based on the Risk Appetite Index (RISE) published by the Central Securities Depository, and trapezoidal fuzzy AROMAN 
extension. Our study presents an important methodological contribution to advanced MCDM in finance by revealing that liquidit y-adjusted 
multi-dimensional risk measures are superior to single-factor beta in regime-change environments.

Introduction 
Accurate measurement of stock risk remains a central concern in asset pricing. Since Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965), the Capital 
Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) has provided a parsimonious single-factor representation of systematic risk through the market beta 
coefficient. Accumulating empirical evidence, however, has documented anomalies that the single-factor beta cannot account for: 
the size effect (Banz, 1981), value premia (Fama & French, 1992), momentum (Carhart, 1997), liquidity risk (Acharya & Pederse n, 
2005; Amihud, 2002; Pástor & Stambaugh, 2003), and downside-conditional risk (Ang et al., 2006; Estrada, 2002). Taken together, 
the anomaly evidence points to risk that is multi -dimensional; no single statistic seems sufficient to summarize cross -sectional 
differences in stock returns. 
Beta nevertheless remains the workhorse risk measure across many emerging markets, the Turkish market among them, and this 
reliance is troubling on two counts. Liquidity heterogeneity in emerging markets is far wider than in developed ones, so igno ring 
liquidity risk carries unusually severe consequences (Brunnermeier & Pedersen, 2009). In addition, the monetary- policy breaks

Türkiye has gone through since 2018 render the beta coefficient non -stationary and erode its reliability as a stable risk measure. 
To address these limitations, this study proposes a multi -dimensional risk profiling framework for the Borsa Istanbul 100 (BIST -

Criterion weights are obtained from the Method based on the Removal Effects of Criteria (MEREC) of Keshavarz- Ghorabaee et al. 
(2021); the ranking step then relies on the Alternative Ranking Order Method Accounting for Two -Step Normalization (AROMAN) 
of Bošković et al. (2023). 
The analysis is organized around three questions: 
(i) How far does the MEREC-AROMAN composite ranking depart from the classical beta ordering? 
(ii) Do the MCDM weights shift with monetary-policy and investor-sentiment regimes? 
(iii) Is the MEREC-AROMAN ranking more accurate out of sample than its beta counterpart? 
Four contributions follow. To our knowledge this is the first application of the MEREC -AROMAN combination to BIST -100 stocks. 
Empirically, liquidity emerges as the principal driver of cross -sectional risk dispersion in the index. On the theoretical side, 
downside-conditional risk gains discriminating power once sentiment turns pessimistic, in line with the loss-aversion argument of 
Kahneman and Tversky (1979), a result obtained thro ugh behavioral regime analysis built on the MKK Risk Appetite Index (RISE). 
In practical terms, the MEREC -AROMAN ranking delivers an out -of-sample prediction of realized volatility over the post -2023 
normalization period that remains significant at the 10 percent level after multiple-testing correction, a test the beta ranking fails. 
The article proceeds with the theoretical background and related literature in Section 1. Section 2 details the data and 
methodology. Section 3 presents the empirical findings. Section 4 discusses the implications, and the final section concludes . 
1. Theoretical Background and Literature Review 
This section reviews the literature underpinning the seven-criterion framework adopted in the present study, organized into four 
thematic strands. 
1.1. The CAPM and Its Multi-Factor Extensions 
The CAPM of Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965) ties the expected excess return on an asset to the covariance of that asset with the 
market portfolio, scaled by the variance of the market. This proportionality coefficient is the asset's beta coefficient. Even allowing 
for the zero-beta extension of Black (1972), empirical tests have rejected the CAPM repeatedly. Fama and French (1992) state that 
the relationship between β and average return is " absorbed by the combination of size and book -to-market equity." Beta alone, 
in other words, cannot carry the cross-section, which led Fama and French (1993) to their three-factor model. Carhart (1997) then 
added momentum as a fourth factor, capturing a further slice of cross-sectional return variation. While these extensions improve 
explanatory power, they remain within the linear factor framework of the CAPM. 
A separate strand asks what happens where linear factor structures fall short. Estrada (2002) introduces the downside beta and 
the D-CAPM on the grounds that variance and beta presume a symmetry that equity returns rarely display. Ang et al. (2006) put 
the annual premium on high-downside-risk stocks at roughly 6%. Bali et al. (2009) and Atilgan et al. (2020) further show that left -
tail measures such as Value -at-Risk and expected shortfall carry significant predictive content for the cross -section of expected 
returns. 
1.2. Liquidity and Tail-Risk Considerations 
Liquidity entered the pricing framework through a different door. Amihud (2002) finds that illiquidity, the daily ratio of absolute 
return to dollar volume, earns a positive return premium, strongest among small firms. Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) build a 
market-wide liquidity factor whose sensi tivity turns out to be priced in the cross -section. Acharya and Pedersen (2005) tie a 
security's required return, within a liquidity-adjusted CAPM, both to its expected liquidity and to liquidity-related covariances with 
the market. Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) supply the theory behind a familiar crisis pattern: market liquidity and funding 
liquidity are determined jointly and can spiral downward together. 
Co-movement in the tails is a further dimension the linear framework misses. Longin and Solnik (2001) show that equity 
correlations across countries rise sharply in extreme downturns, eroding the diversification gains promised under multivariat e 
normality. Joe (1997) gives lower -tail dependence its formal copula treatment: the conditional probability that one series sits in 
its lower tail given that the other one does. 
1.3. Behavioral Finance and Sentiment 
Behavioral finance adds a preference -based reason to care about the downside. Losses weigh more heavily than gains of equal 
size, a property formalized by Kahneman and Tversky (1979) in prospect theory; loss aversion of this kind implies compensatio n

aggregate sentiment to be anything but uniform: stocks that are hard to value or to arbitrage earn lower returns after high-

In the Turkish context, investor sentiment is captured directly by an institutional measure: Türkiye's Central Securities Depository

1.4. MCDM Methods in Finance and Emerging-Market Applications 
MCDM offers a more comprehensive perspective compared to traditional univariate approaches by integrating criteria such as 
financial ratios, risk metrics and market liquidity (Kılıçarslan, 2025; Shah et al., 2024). Zopounidis and Doumpos (2002) pro vide an 
early review of MCDM applications in financial decision-making, and Kou et al. (2014) extend MCDM to clustering-based financial 
risk analysis. Recent advances in objective weighting methods include MEREC (Keshavarz -Ghorabaee et al., 2021) and the 
Logarithmic Percentage Change -Driven Objective Weighting (LOPCOW) method (Ecer & Pamucar, 2022). For the ranking step, 
recent contributions include the Measurement of Alternatives and Ranking according to Compromise Solution (MARCOS) method 
(Stević et al., 2020), the Ranking of Alternatives through Functional mapping of criterion sub-intervals into a Single Interval (RAFSI) 
method (Žižović et al., 2020), and AROMAN (Bošković et al., 2023). 
Hybrid models integrating soft computing techniques improve risk profiling in emerging markets such as Türkiye (Narang et al., 
2023). Goutte et al. (2025) reported higher risk -adjusted returns in Vietnam's VN100 index when compared with MCDM -based 
portfolios. Gupta et al. (2023), on the other hand, found that TOPSIS -based selection integrating financial beta with India's NSE -
100 index revealed favorable risk -return profiles. Yildiz et al. (2022) revealed that Borsa Istanbul exhibits a distinct downside risk 
sensitivity that cannot be captured by classical beta, as one of the emerging markets with the highest global downside beta 
(approximately 1.74). Comparative evidence also extends beyond Asia: Basilio et al. (2018) applied PROMETHEE -II rankings to 
portfolio formation on the Brazilian exchange (B3), and Poklepović and Babić (2014) selected stocks listed in Zagreb through a 
hybrid consensus of five MCDM methods, extending MCDM -based stock evaluation to Latin American and Eastern European 
capital markets. Although the MEREC -AROMAN combination has been applied to sustainable competitiveness assessment for 
Türkiye (Kara et al., 2024), to our knowledge, no previous study has applied it to risk profiling of stocks on the BIST -100. This gap 
is addressed in the present study. 
The choice of methods rests on three grounds. First, MEREC weights are fully data -driven, derived from each criterion's removal 
effect on overall performance scores; no subjective expert input is required in a 91 × 7 matrix, and the logarithmic aggregation is 
relatively robust to extreme values. Second, AROMAN's two -step normalization blends min -max and vector normalization, 
mitigating the scale sensitivity of single -normalization methods, and its few free parameters (β, λ) permit ful l sensitivity analysis 
(Section 3.6). Third, both are post-2018 methods whose combination has a Turkish precedent (Kara et al., 2024); the present study 
applies it to stock-level risk profiling. The weighting choice is further tested against LOPCOW, Entropy, and CRITIC in Section 3.6. 
2. Data and Methodology 
2.1. Data Sources and Sample 
Daily data for BIST -100 firms over January 1, 2015– April 30, 2026 were compiled from the Yahoo Finance online database 
(http://finance.yahoo.com/). The initial stock pool obtained from the union of two BIST -100 component lists during this period 
consists of 118 stocks. After applying the 750 trading day minimum requirement, the full panel contains 97 stocks. For replicability, 
the sample is constructed in four steps : (i) an initial pool of 118 stocks, the union of two independent BIST -100 constituent lists 
for 2024–2026; (ii) exclusion of 21 stocks (delisted issuers and short -history IPOs) with fewer than 750 trading days over January 
2015–April 2026, a threshold ensuring sufficient observations for 5-percent VaR/CVaR and 10-percent lower-tail dependence; (iii) 
a full panel of the remaining 97 stocks; (iv) 91 stocks with sufficient in-sample history (2015–2022) entering the main analysis, the 
remaining six (recent IPOs) entering only the out -of-sample validation. Additionally, sectoral classification information for each 
stock was obtained from company information records on the Public Disclosure Platform (PDP) (https://kap.org.tr). The Risk 
Appetite Index (RISE) wa s obtained from the Data Analysis Platform affiliated with the Central Securities Depository of Türkiye 
(https://www.vap.org.tr/reks). 
The sample is divided into two as the in -sample period (2015– 2022) and the out -of-sample validation period (2023– 2026); this 
division was made in accordance with the structural break in Türkiye’s monetary policy in mid-2023. Descriptive statistics of seven 
risk criteria throughout the in-sample period are presented in Table 1.

2.5. Out-of-Sample Validation Design 
An out-of-sample validation procedure was applied from in- sample (2015–2022) data to test whether it has predictive power for 
realized risk outcomes in the validation period (June 2023 – April 2026). Two realized risk outcomes, namely the realized maximum 
drawdown and the realized annualized volatility, were calculated for each stock throughout the validation period. Three 
complementary tests serve this purpose. A direction-aware Mann–Whitney U test (Mann & Whitney, 1947) compares fourth- and 
first-quartile outcomes under a one -sided alternative. Next, the Spe arman rank correlation (Spearman, 1904) was computed to 
link the in-sample ranking to the realized outcome. Finally, the Diebold and Mariano (1995) test, together with the Harvey et al. 
(1997) correction, was then applied to the ranking-based linear predictions of realized volatility. The same test sequence was also 
applied to the beta ranking for comparison purposes. For each ranking, the two realized-outcome tests (maximum drawdown and 
volatility) form one test family, and Mann–Whitney p-values are reported with the Holm (1979) correction. 
For the fuzzy AROMAN robustness extension (Section 3.6), the stationary block bootstrap of Politis and Romano (1994) is used 
wherever bootstrap-based dispersion is required, keeping serial dependence intact. Augmented Dickey-Fuller tests reject the unit-

2.6. Regime Boundaries: Identification and Statistical Validation 
Sub-period boundaries are chosen on well-documented Turkish monetary policy events, namely the August 2018 currency crisis 
(P1→P2; CBRT, 2018) and the June 2023 return to orthodox monetary policy with the 8.5%→50% rate -tightening cycle (P2→P3; 
CBRT, 2023), and validated by formal structural-break testing. The Bai and Perron (2003) methodology is operationalized with the 
Pruned Exact Linear Time (PELT) algorithm of Killick et al. (2012) applied to BIST -100 and USD/TRY 30-day realized volatilities and 
the log USD/TRY level, using two complementary cost functions: a least -squares (L₂) cost detecting mean -level shifts and a radial 
basis function (RBF) kernel cost detecting broader distributional change. The two cost functions place the USD/TRY level 
breakpoint at 9 July 2018 (L₂) and 21 May 2018 (RBF), within 23 and 72 days of the hypothesized P1→P2 boundary, respectively; 
the closest detected break to the P2→P3 boundary is 4 December 2023 (BIST -100 volatility, L₂), 186 days after the policy event. 
The convergence of two distinct detection criteria on dates close to the policy events indicates that the detected break dates are 
statistically proximate to, though not exactly coincident with, the policy events; the policy-event boundaries are therefore treated 
as externally imposed rather than data-derived.

4. Discussion 
Our study revealed the multi-dimensional risk profile of BIST -100 stocks through the MEREC -AROMAN MCDM framework, and 
compared it with the classical CAPM beta model. First, the dominance of Amihud illiquidity in MEREC weighting confirms the 
central role of liquidity risk in BIST-100. This finding is consistent with the liquidity pricing literature of Acharya and Pedersen 
(2005) and Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) and with the BIST-specific cross-sectional liquidity premium documented by Atilgan 
et al. (2016) for the 1999–2012 period. Therefore, risk metrics not enhanced with liquidity cannot adequately explain risk profiling. 
Second, when (i) the regime dependence of MEREC weights (see Table 2) and (ii) the direction change of AROMAN-beta correlation 
(see Table 5) are evaluated together, single -factor beta is not stationary across monetary policy regimes. Beta suffers significant 
information-content losses during structural -break periods. However, the negative P3 correlation (ρ = −0.305) reveals that the 
multi-dimensional composite index maintains its discriminatory power. This result is consistent with broader evidence on 
conditional and time -varying beta in asset pricing (Lewellen & Nagel, 2006). Third, the out -of-sample prediction superiority of 
AROMAN ranking for realized volatility (Mann -Whitney p = 0.033; Holm -adjusted p = 0.065) represents a methodological 
contribution to the MCDM method. Unlike most MCDM -finance studies, the ranking has been subjected to such validation. In 
contrast, the classical beta ranking exhibits a strong inverse relationship during the same validation period. Fourth, the behavioral 
regime analysis using the RISE shows that downside conditional risk measures become more discriminatory during pessimistic 
regimes. This finding shows parallelism with Kahneman and Tversky (1979) and Baker and Wurgler (2006). The 5.1 percentage 
point increase in Downside Beta weight during the Risk Aversion regime is consistent with the cross-sectional pricing of downside 
conditional risk documented by Ang et al. (2006). The sectoral control analysis addresses the potential concern that the favorable 
AROMAN ranking of banks may reflect sector -specific factors. OLS results show that sectoral identity does not contribute 
additional explanatory power beyond beta and liquidity, and that the Amihud coefficient is approximately 1.6 times larger than 
the beta coefficient. The multi-dimensional ranking functions through observable liquidity rather than sectoral preferences. 
Conclusion 
In this study, a multi -dimensional risk profile was created for stocks in the Borsa Istanbul 100 index by combining seven 
theoretically grounded risk criteria through MEREC and AROMAN multi -criteria decision making methods. The MEREC -AROMAN 
composite ranking exhib its a moderate but theoretically significant deviation when compared with the classical CAPM beta 
ranking. These deviations are particularly concentrated between large-cap holdings and major banks (appearing safer in the multi-
dimensional ranking) and small-cap issuers (appearing riskier in the multi -dimensional ranking). We reveal that Amihud liquidity 
has the largest MEREC weight in the cross-sectional distribution of BIST-100. 
Sub-period analysis reveals that the AROMAN-beta relationship is regime-dependent. This relationship shows positive correlation 
in both heterodox and orthodox policy periods until 2023, and negative correlation in the post -2023 normalization period. This 
direction change is consistent with high -liquid stocks such as major banks and holding companies becoming safer during the 
interest rate tightening period, while low -beta small -scale stocks become riskier. On the other hand, sectoral fixed effect 
regressions, RISE-based behavioral regime analysis, structural break tests and fuzzy AROMAN extension confirm robustness. These 
controls were subjected to robustness checks along four axes and remained stable: (i) sectoral fixed effect regression reveal ed 
that sectoral identity does not provide additional explanatory power beyond beta and liquidity; (ii) RISE-based behavioral regime 
analysis showed that downside risk measures gain discriminatory power in pessimist regimes; (iii) structural break tests supported 
policy-event based regime boundaries on a data -driven basis; (iv) fuzzy AROMAN extension, alternative MCDM methods

The contribution of this study is threefold. Methodologically, it represents, to our knowledge, the first application of the MEREC -
AROMAN combination to BIST -100 and integrates the out -of-sample predictive validation step that is missing in most of the 
previous MCDM-finance literature. Empirically, it documents the dominant role of liquidity risk in the cross -sectional distribution 
of the Turkish stock market and the regime dependence of the beta -multidimensional risk relationship. Practically, it provides 
evidence that liquidity -enhanced multi-dimensional risk profiling can outperform single -factor beta in regime -change 
environments, leading to potential applications in portfolio construction and systemic risk monitoring. 
Future research can extend the results of the study by including different markets. On the other hand, the integration of hig h-
frequency intraday data will enable further improvement of liquidity and tail risk measurement. Finally, the integration of multiple 
investor type-specific RISE variants (domestic, foreign, qualified, fund) will enable a richer behavioral regime analysis .
