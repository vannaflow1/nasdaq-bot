learning: A comparative study of GARCH,

Abstract 
Forecasting stock price volatility at the individual stock level is a major challenge 
in managing financial risks; it has widespread implications for portfolio strategies, 
pricing derivatives, and assessing regulatory capital adequacy. Although there have 
been a number of studies using market indices (e.g., VIX and associated methods) 
to study forecasting stock market volatility, relatively few studies have applied and 
evaluated a number of different types of machine learning methods to individual 
stock data in terms of actual (realised) volatility forecasting. This study fills that 
gap by presenting a comprehensive analysis of the relationship between stock 
price volatility and various machine learning techniques on a sample of ten well-
known US stocks (across three sectors: technology, finance, and energy) based 
on data from January 2018 to December 2024 (1,759 trading days). We apply five 
modelling approaches: a classical GARCH(1,1) econometric model, a classical 
GJR-GARCH(1,1,1) econometric model, a Random Forest regressor, a two-layer 
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, and a two-layer Gated Recurrent Unit 
(GRU) network. For each of the four modelling techniques, we use a rolling 5-day 
annualised actual (realised) volatility forecast as our final outcomes. Evaluation of 
the prediction results of each of these models was measured using RMSE, MAE, 
QLIKE loss, parametric Value-at-Risk (VaR) measures, Expected Shortfall (ES), 
and the Kupiec backtest. We also performed an analysis of the prevailing market 
regime through K-Means clustering, created an ablation study using variables as 
features in the models to assess the importance of those variables, and developed 
a computational efficiency benchmark for each model. In terms of predicted errors, 
Random Forest was proven to yield very low predicted error (mean RMSE = 0.0892) 
in comparison to deep learning methods, plus Random Forest was able to be trained 
approximately 7 times faster than deep learning methods. GRU was ranked second 
and outperformed LSTM on most of the metrics used in this evaluation. GARCH 
models remain a viable option for estimating risk measures and calibrating VaR, 
especially during period of stability in the financial markets.

I. Introduction 
Volatility forecasting is a key aspect of quantitative finance today; and for option pric-
ing, risk-adjusted portfolio construction, Value-at-Risk (VaR) computations and regu-
latory capital allocations (Basel III and IV), accurate estimates of future asset volatility 
are crucial [1–3]. Extensive research has been carried out on volatility modelling, with 
the GARCH family of models dominating the field for the last several decades, taking 
advantage of the empirical pattern of volatility clustering, or the empirical finding that 
big price movements are often followed by other large price movements [4,5].
Another field that has grown in both popularity and prominence recently is the field 
of using implied volatility indices, such as the CBOE Volatility Index (VIX) to predict 
future market returns and risk. Numerous studies including those conducted by Giot 
[6], Rubbaniy et al. [7]; and Elyasiani et al. [8] have found statistically significant 
predictive capability in both VIX and similar indices regarding the eventual direction 
and magnitude of S&P 500 returns. More recently, Campisi et al. [9] evaluated a wide 
range of machine learning classifiers; including logistic regression, LDA, ridge and 
lasso regularization, random forests, bagging and gradient boosting to eleven differ-
ent CBOE volatility indices, ultimately finding that ensemble methods such as random 
forests and bagging provided the highest accuracy in predicting the direction of S&P 
500 index.
The method used to calculate market volatility using an index-based ETF 
(exchange-traded fund) approach fails to provide investors and traders with individual 
stock derivatives, concentrated portfolios, and/or sector risk information (common 
practices within the financial industry). As an alternative to this ETF-based approach 
for market volatility, stock-level volatility forecasts would provide practitioners who 
manage derivatives on equities an actionable manner of obtaining an individual 
equity’s volatility as opposed to market volatility. This study follows a stock-specific 
approach to obtain such stock-level forecasts and uses GARCH, GJR-GARCH, Ran-
dom Forest, LSTM, and GRU to forecast stock-level volatilities for 10 U.S. companies 
across Technology, Financial Services, and Energy Services sectors over the period 
2018–2024.
The following is the organization of this research. The second section will review 
the literature associated with stock volatility forecasting. The third section discusses 
the dataset used in this study, including the variables produced for use in modelling. 
The fourth section will outline the modelling approach to forecasting. The fifth sec-
tion outlines the performance measures that are being used to assess the models 
in this study. The sixth section will report the findings of all of the models used along 
with their respective statistics presented in both tables and/or graphs and Section 7 
concludes the study.

B. Machine learning approaches
Recent advances in the use of machine learning (ML) for predicting volatility and other asset classes have been primar-
ily driven by improvements in deep learning techniques and the availability of larger data sets. In particular, Gu et al. [13] 
conducted a very comprehensive review (2020) of the different ML techniques that can be used to predict asset returns, 
concluding that the performance of both tree-based methods (e.g., random forests and gradient boosting) and Artificial 
Neural Networks (ANNs) is significantly better than that of conventional linear models. Bucci [14] reported on the application 
of several different ML methods to predict daily realized volatility (RV) for multiple equity indices over time. Specifically, Bucci 
found that random forests and gradient boosted models consistently had lower mean square error (MSE) than a generalized 
autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (GARCH) model for several of the indices examined. Kim and Won (2018) 
[15] examined the use of long short-term memory (LSTM) networks to predict stock returns, finding LSTMs outperformed 
GARCH model in predicting VaR on an one-day-ahead basis. Additionally, Rahimikia and Poon [16] developed an LSTM 
model for predicting S&P 500 volatility that integrated both social media sentiment features and time series price features. 
Finally, both Qin and Song [17] and Hu etal. [18] developed models based on gated recurrent units (GRUs) and found that 
GRUs are computationally more efficient than LSTMs for achieving similar forecasting accuracy.
C. Volatility indices as market-level predictors
It is also common in separate studies to use implied volatility to predict future price trends. Giot [ 6] showed that VIX has 
value as a contrarian predictor; when VIX levels are high, market recovery will likely occur soon after. A second article, 
Simon and Wiggins [19], looked at VIX and put/call ratios as predictors of future S&P 500 futures returns. The most 
similar study to the method I used in this paper is Campisi et al. [ 9], evaluated seven machine learning models on eight 
CBOE implied volatility indices — specifically, VIX, VIX9D, VIX3M, VIX6M, VVIX, SKEW, VXN, and GVZ/OVX/PUTCALL 
- to determine whether they could predict directional movements in the S&P 500 index over a 30-day period. Campisi 
et al.‘s major finding was that Random Forest and Bagging were significantly better than Logistic Regression, LDA, and 
Regularized methods for predicting directional movement of the S&P 500.
D. Research gap and contribution
There is evidence from the review that a significant gap exists regarding post-GFC assessment of machine learning’s 
role in predicting volatility. Although machine learning methods have been used to analyse both the behaviour of volatil -
ity at the aggregate level (e.g., how volatility behaves/changes on an overall market basis) as well as predict the future 
behaviour of market volatility on an index (e.g., NASDAQ or S&P), only a handful of studies have benchmarked the 
ability of various machine learning architectures to predict realised volatility of individual stocks across multiple sectors 
of the economy and evaluated the accuracy of their forecasts against standard measures of point forecast accuracy and 
risk calibration (e.g., VaR). The main contributions of this research are: (1) direct comparison of volatility forecasting 
between GARCH, GJR-GARCH, Random Forest, LSTM, and GRU at the stock level for ten US equities across three 
sectors. (2) evaluation using multiple criteria (e.g., RMSE, MAE, QLIKE, VaR/ ES accuracy and time to calculate) of

III. Data
A. Dataset
Our data set contains the daily, adjusted closing prices and daily volume of shares for ten US stocks: AAPL, MSFT, 
GOOGL, AMZN, TSLA (Tech/Automotive), JPM, GS, BAC (Finance), XOM, CVX (Energy). The data was obtained from 
yfinance, which auto-adjusts for stock splits and dividends [20], covering the period of January 1, 2018, to December 
31, 2024 (1,759 trading days). Table 1 summarises the mean daily return, return volatility, and mean/max annualised 
realised volatility for each of the ten tickers over the full sample. All data were retrieved through yfinance’s public API 
in accordance with Yahoo Finance’s terms of service for non-commercial research use, and no proprietary or access- 
restricted data were used at any stage of this study. These years contain the late-cycle expansionary low-volatility period 
of 2018–2019, the extraordinary COVID-19 shock of March through April 2020, the V-shaped recovery and the technology 
bull market of 2020–2021, the Federal Reserve’s tightening of monetary policy during 2022, and the stabilization period of 
2023–2024.
B. Realised volatility construction
The primary forecasting target is the 5-day rolling annualised realized volatility (RV), computed as the standard deviation of 
daily log-returns over a rolling 5-day window, scaled by √252. Daily log-returns are defined as rt = ln(Pt/Pt−1). We use a 5-day 
window because it balances two competing concerns: daily squared returns are a noisy proxy for latent volatility [12], while 
windows longer than a trading week would smooth over the short-horizon risk shifts that VaR and derivatives-pricing appli-
cations need to capture. The √252 scaling factor annualises the 5-day standard deviation using the standard convention 
of 252 trading days per year, so that our RV estimates are directly comparable to the annualised volatility figures quoted 
in options and VaR markets. Each model predicts RVt+1, i.e., the realised volatility over the five days immediately following 
observation t. Fig 1 illustrates the time series of daily log-returns and 5-day rolling RV for all ten tickers over the full sample 
period.

IV. Modelling framework
A. GARCH and GJR – GARCH
The GARCH(1,1) model specifies the conditional variance as:
 σ2t = ω + αε 2t–1 + βσ 2t–1 
The GJR-GARCH(1,1,1) extension adds an asymmetric leverage term:
 σ2t = ω + αε 2t–1 + γ It–1 ε2t–1 + βσ 2t–1 
where It−1 = 1 if εt−1 < 0 and 0 otherwise, and γ captures the leverage effect [10]. Both models were estimated via maximum 
likelihood with a Gaussian innovation distribution, re-estimated on a rolling 252-day window to prevent lookahead bias.

B. Random Forest
Random Forest [22] is a bagging-based ensemble model that fits B decision trees on bootstrap samples, selecting from a 
random subspace of features at each split. We used 200 estimators, maximum depth 8, minimum samples per leaf 5, on 
an 80/20 temporal split. The 13-dimensional feature vector at time t is considered as a flat input; no sequential structure is 
assumed.
C. LSTM and GRU networks
Our LSTM architecture [23] comprises two stacked layers of 64 and 32 hidden units respectively, each layer followed by 
20% Dropout [24], a Dense(16, ReLU) projection, and a scalar output. The GRU architecture [25,26] is architecturally 
identical, replacing LSTM cells with GRU cells. Both networks were trained using the Adam optimiser [27] with MSE loss 
on 20-step sliding windows, for up to 30 epochs with early stopping (patience = 5).
D. Training protocol
All ML models follow an 80/20 temporal split (~1,400 training observations, ~ 350 test observations). Temporal ordering is 
strictly preserved with no shuffling. GARCH models use a rolling 252-day estimation window at each test-set time step. 
This ensures that the evaluation metrics are computed on the perfect out-of-sample forecasts.
V. Evaluation metrics
Five evaluation metrics are used to evaluate model performance. RMSE and MAE are common types of symmetric loss 
functions. RMSE gives greater weight to large errors while exhibiting sensitivity to the degree of outlier forecast failures 
when markets are under stress. The definition of QLIKE loss is:

VI. Empirical results
A. Overall forecasting performance
The performance of the forecasting method, as shown in Table 2, can be quantified by evaluating the metrics for all ten 
tickers. With the lowest RMSE (0.0892), MAE (0.0614), and lowest QLIKE (0.3105), Random Forest provides the best fore-
casting accuracy of all three metrics, confirming that Random Forest has the best point-forecast accuracy of all forecasting 
methods considered. The metric that most clearly demonstrates this divergence in Random Forest and LSTM forecast 
accuracy is the QLIKE metric. The QLIKE for LSTM (0.6159) is nearly 2X that of Random Forest, indicating that while 
LSTM consistently underestimates the magnitude of volatility; GRU is relatively less likely to do so (QLIKE = 0.4618). Lastly, 
GARCH models can achieve competitive calibration of VaR, as their breach rates are very close to the specified 5%.
B. Ticker-level RMSE and heatmap
Fig 3 displays all tickers’ continuous Machine Learning Models’ RMSE data in a heatmap, while Table 3 delivers a numer-
ical breakdown. Of the ten tickers studied, Random Forest produced superior performance for all of them. However, the 
stock that was least easy to forecast was TSLA as demonstrated by the RF RMSE of 0.187 due to the very high idio-
syncratic volatility from news flow that was not included as lagged price features. Meanwhile, LSTM performed worse 
compared to Random Forest in forecasting AMZN, with LSTM RMSE 74% larger than RF, indicating that LSTM, due to its 
recurrent bias, is not a well-suited forecasting tool for the underlying volatility patterns associated with AMZN.
C. Feature ablation study
The RF RMSE comparison in Fig 4 and Table 4 displays the difference between the returns-only baseline and the com-
plete 13-feature set. The addition of realised volatility lags and volume results in reduced RMSE for 7 of the ten tickers, 
while AMZN (−3.2%), BAC (−6.9%), and XOM (−0.01%) show a slight increase in RMSE instead. The greatest reduction 
occurs at both MSFT (+11.6%) and AAPL (+7.2%), while TSLA has little to no improvement (+0.9%). The low improvement 
for TSLA indicates that its volatility is primarily driven by news flow rather than autocorrelation, while the negative results 
for AMZN, BAC, and XOM suggest that for some tickers the additional lagged features add noise rather than signal. The 
aggregate improvement across all tickers is 3.17%.

E. Computational efficiency
The time and memory benchmarks are shown in Fig 6 and detailed in Table 6. Random Forest has a ~ 7 × advantage in train-
ing speed vs. LSTM/GRU and uses <1 MB of memory vs. ~ 10 MB of memory consumed by deep learning models. Since RF 
also has a higher level of forecast accuracy than either LSTM or GRU, it is superior in terms of both accuracy and efficiency 
for this application and therefore comes to dominate the accuracy vs efficiency frontier for the application described.
