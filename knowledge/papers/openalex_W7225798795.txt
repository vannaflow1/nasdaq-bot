Prediction Inference for Hybrid Models on Realized
Volatility Forecasting
Kyle Parrella1 and Kejin Wu1
1Loyola University Chicago, Department of Mathematics and Statistics
Abstract
GARCH-LSTM hybrid models have become increasingly popular in the volatility forecasting
literature. To date, these models have almost exclusively focused on point estimation and
accuracy measures such as RMSE and MAPE, while failing to assess the uncertainty of these
point estimates. We approach this gap in two ways. First, we extend the existing GARCH-
LSTM model by modeling the conditional mean with an ARMA structure. Second, we develop
a forward bootstrap algorithm to build multi-step-ahead prediction intervals for future realized
volatility. In order to address the notorious undercoverage of prediction intervals, we imple-
ment two solutions. One draws bootstrap innovations from the empirical residual distribution
to mimic the fat-tail property of financial series. The other utilizes principles from conformal
prediction to systematically extend the prediction interval length. We evaluate the methods
in simulations with heavy-tailed and time-varying GARCH processes and in rolling-window
forecasts of four U.S. stock indices from 2020 to 2025. The ARMA-based hybrid gives the low-
est point-forecast errors in most settings, and combining both remedies with the mean-based
hybrid yields intervals closest to the nominal level among competing methods.
1 Introduction
Volatility forecasting plays a critical role in many fields of finance, such as derivative pricing and
portfolio risk management. As a result, reliable and accurate methods for volatility forecasting are
essential to the success of these industries. Traditionally, these markets have used the econometric
ARCH and GARCH-type models proposed by Engle (1982) and Bollerslev (1986) to meet this
need. Over the years, variations of these models, such as the EGARCH (Nelson, 1991) and GJR-
GARCH models (Glosten et al., 1993), have evolved from their predecessors as improved methods
in certain use cases. Similarly, the EWMA model was originated with Roberts (1959) and further
refined by Longerstaey and Spencer (1996); see Francq and Zakoïan (2019) for a comprehensive
introduction of GARCH-type models.
In response to the limitations of traditional econometric techniques for volatility forecasting,
researchers have turned to machine learning techniques because of their ability to better capture
nonlinear and nonparametric trends. It is well-known that neural networks have the universal
approximation property, i.e., they can approximate any functions under some smoothness condi-
tions; see McCulloch and Pitts (1943) and Cybenko (1989); Hornik et al. (1989); Yarotsky (2018);
Yarotsky and Zhevnerchuk (2020) for this property displayed in simple, multilayer and deep feed-
forward neural networks, respectively. Motivated by this powerful estimation ability, researchers

have increasingly applied these neural network-type models to a wide range of scientific areas,
including finance. In financial mathematics, researchers derive the prediction of volatility directly
from a well-trained neural network and prediction is conducted a priori. One unfortunate feature
of the neural networks in this application is that they function as a sort of “black box”, which makes
statistical reasoning between predictions and historical series difficult. Instead of purely relying
on neural networks, Hajizadeh et al. (2012) took the output from standard GARCH models as the
input of neural networks to improve the accuracy of forecasting volatility, resulting in a so-called
hybrid model. Similar works include those of García-Medina and Aguayo-Moreno (2024) and Wang
and Lu (2024). Kim and Won (2018) proposed the idea of feeding the estimated parameters of dif-
ferent types of GARCH models with other explanatory variables as input to the Long Short-Term
Memory (LSTM) model, a form of recurrent neural networks originally proposed by Hochreiter
and Schmidhuber (1997). LSTM models have modeling applications in a variety of fields including
physics, chemistry, and finance; see Zhang et al. (2020), Mandis et al. (2024) and Moghar and
Hamiche (2020) for more details, respectively.
GARCH-LSTM models have become increasingly common in volatility forecasting literature.
This is due to the theoretical and empirical advantages these models have (Kim and Won, 2018;
García-Medina and Aguayo-Moreno, 2024). The fundamental idea of the hybrid proposed by Kim
and Won (2018) is that the LSTM is exceptional in leveraging the high-level temporal patterns of
the time series, which are revealed by the parameters of various GARCH models. By combining
the networks and classical models, hybrids are able to capture the econometric and deep learning
techniques each model presents individually. GARCH creates a structured framework for defining
conditional volatility, while LSTM is able to capture long-range and nonlinear interactions that
can be missed by GARCH. Hybrid models also offer computational benefits, as they can be less
computationally intensive than strict LSTM modeling (Wang and Lu, 2024).
However, the existing literature on this topic emphasizes point estimation and improving em-
pirical accuracy metrics such as root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute percentage
error (MAPE). There is a lack of literature that references more rigorous statistical measures, such
as prediction intervals or uncertainty measures. Motivated by the forward bootstrap prediction
methods developed gradually in Pan and Politis (2016); Politis and Wu (2023); Wu and Politis
(2024a), we propose a form of forward bootstrap algorithm to build prediction intervals for volatil-
ity forecasting involving LSTM. In short, the forward bootstrap prediction technique offers an
approach to evaluate the whole distribution of forecasting. We additionally propose a systematic
wayinspiredbytheconformalpredictionideaofVovketal.(2005)towidenthepredictionintervals,
improving the coverage rate for finite sample cases. Similar to the usage of a confidence interval
in quantifying the accuracy of parameter estimations, the prediction interval can be utilized as a
complementary tool to guide the decisions of economists on determining the prices of derivative
goods, the risk of assets and the allocation of portfolios, etc. Moreover, we further improve the
current GARCH-LSTM model in Kim and Won (2018) by modeling the mean component of the
financial series with an ARMA model; consistent with the idea of ARMA-GARCH modeling. Our
contributions are summarized as follows in Section 1.1.
1.1 Our contributions
• We refine the hybrid model in the seminal work Kim and Won (2018) to forecast realized
volatility. More specifically, we incorporate a machine learning model with the ARMA-
2
GARCH framework to perform volatility forecasting. To train the model, we define a specific
MSE loss criterion that trains an LSTM model to approximate the conditional variance when
the true variance is not observed. Moreover, we propose a maximum likelihood estimation
algorithm to train all parameters of the LSTM and the ARMA-GARCH framework simulta-
neously.
• Beyond point prediction, we propose the forward bootstrap algorithm to make (multiple-

classical models. Several empirical findings are clear: (1) the hybrid LSTM models with the
additional information from the estimated variance and coefficients of classical models have
a better point prediction performance compared to naive LSTM and classical models; (2)
the naive LSTM method can be beaten even by classical models for some simulations and
real data scenarios; (3) even predictions over long horizons perform remarkably well, with
the hybrid models outperforming classical models in coverage on horizons of 20 days (see
Tables 3 and 4).

2.2 EGARCH model
While innovative and more practical than ARCH models, the GARCH(p,q) process was flawed
in certain applications. Nelson (1991) identified and reconciled three major flaws. The first of
these issues was that the GARCH(p,q) process failed to capture some trends that were observed in
financial data. Namely, the negative correlation of stock returns and changes in returns volatility
that was observed by researchers was not captured in the model. Secondly, it was difficult to
interpret the “persistence” of shocks to the conditional variance. The final flaw identified was that
the non-negativity constraints of certain variables were restrictive in practice.
To reconcile these flaws, Nelson (1991) proposed a new GARCH mechanism known as expo-
nential GARCH (EGARCH). It is obvious that if σ2 t is the conditional variance given Ft−1, i.e.,
the information at time t − 1, then it must be nonnegative with probability one. As opposed to
other GARCH models that ensure nonnegativity by making σ2 t a linear combination of positive
random variables, Nelson (1991) adopted a different function to ensure σ 2 t remained nonnegative:

2.3 EWMA model
The Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) is a statistic that represents the average
of a value of interest over a given time period of length T where the values of earlier inputs are
given less weight in the EWMA value. By including a weighting factor λ, the EWMA’s sensitivity
to drift can be adjusted. Our definition of the EWMA process originates from Roberts (1959) and
is as follows:
St = λSt−1 + (1 − λ)Yt,
where t = 1, 2, ..., T; Yt is the point observation at time t, and St is the weighted average of interest
a time t. By the definition above, we can determine the EWMA process’s “depth,” that is, how
much past observations impact St, by adjusting λ ∈ (0, 1], where the larger λ is, the more weight
past observations have.

3.3 Conformal extension on interval
It is well known that prediction intervals suffer from undercoverage in practice, especially in finite-
sample settings. In particular, Politis (2015) provided a perspective to understand this undercover-
age issue, i.e., the standard prediction interval does not capture the model’s estimation variability;
see the following Remark 3.2 to understand the effects of model estimation variability on influencing
the coverage rate of prediction intervals.
Remark 3.2. Suppose we consider the linear regression model Yi = X T i β + ϵi with n samples.
By the least squares technique, we can get the estimation of the parameter coefficient ˆβ. Given
a new predictor xf , we have the predictive root Yf − bYf := xT f β + ϵf − xT f ˆβ = ϵf + ef , where
ef := xT f β − xT f ˆβ stands for the estimation error. Under standard conditions and a well-specified
model, E(xT f ˆβ) = xT f β and V ar(xT f ˆβ) = O(1/n), as n → ∞. Since ef p → 0, it is tempting to apply
[ bYf + bF −1 ϵ,α/2, bYf + bF −1 ϵ,1−α/2] (9)
as a PI, where bF −1 ϵ,α/2 and bF −1 ϵ,1−α/2 are α/2 and 1 − α/2 quantiles of bFϵ, which is the empirical
distribution of residuals {ri}n i=1 from regression. In other words, PI (9) treats ef as 0 and typi-
cally results in undercoverage. However, by Chebyshev’s inequality, ef = Op(1/√n), which is not
negligible with n being small. Thus, it is important to take the model estimation variability into
account when the prediction interval is built.
There are multiple ways to improve the coverage rate of prediction intervals. For example,
Politis (2015) defined a so-called pertinent prediction interval (PPI) to capture the model esti-
mation variability by approximating the distribution of the predictive root with the bootstrap
9
technique; see more explanation and applications of this pertinent prediction interval in Wang
and Politis (2026); Wu and Politis (2024b); Wu (2026). However, the procedure to build the PPI
requires the repetition of the training procedure with the bootstrap data multiple times, which is
computationally heavy when the complex model estimator is adopted. On the other hand, con-
formal prediction, which can guarantee the marginal coverage, introduced by Vovk et al. (2005),
has become increasingly popular recently; see variants of conformal prediction methods from Cher-
nozhukov et al. (2021); Sesia and Romano (2021); Romano et al. (2019); Lei et al. (2018), for other
examples. Besides, we should mention that the PPI and conformal prediction can be related; see
that one specific type of PPI shares a common intermediate step of making the special conformal
prediction in Chernozhukov et al. (2021); Dai et al. (2026).
In our case, since the complex LSTM is used for our prediction problems, we propose a system-
atic way inspired by the conformal prediction idea to extend the length of the prediction intervals
returned by Algorithms 1 and 2. In short, we use a recursive prediction scheme to compute the
conformal score based on the miscoverage error in the recursive prediction interval. Then, the lower

4 Simulations
For the M-H-GARCH method, the conditional mean is the rolling average over the most recent
p observations. In practice, p is a tuning parameter whose value can be determined by cross-
validation. In our simulation studies, we take p = {1, . . . , 5} to ensure the simulation is compre-
hensive and report the optimal p according to the root mean squared error of the point predic-
tions. For the ARMA-H-GARCH method, the orders (L, K) are selected per replication by AIC
over L, K ≤ 5. The chosen order is then estimated using the conditional likelihood of Eq. (7)
with t0 = max(L, K) + 1. For the naive LSTM method, the mean component is also taken as
the rolling average, as in the M-H-GARCH model. This results in the difference between naive
LSTM and M-H-GARCH models being due to the inputs of the models; see Appendix A for more
implementation details of different methods.

Throughout the study, we consider five methods: (1) M-H-GARCH; (2) ARMA-H-GARCH;

λ = 0.94. Both GARCH and EGARCH are estimated by Gaussian quasi-maximum likelihood with
the arch package in Python. Prediction intervals based on methods (3)–(5) are constructed with

4.1 Simulation Results
All simulation results are presented in Tables 1 to 4, where sample standard deviations over repli-
cations are given in parentheses; best values, i.e., smallest for RMSE, MAPE and MDAE, closest
to 0.95 for Coverage and shortest interval length are bold. From the results, we summarize several
findings as follows:
1. For point prediction, the ARMA-H-GARCH method dominates other methods. Generally
speaking, it has the smallest RMSE, MAPE and MDAE for the two simulation models.
Moreover, it always outperforms the naive LSTM method on point prediction. On the other
hand, the M-H-GARCH method based on Kim and Won (2018) does not guarantee consis-
tently superior performance over the naive LSTM method in point predictions. In short, the
implication is two-fold: (1) using the estimates of variance and the parameters from classical
models improves the accuracy of the LSTM’s realized volatility forecast; (2) using bootstrap
prediction intervals, the ARMA mean structure is superior to a rolling mean estimate when
estimating the mean of financial series. We also note that the naive LSTM method is beaten
by GARCH(1,1) and EGARCH(1,1). This implies that the solely network-based method
does not outperform the classical methods in some situations.
2. For the prediction intervals, all optimal coverage rates come from our ARMA-H-GARCH or
M-H-GARCH method. Moreover, all optimal coverage rates are obtained based on generating
η∗ T +h from its empirical distribution bFη , which emphasizes the importance of mimicking the
fat tail property of financial series as we mentioned in Remark 3.1. In addition, the conformal
extension always makes the coverage rate larger than the variant without this modification,
though the sacrifice is the wider prediction interval.

6 Conclusions
In this work, we give concrete algorithms based on forward bootstrap construction to make point
predictions and build prediction intervals for future realized volatility. In summary, the hybrid
LSTM model with the additional information from the estimated variance and coefficients of clas-
sical models has a better point prediction performance compared to naive LSTM and classical
models. Moreover, improved by our two strategies, the resulting prediction interval has a good
coverage on simulation and real data.
However, it is clear that our prediction interval still undercovers future volatility even after
being widened by conformal prediction. The reason for this is partially illustrated in Remark
3.2, i.e., the estimation variability of our model is still not fully captured. A more conservative
way to further relieve this undercoverage issue is making the so-called pertinent prediction inter-
val by approximating the predictive root distribution with bootstrap. However, this procedure is
computationally heavy due to the training procedure needing to be performed on each bootstrap
data. Recently, Politis (2026) proposed a hybrid resampling scheme to construct pertinent predic-
tion intervals in a computationally efficient way. A future extension of this work could leverage
this hybrid resampling scheme to further improve the coverage of prediction intervals from the
hybrid model. Also, the increasing availability of high-frequency financial data has spurred the
development of new methods in financial economics. This high-frequency data analysis has wide
application in financial economics; see Fan et al. (2012) for portfolio selection, Fan and Wang
(2008) for instantaneous volatility estimation, and Russell and Engle (2010) for an analysis of the
mechanisms through which prices reach equilibrium. It would be interesting to see how the hybrid
models proposed in this work handle high-frequency data. Moreover, it is also intriguing to see
how the hybrid model can take advantage of the high-frequency volatility models, e.g., parallel
GARCH and realized GARCH models developed by Shephard and Sheppard (2010); Hansen et al.
(2012).

A Implementation details
In this section, we provide more implementation details and the training policies for various meth-
ods. Every method involving a network (M-H-GARCH, ARMA-H-GARCH, and the naive LSTM)
uses the same architecture: a single LSTM layer with 32 hidden units, a sequence length of 5, i.e.,
the 5 most recent inputs, and the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10−3. The naive LSTM
thus differs from the hybrids only in its inputs, which consist of the return series alone without
the parameter block Θ or the fitted GARCH, EGARCH and EWMA variance series. For these
variance series, to improve numerical stability, they are truncated to [10−12, 1] whenever they are
updated along a bootstrap pseudo series {y∗ T +h}. The single LSTM layer structure has been taken
from previous studies Kim and Won (2018); Hajizadeh et al. (2012); Wang (2009), etc.
