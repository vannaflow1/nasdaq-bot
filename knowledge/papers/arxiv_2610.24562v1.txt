Abstract
Market makers are fundamental actors in today’s economy, as they provideliquidityby
accelerating the matching between demand and supply. Accordingly,sequential market making
models the problem of an agent who repeatedly interacts with a population of buyers and
sellers. In each round, the market maker simultaneously posts a bid price and an ask price
to a buyer-seller pair. A trade occurs whenever the posted prices meet the agents’ valuations:
the buyer buys if their valuation is at least the ask price, and the seller sells if their valuation
is at most the bid price. The two trades occur independently, so the market maker may sell
without buying (short selling), buy without selling, or observe no trade at all. Crucially, the
market maker must satisfyinventory constraints: over the entire horizon, the total number of
bought and sold items should balance out, while during the process the inventory must remain
sufficiently small, thus avoiding excessive accumulation of items and risky short-selling exposure.
We study this problem through the lens ofregret minimization, when buyers’ and sellers’
valuations are sampled from unknown distributions. In this setting, the market maker (that is, the
learner) sequentially posts bids and asks so as to maximize its cumulative revenue over T∈N >0
interactions while satisfying the inventory constraints. Specifically, the goal is to minimize the
difference between the learner’s performance and that of the optimal inventory-aware bid-ask pair
that knows the valuation distributions (i.e., thecumulative regret). We first study the hardest
scenario, where the learner only observes whether each trade (buy and/or sell) occurs. In this
case, we provide a computationally efficient algorithm attaining eO(T 2/3) regret, thus matching
the well-known lower bound for the easier dynamic pricing setting of order Ω( T
2/3) [ KL03],
while satisfying the global inventory constraints and keeping the inventory at every round below
eO(T 2/3) items. We then study the more interesting, and arguably more realistic, setting where
the market maker has access to aLimit Order Book(LOB). Accordingly, the learner observes
the realized valuation whenever the associated trade does not occur; otherwise, it only observes
that the trade has occurred. In this case, we provide a computationally efficient algorithm that
attains eO(√T ) regret, satisfies the global inventory constraints, and keeps the inventory below
eO(√T ) items at every round. We complement these results by providing instance-dependent
bounds that improve according to the c.d.f. value associated with the discretized optimum. We
conclude by establishing alower boundshowing that any algorithm that guarantees per-round
inventory of at most O(T β) must incur regret of at least Ω( T 1−β) for every β <1/2, even in the
significantly easier setting where the agent knows the valuation distributions.

1 Introduction
Market making is a foundational part of modern financial markets [ GM85, AS08]. At a basic
level, market makers sustain trading by continuously offering to buy and sell, thereby providing
theimmediacyandliquiditythat make markets usable in the first place. In the absence of such
intermediation, even modest imbalances between buyers and sellers could translate into large delays,
wide spreads, and severe price dislocations. This role is not minor and in today’s electronic markets,
liquidity provision is carried out at a massive scale by highly sophisticated firms, whose business is
precisely to keep markets tradable under a broad range of conditions. Market making is therefore
not merely a financial activity, but a central problem inmarket design,algorithms, andonline
decision-making[ MMR24, CBCC+25]: a market maker must repeatedly quote prices in an uncertain
environment, balancing the short-term opportunity to earn spread revenue with the longer-term
need to remain resilient as order flow evolves. What makes this problem especially subtle is that
liquidity provision is inseparable from risk-taking [ GLFT13]. A market maker does not simply post
prices; it absorbs temporary supply–demand imbalances by taking them on directly. For this reason,
inventoryis not a secondary accounting quantity, but one of the key forces governing the entire
process: without controlling inventory, apparent revenue gains may reflect unsustainable exposure
rather than economic performance.
More broadly, market making is closely connected to other well-studied problems inrevenue
maximization, such asdynamic pricing[ KL03] andbilateral trade[ CLM26]. As in dynamic pricing,
the seller repeatedly sets prices in the presence of uncertain and evolving demand, with the goal of
learning how prices affect trade and revenue over time. At the same time, market making also has a
two-sided nature that brings it closer to bilateral trade settings, since the intermediary interacts
with both buyers and sellers and must jointly manage the terms at which it is willing to trade
on each side of the market. There is, however, an important difference. In standard bilateral
trade models, trade is often viewed as a more localized interaction between the two sides. In
market making, by contrast, trades may occur in only one direction for extended periods, as long as
the intermediary is able to absorb the resulting imbalance. This makesinventorycentral to the
problem: the market maker can keep trading on one side only as long as its inventory remains within
acceptable bounds. For this reason, market making is not simply a two-sided pricing problem, but a
sequential revenue maximization problem in which pricing, trade, and inventory are tightly linked
across time. Thus, market making shares important features with classical revenue maximization
problems, but differs in that current pricing decisions directly shape future feasible actions through
their effect on inventory.
These connections also point naturally to the framework ofregret minimizationandonline learn-
ing[ CBL06, Ora19, Sli19]. In market making, as in other sequential revenue maximization problems,
the distributions governing buyers and sellers are typically unknown to the maker. The market
maker must therefore learn how prices affect trade while continuing to make sequential decisions,
without knowing in advance which pricing policy maximizes revenue. Regret minimization cap-
tures this objective by comparing the cumulative revenue of the algorithm to that of the best
benchmark policy computed knowing the valuation distributions of both buyers and sellers (we
refer to this difference as thecumulative regret). This viewpoint is standard in related problems
such asdynamic pricingandbilateral trade, where the central challenge is to balance exploration
and revenue over time (or in welfare-maximization settings, to balance exploration and social
welfare) [BZ09, BDKS15, BCBCC24, GDFS25]. It is thus a natural framework for studying market
making in uncertain environments.

1.1 The Model
We model market making as a discrete-time online learning problem between an agent (the “market
maker”, or simply the “maker”) and a sequence of “takers”. Specifically, at each round t∈ [T ],1
anewbuyer–seller pair arrives, each interested in trading one unit of the underlying asset. Their
private valuations are denoted by V buyt , Vsellt ∈ [0, 1], where V sellt is the minimum amount the seller
is willing to accept to sell one unit, and V buyt is the maximum amount the buyer is willing to pay to
buy one unit. We assume that the valuations are sampled i.i.d. across rounds from an unknown
distribution; however, within any given round, the buyer’s and seller’s valuations may bearbitrarily
correlated. Consequently, the maker selects a bid–ask pair ( Bt, At) such that Bt < At without
any knowledge of V buyt , Vsellt . On the buy side, if V sellt ≤B t, the seller accepts the bid and the
maker buys at price Bt, obtaining a reward −Bt; otherwise, no trade occurs, and the reward is
0. Symmetrically, on the sell side, if V buyt ≥A t, the buyer accepts the ask and the maker sells at
price At, obtaining reward At; otherwise, no trade occurs, and the reward is 0. Hence, the maker’s
per-round reward is:
At ·I(V buyt ≥A t)−B t ·I(V sellt ≤B t).
A specific feature of market making is that the maker can sell an asset before buying it, thereby
taking ashortposition. Nevertheless, market makers typically aim to keep theirinventorysmall, so
as to avoid a large imbalance between the number of units bought and sold over time. Indeed, a
large positive inventory exposes the maker to downward price movements, while a large negative
inventory exposes it to upward ones. In both cases, profits become increasingly dependent on
the market value of the asset, rather than solely on the bid–ask spread. To capture this aspect,
we impose two inventory constraints. First, we requireglobal inventory balanceover the entire
horizon, namely, PTt=1 I (V buyt ≥A t) = PTt=1 I (V sellt ≤B t). This ensures that the maker ends with
zero inventory, while still allowing temporary short or long positions during learning. Second, we
require theinventorylevel to remainuniformly boundedat every round by a parameter α∈N >0,
that is, |It| ≤α, for all t∈ [T ], where It := Ptτ=1 I (V sellτ ≤B τ ) − Ptτ=1 I (V buyτ ≥A τ ). This
second constraint limits the maker’s exposure to the market throughout the learning process. To
summarize, the goal of the agent is not only to maximize the rewards attained during learning, but
also to simultaneously satisfy the aforementioned constraints. This makes the problem particularly
challenging, since the actions taken in the early rounds directly affect both the feasibility and the
profitability of the decisions taken later on.
We consider two feedback models, depending on the information revealed to the market maker at
the end of each round.
1. Undertwo-bit feedback, the maker only observes whether a trade occurred on the buy side and
on the sell side, namelyI(V buyt ≥A t) andI(V sellt ≤B t).
2. Differently, when access to thelimit order book(LOB) is available, the maker receives additional
information whenever no trade occurs. More precisely, if Bt < Vsellt , then the maker observes
V sellt , and if At > Vbuyt , then it observes V buyt . Thus, information is revealed exactly on those
sides where no reward (loss) is obtained. This captures a natural feature of market making:
when a trade occurs, takers have no incentive to reveal their true valuation, whereas when
no trade occurs, they may expose it by posting an order in the book. In this sense, LOB
feedback can be viewed as a realistic and intermediate regime between the two-bit model and
full-feedback, where the entire valuations are observed at the end of each round.

1.2 Related Work
In this section, we discuss the work most closely related to ours.
Market makingMarket making has also been studied from a mechanism-design perspective (e.g.,
see [MMR24]), where the mechanism must provide an optimal allocation and an incentive-compatible
payment rule, assuming access to both buyers’ and sellers’ valuation distributions. Our work is
mainly related to sequential market making [ AK13, XDX25], which studies the performance of a
regret-minimization algorithm against afiniteclass of constant-spread dynamic strategies. Our
results are not directly comparable to theirs, since we operate in a continuous bid–ask decision space
and do not assume full feedback. The work most closely related to ours is [ CBCC+25]. In this work,
the authors study an online market-making setting with asingleper-round taker in which the reward
attained by the market maker (the agent) is given by the difference between the bid (or ask) and the
unknown market value of the item. They provide optimal regret bounds under various assumptions
on the nature of the environment, assuming that the market maker observes the market value
and a one-bit feedback signal on the trade at the end of each round. While [ CBCC+25] provides
a foundational and technically very strong regret-minimization treatment of market making, our
setting captures two features that are central in practice and that substantially change the nature
of the problem. First, unlike [ CBCC+25], which assumes the existence of a market value relating
the rewards associated with sold items to those associated with purchased items, we explicitly
model inventory constraints. As a result, in our setting, actions taken in early rounds affect the
feasibility and profitability of the decisions in later rounds, posing additional and unique technical
challenges. Second, we study different feedback models. We consider two-bit feedback—which is
the natural minimal model in our setting, since buyers and sellers are treated separately, as in
real-world market making—and we also study a more realistic partial-information model motivated
by thelimit order book. In this sense, we believe that our framework is arguably closer to the
informational and operational constraints faced by actual market makers. Thelimit order book
feedback was originally introduced in [ MR26] and then further studied by [ VSM26], who consider
the market-value-based setting of [ CBCC+25] while allowing full feedback when no trade occurs.
Despite the shared motivation for the feedback model, the underlying setting differs substantially
from ours since our explicit inventory constraints make the feasibility and profitability of future
5
decisions depend on past trades.
Online learning in economic settings and bilateral tradeRegret minimization and online
learning methods have become a standard toolkit for studying digital markets and economic de-
sign under uncertainty, replacing strong prior assumptions with robust online guarantees. These
techniques have been applied to one-sided and dynamic pricing [ KL03, BDKS15, FKL+16], re-
peated auctions [ CGM15, CCC+24, MR15, WPR16, LST16, DS22, NCKP22, BG19], contract
design [ HSV16, DGSW23, ZBY+23], brokerage [ BCC24b], and Bayesian persuasion [ CCMG20,
CCMG23, ZIX21]. A particularly relevant related line of work is repeated bilateral trade, where a
learner acts as a broker and posts prices to agents with unknown private valuations, with the objective
of maximizing the social welfare. Foundational results established optimal regret guarantees under
i.i.d. assumptions, smoothness conditions, and budget-balance constraints [ CCC+21, CBCC+24].
Subsequent work extended these guarantees to adversarial environments, that is, beyond i.i.d.
assumptions, weaker budget-balance notions, and more general dependence structures between
buyer and seller valuations [ AFF22, CCC+23, BCCF24]. Recently, [ GDFS25] studied the problem
of revenue maximization in bilateral trade. Our work naturally fits within the online learning in
the economic category; nonetheless, we remark the following key distinctive features between our
setting and the aforementioned ones. On the one hand, our setting involves a two-sided interaction
with both a buyer and a seller, which distinguishes it from standard dynamic pricing or auction
settings. On the other hand, in market making, trades may occur in only one direction for extended
periods, until some inventory constraints are satisfied, while in bilateral trade, a trade may happen
in both directions only.
Online learning with general and budget constraintsA strongly related research direction
is online learning under unknown constraints, where the objective is to design online learning
algorithms that attain sublinear regret together with sublinear cumulative violation of some un-
known constraints [ MTY09, MYJ13, JHA16, YNW17]. A particularly influential framework in
this area is that of bandits with knapsacks (BwK), introduced by [ BKS18]. In BwK, the learner
observes bandit feedback and faces stochastic rewards and resource consumptions, with the goal of
maximizing cumulative utility while keeping the total consumption of each of the m resources within
a budget B. For the stochastic setting, improved and optimal algorithms were later developed
by [AD19]. The adversarial BwK version, that is, allowing the rewards and consumption to change
arbitrarily during the learning dynamic, was first investigated by [ ISSS22], who adopt as benchmark
the best fixed distribution over arms in hindsight. Under this benchmark, standard no-regret
guarantees are in general unattainable, and one must instead resort to weaker notions such as
α-regret. If ρ denotes the learner’s per-round budget, the best currently known competitive-ratio
guarantees are 1/ρ when B = Ω(T ) [CCK22], and O(logmlogT ) in the general case [ KS20]. More
recently, [KK22, BCC+24a] generalized the BwK model to allow non-monotone resource dynamics,
so that resources may also be replenished over time. Related in spirit, but closer to reinforcement
learning [SB18] formulations, is the literature on online learning in constrained Markov decision
processes (CMDPs). In CMDPs [ Alt99], the learner must optimize cumulative reward while enforc-
ing trajectory-level constraints in an environment with unknown state dynamics. A foundational
contribution in this direction is due to [ EMP20], who studied the exploration–exploitation trade-off
in unknown CMDPs and provided sublinear guarantees for both regret and constraint violation.
More recent works extended the model to settings with adversarial losses [ QWY+20] and adversarial
constraints [ SGG+24], while others investigated stronger safety notions that do not allow past
constraint satisfaction to compensate for future violations [MAC+24, SCMG25b]. Our setting shares
6
similarities with the online learning with general constraints (or even online CMDPs) framework and
the replenishable budget ones. Nonetheless, the aforementioned techniques cannot be transferred
directly to our problem, due to the presence of a continuous and structurally richer action space, as
well as a much weaker feedback model than the one usually assumed in bandit settings. Additionally,
we remark that our setting does not present any Slater-like condition for the offline optimum.
1.3 Technical Challenges
In this section, we discuss the main technical challenges of our setting. In particular, online market
making under inventory constraints presents several unique difficulties that make the learning
problem substantially challenging.
On the action space and the reward functionThe action space of bid-ask pairs available to
the agent during learning is continuous and two-dimensional, namely {(b, a) ∈ [0, 1]2 : b < a}.This
already rules out standard multi-armed bandit techniques [ LS20], which are inherently tailored to
discrete action spaces. At the same time, existing methods for continuum-armed bandits, such as
the Zooming algorithm [ KSU08, KSU19], do not apply directly to our setting for several reasons.
First, in two-dimensional problems, these methods typically yield regret bounds of order T 3/4,
which we show to be suboptimal in our setting. Second, our reward function is neither Lipschitz
nor smooth, since we impose no regularity assumptions on the c.d.f.’s of buyers and sellers. As
a result, standard discretization arguments or smoothing-based reductions cannot be applied in
any straightforward way to obtain sublinear regret. We acknowledge that related, but substantially
different, challenges also arise in no-regret algorithms for bilateral trade [ CBCC+21]. In such
settings, however, these challenges are typically overcome either by assuming regularity of the buyers’
and sellers’ c.d.f.’s [CBCC+21, CCC+23], or by leveraging the special structure of the bilateral trade
objective [BCCF24]. Intuitively, that structure makes discretization considerably less challenging
since trades can occur only when both sides simultaneously accept the proposed prices.
On the inventory constraintsThe inventory constraints play a crucial role in our setting.
Indeed, they clearly relate the bid and ask sides of the problem, preventing us from optimizing
them independently. More precisely, the bid-ask pair selected in earlier rounds directly affects the
inventory level carried into later ones, and therefore affects both the feasibility and the profitability
of future bid-ask pairs. More generally, while the literature on online learning under unknown
budget and general long-term constraints is huge [ BKS18, CCM+22], our inventory constraints
have a structure that does not allow for any direct application of existing techniques. First of all,
the overall structure of the problem (e.g.,action and rewards space) is already substantially more
involved than in standard constrained online learning models, thus preventing simple adaptation of
existing techniques from the literature. Second, our constraints do not fit the canonical templates
studied in the literature. On the one hand, theoverallinventory requirement is closely related to
replenishable-budget models [ KK22, BCC+24a], but with some additional challenges: indeed, i)
boththe initial budget and thefinalone must be 0 and ii) more importantly,no Slater’s condition is
available—that is, there is not a bid-ask pair whichstrictlysatisfies the offline expected constraint,
since it is an equality constraint, leaving no slack around the optimum. On the other hand, the
per-roundinventory constraints can be seen asany-timegeneral constraint [ CCM+22], where α is
the violation rate and Slater’s condition isnot available. Overall, these features make our setting
significantly different from the ones considered in the existing literature, and show that handling
the inventory constraints in our problem requires substantially different ideas.

Proving an upper bound for the following notion ofsurrogate regret, namely, RJT := T·J ∗ − PTt=1[At ·

solutions with strictly positive utility, that is, when the optimal safe solution 2 is simply to never

solution with strictly positive utility exists. To the best of our knowledge, this is a novel feature in
the constrained online learning literature and, more importantly, it requires substantially different
ideas, since one cannot simply aim to approach OPT directly, thus guaranteeing both safety and
optimality. Instead, it is necessary to satisfy the constraints while learning through a surrogate
objective based onJ, which may induce optimal bid-ask pairs that are not safe.
On the feedbackThe feedback structure is another major source of difficulty in our problem.
Under two-bit feedback, after posting a bid-ask pair, the learner only observes whether the seller
accepts the bid and whether the buyer accepts the ask, while the agents’ valuations remain unobserved.
Equivalently, on each side of the market, the learner only learns whether the corresponding valuation
lies above or below a posted threshold, rather than observing its actual value. This provides
extremely coarse information relative to the complexity of the decision space. The LOB model is
also non-trivial: whenever trade occurs on one side of the market, the learner only receives a one-bit
signal for that specific direction of trade. Thus, although this hybrid observation model is more
informative than purely two-bit feedback, it still reveals only partial information. Notably, despite
this limitation, we show that in the LOB setting, one can still attain the same optimal bounds as in
the full-feedback case. We also note that even the full-feedback setting, which is not the focus of
this work, is far from trivial and has been studied in several related settings [ CBCC+21, CBCC+24].
We believe that this makes our results for the LOB setting particularly interesting, as it shows that
the same optimal bounds can be achieved under weaker feedback.

2 Techniques and Proofs at a Glance
In this section, we describe the main ideas employed by our algorithms to address the several
technical challenges posed by our setting. The common difficulty is to learn profitable bid–ask pairs
while keeping purchases and sales balanced. Indeed, exploration itself generates trades, and a pair
with a large spread may lead to many more purchases than sales, or vice versa.
Our approach relies on thesurrogateobjective introduced above, which separates the value of a
pair from the imbalance it may generate. Writing F (b) := P(V sellt ≤b ) and S(a) := P(V buyt ≥a ) for
the two trade probabilities, recall that:
J(b, a) = (a−b) min{F(b), S(a)}, T J ∗ ≥OPT.
The minimum captures the trading rate that both sides can sustain after reducing activity on the
more frequent side. Thus, our algorithms aim to learn a nearly optimal pair for J and to control
inventory without losing the revenue associated with this common trading rate.
A first obstacle is the continuous action space. Since we impose no regularity assumptions on the
valuation distributions, rounding prices can substantially change the trade probabilities. Nevertheless,
the structure of J allows us to control the loss through a one-sided discretization. Specifically,
round a bid b upward to b′ and an ask a downward to a′ on a uniform grid of mesh 1 /N. Whenever
a−b >2/N, the rounded pair is feasible and monotonicity gives:

Two-Bit Feedback
Under two-bit feedback, each posted pair only reveals whether a purchase and a sale occurred.
Estimating F and S therefore requires repeatedly posting prices, which may itself create a large
imbalance. Our algorithm (Algorithm 1) addresses this through aphased eliminationprocedure
over bid and ask grids of size Θ( T 1/3). At phase ℓ, it maintains upper and lower confidence bounds
forFandS. These induce corresponding bounds forJ; for instance, the optimistic value is:
J upℓ (b, a) = (a−b) min{Fupℓ (b), Supℓ (a)}.
The pessimistic value J lowℓ is defined using the lower bounds. A feasible pair is calledcompatible
if its optimistic value reaches the largest pessimistic value among the active pairs. Prices with no
compatible partner are eliminated.
The key difficulty is to refine these bounds without continuing to trade on a side that is already too
active. To estimate a bid b, the algorithm pairs it with the compatible ask a′ having the largest
upper bound on S. It then runs the pair in a block, using a common target for the number of
purchases and sales, where the target is the optimistic estimate of the number of trades sustainable
on both sides, plus a confidence term. Once one side reaches this target, it is deactivated by bidding
0 or asking 1.
At first sight, halting seems to prevent accurate estimation. However, in a completed block, reaching
the common target guarantees that the halted side is not the one determining the minimum in J.
In particular, if the bid is halted, its true trade probability exceeds even the upper bound at a′.
Since a′ has the largest such bound among compatible asks, on the concentration event we obtain,
for every askastill compatible withb:
F(b)> S(a), J(b, a) = (a−b)S(a).
Further accuracy on F (b) is therefore unnecessary for evaluating any relevant pair. If the bid is not
halted, it is sampled throughout the block and its estimate is refined in the usual way. The ask side
is symmetric. This is what allows the confidence bounds on J to tighten even when one of the two
trade probabilities is not estimated accurately.
The same halting rule controls inventory and revenue. In a completed block of n rounds, both trade
counts are close to nmin{F (b), S(a′)}, up to eO(√n). Their difference controls the inventory change,
while the revenue is close to nJ(b, a′). Since compatible pairs are nearly optimal at the current
confidence, each completed block contributes at most eO(√n) to both virtual regret and imbalance.
It remains to control inventorywithina block, since purchases and sales may occur at different times
even if their final counts are close. For this reason, block lengths double across phases only up to
order T 2/3; estimation and elimination then continue with blocks of that length. The growing blocks

cost at most eO(T 1/3) per grid price, while the later blocks cost eO(T −1/3) per round. Summing
these two contributions gives order eO(T 2/3). The unfinished-block excursion and the discretization
error have the same order. Hence, with high probability, the algorithm guarantees:
RJT ≤ eO(T 2/3),maxt∈[T] |It| ≤eO(T 2/3).

optimal up to the confidence error.

such that P(V buyt = 1) = P(V sellt = 0) = 0. This assumption is natural, since the boundary prices
correspond to the case in which no buyer is willing to buy and no seller is willing to sell. Therefore,
bidding 0 and asking 1 correspond, from the agent’s perspective, to doing nothing. In this sense,
the assumption is always satisfied in practice.

3.1 Feedback Type
Throughout the paper, we will focus on different feedback types denoting the information the market
maker receives at the end of each round. Specifically, we can have one of the following feedback:
• Two-bit feedback (2-Bit).When only two-bit feedback is available, the agent observes
solely whether a trade has occurred. Specifically, at the end of round t∈ [T ], the agent
observes I (V buyt ≥A t) and I (V sellt ≤B t). This two-bit feedback model (and, similarly, its
one-bit counterpart) is predominant in revenue maximization settings [ KL03, CLM26] and
is generally regarded as the hardest form of feedback, in contrast to the easier full-feedback
setting, where the valuations are fully observed at the end of each round.
• Limit order book access (LOB).When access to the limit order book is available, at
the end of each round the agent observes additional information whenever no trade occurs.
Specifically, if Bt < Vsellt , the agent observes V sellt . Similarly, if At > Vbuyt , it observes V buyt .
In other words,information arrives exactly when reward does not. This assumption captures
a key feature of the market-making problem. Indeed, whenever a transaction occurs (either
on the buy side or on the sell side), the agent never observes the actual valuation, since
takers have no incentive to reveal it. By contrast, when no transaction takes place, takers are
incentivized to post their valuation in the order book, hoping that their order will be executed
in the near future. Notice that LOB feedback can be viewed as a meaningful hybrid feedback
model, interpolating between the two-bit and the full-feedback settings.

3.2 The Inventory Constraints
Market making differs from standard buy–sell interactions between agents. In particular, market
makers can sell an asset even if they have not bought it yet (this is the so-calledshort selling).
However, market makers usually try to keep theirinventorysmall, meaning they try to avoid
building a large imbalance between how many units they have sold and how many they have bought.
The reason is simple:
• if the maker ends up holding too much of the asset (positive inventory), they are exposed to
the risk that the market price goes down;
• if the maker ends up too short (negative inventory), they are exposed to the risk that the
market price goes up.
In both cases, the market maker becomes more sensitive to price movements, because part of their
profit (or loss) starts depending on the market valuation of the asset, not only on the trading activity

itself. This is typically against the idea of market making, which (in the simplest view) aims to
