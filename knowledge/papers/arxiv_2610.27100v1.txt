Abstract
Realistic market impact is essential for generative limit order book
(LOB) models to serve as world models for trading agents. If a sim-
ulator misprices this mechanism, agents learn policies that are opti-
mal in the simulator but wrong in the real market. Existing metrics
assess generated streams purely through distributional comparison
with historical data, leaving counterfactual response to an agent’s
own orders largely unexplored. Here, we show that market impact
emerges naturally in neural LOB generators trained solely for next-
token prediction. We inject execution-style metaorders into the gen-
erated message flow of five autoregressive models across five NAS-
DAQ stocks and measure the response using estimators from the
empirical impact literature, validated on baselines whose physics
is known in advance. Our benchmark evaluates any message-level
generator in three phases: free generation (reading order-flow per-
sistence against price diffusivity); metaorder injection (yielding
the impact law’s exponent and amplitude); and a post-execution
window (measuring relaxation). In addition, comparing each visible
injection to a blind, simulator-only control isolates the model’s

1 Introduction
Generative LOB models (GANs [10], recurrent and state-space mes-
sage generators [14, 26, 31, 33], transformers and diffusion [2, 18])
are judged today by how closely their message streams resemble
historical ones. Distributional benchmarks such as LOB-Bench [25]
make this comparison precise by scoring spread, volume, inter-
arrival, and imbalance distributions against real data. Such simu-
lators are candidates to serve as world models for reinforcement-
learning agents [12, 23], and an agent trained inside a simulator that
gets the response to its orders wrong will learn mispriced policies,
even if the surrounding stream looks realistic. Distributional scores
are blind to this response, and existing benchmarks measure it only
for a single event.
Constructing such a benchmark requires examining how real
markets absorb large trades. Institutions do not execute a large
order at once; they split it into small child orders. Empirical finance
provides the reference for what such a metaorder does to the price:
the square-root law of metaorder impact,

amplitude; the law is documented across equities, futures, and
FX [1, 6, 32]. This law is driven by two forces:persistenceand
resilience. Order flow is highly persistent: the sign of the next trade
is predictable from the previous one [19]. If every trade permanently
moved the price, this persistence would cause indefinite trends.
Instead, prices remain diffusive because the market absorbs and
relaxes from each push [7]. The law’s concavity emerges when the
liquidity a metaorder consumes is replenished behind it, and this
same replenishment brings the price back down once execution
stops [13]. In free generation, persistence is directly observable;
resilience appears only indirectly as a diffusive price.
Isolating resilience therefore needs a counterfactual: how prices
would have moved under an exogenous metaorder. A historical
trace cannot answer that; a generator can. LOB-Bench measures
this response at the scale of a single event. At the metaorder scale,
MarS reports a square-root-shaped response to injected TWAP
orders [18], and Bodor and Carlier [5] an exponent of0 .55on a
ten-child metaorder. However, these experiments do not separate
the mechanical impact, where the injected orders remove levels
of the price, from indirect impact through the reactions of other
participants to those orders. In a generator, other participants are
the model itself, and resilience lives in its reaction alone.
Our aim is to design experiments that measure these counter-
factuals and isolate indirect impact. In our procedure, metaorders
injected into five neural state-space generators produce an iden-
tifiable impact law on every stock we run:25model–stock pairs,
25identifications. We provide an automatic per-stock calibration;
every build-up injection is paired with a blind run that separates
the model’s reaction from the book’s mechanics, and a number is
reported only where a law is identified. This procedure extends dis-
tributional benchmarking, providing an instrument for evaluating
the interactive fidelity of LOB simulators.
2 Methodology
2.1 Benchmark design
The benchmark takes four inputs: the model under test, a stock, a
calibration computed from that stock’s own data (Section 2.3), and
a budget of𝑛 rollouts per direction per phase, spread over every
trading day of the month. It runs three phases with two paired
controls (Figure 1, left). Everything downstream is fixed before any
model is run: the injection schedule, calibration and estimator are
set by the stock and the protocol, never by the model under test.
Phase 1: free generation.The model runs freely over the injection
horizon (the length of one build-up metaorder: 12k–73k messages
depending on the stock, Table 2). For this phase, we measure the
following three observables. The first is a consistency pair:𝛾flow, the
decay exponent of the trade-sign autocorrelation (smaller means
longer memory), read against 𝐻, the Hurst exponent of the gen-
erated mid-price (𝐻= 0.5is diffusive). Persistent signs must be
compensated by transient impact, so a generator that combines
persistent flow with a super-diffusive price (𝐻> 0.5) has built a
free lunch into its own market [7]. The second is the free drift a
rollout accumulates with nothing injected. The third is the price
response to a single trade, against the real stream. Phase 1 serves
as a first filter: the replay passes it perfectly yet has no impact at
all, so establishing impact requires an intervention.
Phases 2 & 3: metaorder injection and relaxation.The injection
realises the counterfactual of Section 1: the same conditioning
windows, run again under an exogenous metaorder. After a condi-
tioning window from the real stream, we alternate one aggressive
child of median trade size with Δ𝑁 generated messages, the spac-
ing that holds participation at 𝜂= 10%(Section 2.3) (Figure 1,
right). Two shapes probe the two faces of impact. Thebuild-up
shape(𝑖=100,𝑐= 0) reads the impact exponent along a100-child
metaorder, a typical real length (Section 2.3). Therelaxationshape
(𝑖=10,𝑐= 100) executes a short metaorder and then stops injecting
for100silent blocks: ten execution durations, over which real im-
pact falls to about two-thirds of its peak within one to two durations
and to about one-half by the tenth [9, 24]. Every configuration runs
in both directions, and the two are combined as

2.2 Models under test
The benchmark accepts any message-level generator; Table 1 lists
the panel of models we test. The replay block spans no causal re-
sponse (Historic, the negative control), permanent impact (Heuris-
tic), and two kernels with the target physics written in: the Prop-
agator, whose power-law decay to 23 is the positive control, and
Obizhaeva–Wang (OW), with exponential resilience. The five neu-
ral generators carry a continuous hidden state across generated
and injected messages, the mechanism through which relaxation
could arise, and share one eight-name corpus including AMD, eval-
uation month held out. Book reconstruction and matching use the
JAX-LOB engine [12]; every number comes from the one campaign
per stock of Section 2.

3.1 Phase 1: Free generation
In identical windows, the real AMD stream is balanced: persistent
trade signs (𝛾flow = 1.66) with a diffusive mid-price (𝐻= 0.54). No
generator holds both legs. The Mamba family carries longer flow

is too diffusive (𝐻= 0.58for both): persistence left uncompensated.

flow memory (0.79) with a price below diffusive (𝐻= 0.49). Every
neural generator also drifts when left alone, from+9(S5-4k) to−251
(Mamba3-4k) ticks per rollout (Table 3). Every neural generator
therefore fails C1.

3.3 Phase 3: Relaxation
The bottom band asks whether impact comes down once execution
stops. Real metaorders fall to about two-thirds of peak within one to
two execution durations and to about one-half within ten [6, 9, 24],
the span of our window, so a flat curve cannot be blamed on it. The
kernel baselines set the scale: at realistic child spacing, a kernel
spends most of its transient between children, so its closed-form
end-over-peak is0.94for the Propagator and0 .87for OW, and the
measured values land beside these (0.89and0 .81on AMD, against
the permanent Heuristic’s0.95).
The neural panel shows no credible relaxation. End-over-peak
on AMD reads1 .19(S5-120M),1 .15(S5-4k),1 .16(Mamba3),1 .36
(Mamba3-4k), and0.88(GDN), with post-execution slopes of +0.6,
±0.0,+0.4,+3.7, and−2.4%of the peak per execution duration.
Four of the five remain above peak: the price keeps moving in the
metaorder’s direction after the last child has executed. GDN falls
below peak, but its free drift of−127ticks prevents attributing the
decline to resilience. That is claim C5, and no neural cell passes it.

4 Discussion
Our results separate three questions that distributional evaluation
conflates: whether impact exists, whether it is the generator’s own,
and whether its dynamics are market-realistic; across the neural
panel, the answer is yes, yes, and no. The joint C4/C5 failure is
consistent with insufficient replenishment: in a real book, each
child’s push decays before the next arrives, turning𝑁 pushes into√
𝑁 rather than 𝑁 . With persistent flow but insufficient replen-
ishment, impact instead builds up too steeply and fails to relax
credibly (Sections 3.1–3.3). The blind control shows that the build-
up is not explained by book mechanics alone; the defect hides at
the single-event scale. We attribute the missing replenishment to
limited long-horizon memory: relaxation requires retaining a refer-
ence price from before the metaorder, which the models’ still-short
context windows do not preserve.
These impact errors matter directly for execution: an execution
cost model depends on the shape, amplitude, and relaxation of
market impact [ 15, 23], all measured here per model and stock.
Today’s generators may achieve message-level realism, but they
remain unsuitable as environments for execution agents while the
endogenous impact they do produce has the wrong shape and does
not decay: such a simulator can reward schedules that a real book
would punish.
The results suggest how to improve these models. At each train-
ing checkpoint, Phase 1 can be run on freely generated rollouts: the
goal is for the generated𝛾flow and𝐻 to approach their empirical
values for the stock. The direction matters: learn replenishment
rather than unlearn persistence; killing flow memory would restore
the balance vacuously. Phases 2 and 3 then test whether the repair
restores realistic build-up and relaxation. The 4k-context variants
fail the same tests but remain well short of a metaorder, leaving the
attribution untested rather than refuted; longer contexts and larger
models are the natural next test.
Limitations.One stock is reported band by band, the other four
only through Table 3, and everything is measured at one operat-
ing point: one participation rate, one child size, aggressive chil-
dren [21, 30], one metaorder shape, one month of one exchange;
and𝛿 depends on that point. The10%target lies inside the retained
empirical samples [3, 34]; the extreme-volume regime [ 8] is not

5 Conclusion
Market impact emerges in generative LOB models. Five neural mes-
sage generators, trained only on next-token prediction, answer an
injected metaorder with an identifiable law in all 25 model–stock
pairs, while a replay that cannot react does not answer at all. The
models fail in two related ways: impact builds up more steeply than
the square-root reference and shows no credible post-execution
relaxation. Together, these failures point to insufficient replenish-
ment to compensate persistent order flow. We tentatively attribute
both to limited long-horizon memory, induced by still-short context
windows, a concrete target for future models.
