Neural Network Calibration of the Heston Stochastic
Volatility Model
 Yizhong Zhang
School of Mathematics, Southwestern University of Finance and Economics, Chengdu, China
gumuzhang0927@gmail.com
Abstract. The Heston stochastic volatility model remains a standard benchmark for equity-
option smiles, yet calibration is a nonlinear inverse problem shaped by pricing cost,
objective design, parameter constraints and the information contained in the observed option
grid. Neural approaches follow two main routes. Parameter-to-price (MtP) networks
approximate the forward pricing map and retain an optimiser, whereas price-to-parameter
(PtM) networks learn a direct inverse map and amortise calibration into a single inference
step. This review compares both routes with differential, physics-informed and hybrid
variants. PtM offers the clearest advantage in online latency, while MtP retains an explicit
calibration objective and more transparent residual diagnostics. Existing studies do not
establish a universal ranking in parameter stability, robustness or data efficiency, because
results remain sensitive to the training domain, sampling scheme, quote representation and
validation design. Both approaches can be trained on synthetic data, and neither resolves
weak parameter identifiability. For practical deployment, neural acceleration should be
combined with exact repricing, admissibility checks, uncertainty monitoring, distribution-
shift controls and numerical fallback. The most credible architecture is therefore determined
by the service requirement and the surrounding validation workflow rather than by the
direction of the learned map alone.
Keywords: Heston model, neural calibration, surrogate modelling, parameter identifiability,
model risk
1. Introduction
The Black–Scholes model assumes constant volatility and therefore cannot reproduce the strike- and
maturity-dependent smile and skew observed in equity-option markets. Heston introduced a mean-
reverting square-root variance process that generates stochastic volatility while preserving a
characteristic-function representation for European-option pricing [1]. Empirical comparisons show
that stochastic-volatility specifications improve on constant-volatility benchmarks, although no
single model eliminates specification risk [2]. Practical value therefore depends on whether Heston
parameters can be calibrated quickly, consistently and transparently.
Traditional calibration minimises price- or volatility-space errors using a numerical pricer and an
optimiser. Its practical speed depends heavily on implementation. Cui et al. show that analytical
gradients and careful handling of the characteristic function can make Heston calibration markedly

faster [3]. The more persistent concern is calibration risk: changes in the objective, weights, bounds
or quote set may produce a different parameter vector, even when the vanilla fit remains excellent

Neural calibration shifts part of this repeated work into training. Dimitroff et al. estimate Heston
parameters directly from implied-volatility surfaces [6]. Liu et al. instead place a neural pricer inside
a global search routine [7], while Horvath et al. develop the broader two-step surrogate approach [8].
Roeder and Dimitroff compare the direct and indirect maps on common synthetic data [9].
Subsequent studies extend the discussion to production validation, interpretability, differential
training, physics-informed operators, sampling design and no-arbitrage checks [10–15].
Three conclusions emerge. First, the clearest trade-off is between online latency and the visibility
of the calibration objective; it is not a simple choice between speed and stability. Second, reversing
the learned map does not remove weak identification in the Heston inverse problem. Third,
inference time is meaningful only when reported alongside exact repricing, admissibility checks,
uncertainty and controls for distribution shift.

Neither paradigm removes this information problem. MtP inherits flat regions from the forward
map and may add surrogate error. PtM must assign one label to surfaces that may be nearly
observationally equivalent, so its sampling distribution implicitly selects among plausible vectors.
Low parameter RMSE on synthetic data does not establish unique recovery from noisy markets; set-
valued, posterior or uncertainty outputs may therefore be more honest than a single precise vector.
Identifiability should be assessed locally and economically. Useful diagnostics include singular
values of the price Jacobian, profile objectives, repeated calibration from dispersed starting points
and perturbation tests around the observed surface. A parameter may be statistically unstable yet
economically unimportant if the relevant portfolio prices and hedges remain stable; the reverse can
also occur outside the calibration grid. Accordingly, parameter dispersion, in-sample fit and
downstream pricing error should be reported together rather than collapsed into one scalar score.
3. Neural calibration paradigms
3.1. MtP forward-surrogate calibration
MtP approximates the forward map from model and contract inputs to prices or implied volatilities.
Synthetic pairs are generated from a trusted Heston pricer over a chosen parameter domain, after
which the network replaces the native pricer inside an optimiser. Liu et al. combine this structure
with differential evolution [7], Horvath et al. formalise the two-step approach [8], and Amici et al.
add price derivatives to improve the surrogate gradient [12].
MtP's advantage is not uniqueness but a visible objective: bounds, quote weights, robust losses,
stopping rules and residuals remain inspectable. Calibration is still iterative, and accuracy is
controlled only within the training domain. Small pricing errors may distort gradients and the
optimum, so validation should test prices, sensitivities and calibrated residuals rather than average
prediction loss alone.
The forward-surrogate studies are not interchangeable. Liu et al. train an artificial neural network
on synthetic Heston and Bates prices, then use differential evolution to search the parameter domain
[7]. Their reported robustness therefore reflects both the learned pricer and the global optimiser.
Horvath et al. place greater emphasis on a reusable two-step architecture across stochastic and
rough-volatility models [8]. Taken together, the studies support MtP most strongly when the native
pricing engine is costly or must be called at scale. They offer less reason to replace a semi-closed-
form Heston implementation that is already fast and well controlled.
3.2. PtM direct-inverse calibration
PtM learns a direct map from an option-price or implied-volatility surface to Θ and produces a
vector in one forward pass. Dimitroff et al. apply convolutional networks to Heston surfaces [6]. On
common synthetic datasets, Roeder and Dimitroff report that their direct network outperforms the
two-step benchmark, although Heston remains harder than several rough-volatility cases [9]. That
result is a useful warning against assuming that PtM must always be less stable.
Like MtP, PtM can be trained on synthetic surfaces [6,9]. Its distinctive difficulty is label
ambiguity in weakly identified regions and the usual reliance on a fixed grid. Bounds can be

a residual is therefore a workflow failure, not an unavoidable property of direct networks.
The direct evidence also needs to be read in context. Dimitroff et al. use a convolutional structure
suited to regular implied-volatility grids, so performance depends on how the grid and parameter
labels are constructed [6]. Roeder and Dimitroff improve comparability by testing direct and indirect
methods on common synthetic datasets [9]. Their result favours the direct map in the reported
experiments, but the Heston case is less accurate than several rough-volatility examples and the
market-noise analysis is limited. The practical conclusion is narrower than a general ranking: a
direct network can work well when its deployment surface closely resembles the surfaces used for
training.
3.3. Differential, operator and hybrid methods
Recent work no longer fits neatly into a two-way classification. Amici et al. add price sensitivities to
forward-surrogate training [12]. DeepSVM learns a constrained Heston pricing operator from
physics-informed residuals [13], while Gutierrez shows that sampling design materially changes
inverse-map generalisation [14]. No-arbitrage penalties and derivative checks provide a further layer
of control [15]. These studies answer different questions, so their headline results should not be read
as a leaderboard.
Their contributions are best understood by the error they target. Differential training is intended
to improve the gradients used during calibration, although derivative quality can still deteriorate near
the boundary of the training range [12]. DeepSVM reduces dependence on labelled prices by
enforcing pricing residuals and hard constraints, but it remains a learned forward operator rather
than an end-to-end parameter estimator [13]. Hoshisashi et al. impose derivative-based no-arbitrage
conditions on volatility smiles and skews [15]. Such restrictions strengthen the surface layer; they do
not, by themselves, identify a unique Heston parameter vector.
A practical hybrid uses PtM for a warm start, exact repricing for acceptance, and an MtP or exact
pricer for refinement. This separates acceleration from validation and provides a clear control
sequence: reject inadmissible outputs, reprice accepted candidates, refine those above a residual
threshold and fall back to classical calibration when the input is outside the training domain. The
design is attractive because neural approximation need not determine final acceptance. However, it
adds operational complexity and duplicated validation, and the literature does not yet show that it
dominates production deployment.
4. Comparative evidence
The studies are easiest to compare when speed, total cost, fit, robustness and validation are
considered together. A fast inference result says little on its own if the candidate parameters must
often be rejected or recalibrated.
The published evidence is also heterogeneous. Some papers report parameter RMSE on
independently generated synthetic surfaces; others report price or implied-volatility errors, and
hardware descriptions vary in detail. A small error in one study cannot be placed directly beside a
millisecond runtime in another without recreating the parameter range, quote grid, numerical
tolerances and baseline implementation. The comparisons below therefore focus on what each
experiment establishes about the calibration workflow. Quantitative claims are treated as conditional
results, especially where the evidence comes from a working paper rather than a peer-reviewed or
market-tested study.

4.1. Efficiency and total computational cost
PtM has the clearest online-latency advantage because the optimiser is removed from the critical
path. The exact gain depends on input shape, batching and hardware. MtP still requires repeated
forward passes, although automatic differentiation and batch evaluation can make each pass
inexpensive [7, 12]. The relevant benchmark also matters: a well-implemented Heston calibration
with analytical gradients may already be quick [3].
Inference time is not total cost. Comparisons should include data generation, training,
revalidation, hardware, model-risk controls and fallback calibration. In an interest-rate setting,
Büchel et al. report roughly fourfold acceleration against an institutional benchmark with validation
controls [10]. Although not a Heston experiment, it illustrates the evidence needed for production
claims.
4.2. Accuracy, stability and robustness
Surface fit and parameter recovery are different targets. Synthetic data permit parameter error, but
market data contain no observable 'true' Heston vector. Market evaluation should therefore
emphasise reconstruction error, temporal stability and downstream pricing or hedging. Because
distant vectors can produce similar fits, studies should report both parameter- and surface-space
metrics whenever synthetic truth is available.
MtP exposes residuals directly; PtM obtains the same diagnostic through exact repricing. Neither
architecture guarantees parameter stability, which may arise from regularisation, warm starts,
restricted domains or averaging. The stability reported by Büchel et al [10]. reflects their complete
optimiser and validation design rather than a general theorem about MtP.
Neither direction is inherently robust to noise. MtP's outer least-squares objective can be
dominated by outlying quotes. PtM can be trained with noise, missing quotes and bid–ask
perturbations, but it fails when live surfaces leave that augmentation scheme. Robustness comes
from losses, weights, preprocessing and validation. The synthetic generalisation reported by Roeder
and Dimitroff [9] and the sampling sensitivity found by Gutierrez [14] are compatible once the data-
generating process is stated.
Regime change is an out-of-distribution problem for both routes. Useful controls include range
and distance-to-training checks, ensembles, exact repricing and a fallback optimiser.
4.3. Data, constraints and deployment
Both routes can use synthetic Heston evaluations. MtP must cover the parameter and contract
domain; PtM must additionally represent surface grids, missingness and inverse ambiguity. Space-
filling designs are useful in both [12, 14]. Historical surfaces remain important for realistic noise and
external validation, not because direct calibration requires historical labels.
Sampling is therefore part of the model specification rather than a neutral preprocessing step.
Uniform draws over broad parameter boxes may devote many observations to surfaces that are
economically unusual, while historical sampling can underrepresent stress regimes. Gutierrez shows
that changes in the sampling design materially affect inverse-map generalisation [14]. A defensible
training set should combine space-filling coverage with denser sampling near plausible market
regions, then reserve boundary and out-of-domain cases for testing. For PtM, repeated surfaces
generated by nearly equivalent parameter vectors are particularly informative because they expose

whether the network is learning identification or merely reproducing the chosen labelling

No-arbitrage operates at different layers. A network that outputs prices or implied volatilities may
violate strike or maturity conditions unless constrained [13,15]. PtM outputs parameters; if they are
admissible and repriced with the Heston model, resulting prices inherit the model's risk-neutral
structure. Its immediate risks are inadmissible parameters, poor reconstruction and model
misspecification rather than neural-surface arbitrage.
This distinction also changes the test suite. Forward surrogates should be checked for
monotonicity, convexity, calendar consistency and derivative accuracy across the training boundary.
Direct inverse models should be checked for parameter bounds, reconstruction error and stability
under small quote perturbations. In both cases, passing a penalty term during training is not
equivalent to a hard guarantee at deployment; exact post-inference tests remain necessary where
violations have financial consequences.
Design should follow the use case. MtP suits expensive native pricers, flexible quote sets and
visible objectives. PtM suits standardised grids and very frequent repeated calibration. Hybrids suit
rapid proposals screened by exact repricing. In risk-sensitive use, documented training ranges,
reproducible residuals, uncertainty and fallback procedures matter more than the label.
A practical selection decision should therefore begin with the service requirement rather than the
architecture. If calibration is occasional and the semi-closed-form pricer is already fast, neural
training may not repay its fixed cost. If thousands of standardised surfaces must be processed under
a tight latency budget, PtM's amortisation is compelling. When quote sets change frequently or
residual explainability is central to model governance, MtP is easier to defend. Hybrid deployment is
most credible when the acceptance threshold and fallback path are specified before model training.
5. Open challenges and research priorities
5.1. Benchmarks, identifiability and uncertainty
The field lacks a shared benchmark that varies parameter ranges, grids, noise, missingness and
regimes while holding evaluation fixed. It should report reconstruction and parameter error,
specified-hardware runtime, constraint violations, sensitivity quality and downstream pricing or
hedge error. Without this design, claims that one architecture is 'more accurate' remain
underdetermined.
Such a benchmark should publish training, interpolation and deliberately out-of-domain splits,
together with the exact pricing engine and numerical tolerances used to generate labels. Evaluation
should separate offline cost from online latency and include both a competitive analytical-gradient
calibration and a simple reproducible baseline. Releasing multiple random seeds and failed
calibrations is equally important: averages alone conceal tail failures that determine whether a
method is acceptable for risk systems.
A deterministic PtM output conceals inverse uncertainty. Probabilistic networks, ensembles or
Bayesian methods can represent plausible parameter sets; MtP can use profile objectives and
Hessian or Jacobian diagnostics. Any coverage claim must state its distributional assumptions and
connect uncertainty to repricing and risk-report consequences.

5.2. Constraints, monitoring and governance
Future work should separate hard admissibility, soft economic priors and surface-level no-arbitrage.
Box and Feller constraints operate on parameters; static-arbitrage conditions operate across strikes
and maturities; temporal smoothness operates across dates. Combining them in one penalty obscures
the guarantee achieved. Physics-informed and derivative-constrained methods are valuable because
they make selected restrictions explicit [13, 15].
Interpretability can identify influential strikes and maturities [11], but it complements rather than
replaces numerical validation. A production system should record the input surface, domain
diagnostics, predicted parameters, exact reconstruction error, constraint checks, uncertainty, model
version and fallback outcome. Defined performance deterioration should trigger retraining.
6. Conclusion
The literature does not support a simple verdict that one neural direction is both faster and more
stable. PtM removes online optimisation and is therefore the natural choice when latency is the
overriding constraint. MtP keeps the objective visible, which makes weights, bounds and refinement
easier to scrutinise. The remaining questions—identifiability, robustness and data efficiency—turn
mainly on the option grid, sampling design, loss and validation protocol.
For production use, the label attached to the network matters less than the surrounding controls. A
usable system must return an acceptable fit, respect parameter constraints, produce stable
downstream risk and fall back safely when the input leaves the training domain. On the present
evidence, neural proposals or surrogates are most persuasive when combined with exact repricing
and, where needed, numerical refinement. Shared Heston benchmarks would make that full-system
trade-off much easier to judge.
