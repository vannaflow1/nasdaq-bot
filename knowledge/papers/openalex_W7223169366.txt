Implementing BBNJ across
ﬁsheries institutions: policy
options for interoperable
ocean governance
Wanwoo Nam*
Department of Law, Jeonju University, Jeonju, Republic of Korea
The entry into force of the Agreement under the United Nations Convention on
the Law of the Sea on the Conservation and Sustainable Use of Marine Biological
Diversity of Areas beyond National Jurisdiction (BBNJ Agreement) creates a
practical implementation problem for ﬁsheries governance. Biodiversity informa-
tion, area-based management, environmental assessment, ﬁsheries authoriza-
tion, vessel identity, port measures, maritime safety and market controls are
governed by institutions that were not designed as a single compliance system.
This Policy and Practice Review uses a structured qualitative institutional appraisal
to compare four governance architectures —autonomous implementation, cen-
tralized integration, sectoral liaison, and federated interoperability with phased
implementation. The method speci ﬁes a source hierarchy, four units of compar-
ison, six pre-de ﬁned criteria, and an evidence-to- ﬁnding protocol that distin-
guishes existing-authority ﬁt, conditional adaptation and structural constraint.
Each ﬁnding is tied to treaty text, of ﬁcial institutional material and relevant peer-
reviewed scholarship available through 31 August 2026. The legal foundation for
cross-regime coexistence is anchored in BBNJ Agreement articles 5(2), 22, 29, 47
(6)(c) and 51, which combine non-undermining, cooperation and coordination,
respect for institutional competences, and links to existing information mecha-
nisms. The comparison does not claim to predict governmental policy choices or
empirically prove an optimal design. Instead, it identi ﬁes institutionally workable
options under stated legal and administrative constraints. On that basis, federated
interoperability is carried forward as a conditional policy proposition for staged
testing, subject to provenance controls, veri ﬁcation and legal assessment by the
competent authorities, cybersecurity, capacity support and review. The article
proposes a three-stage pathway —pre-COP mapping, ﬁrst-COP institutionaliza-
tion, and post-COP pilots and review —linking BBNJ bodies, RFMOs, FAO/PSMA
mechanisms, IMO-related systems and national administrations.

1 Introduction
Illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) ﬁshing is often
described as a problem of enforcement intensity. That description
is incomplete. The international system already contains a dense set
of legal and administrative tools: ﬂag-State duties under the United
Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS; United
Nations, 1982 ), cooperation and compliance arrangements under
the 1995 United Nations Fish Stocks Agreement (UNFSA; United
Nations, 1995 ), regional ﬁsheries management organizations
(RFMOs), the FAO International Plan of Action on IUU Fishing
(Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, 2001 ),
the Agreement on Port State Measures (PSMA; Food and
Agriculture Organizatio n of the United Nations, 2009 ), catch
documentation and market controls, and a growing set of maritime
safety and identity mechanisms. The persistent dif ﬁculty is that
these tools are distributed across institutions, agencies, databases,
and decision points. A vessel can move faster than the information
needed to determine who it is, what it is authorized to do, whether
its conduct con ﬂicts with a conservation measure, and which
authority can lawfully respond. The persistence and transnational
complexity of IUU ﬁshing, including the dif ﬁculty of converting
fragmented information into effective control, are well documented
in the wider literature ( Agnew et al., 2009 ; Stefanus and
Vervaele, 2021 ).
The BBNJ Agreement, which entered into force on 17 January
2026, adds another consequential layer to this institutional land-
scape. It establishes procedures and institutions for marine genetic
resources, area-based management tools (ABMTs), environmental
impact assessments (EIAs), capac ity-building and transfer of
marine technology, and a Clearing-House Mechanism (CHM).
The legal basis for institutional coexistence is explicit. Article 5
(2) requires the Agreement to be interpreted and applied in a
manner that does not undermine relevant legal instruments and
frameworks or relevant global, regional, subregional and sectoral
bodies, while promoting coherence and coordination with them.
Article 22 applies that logic to ABMTs by requiring the Conference
of the Parties (COP) to respect the competences of relevant bodies
and by permitting compatible measures and recommendations
within their respective mandates. Article 22 (4) goes further by
allowing the COP, where required for the objectives and imple-
mentation of Part III and subject to paragraphs 1 and 2, to consider
and, as appropriate, decide to develop a mechanism regarding
existing ABMTs, including marine protected areas, adopted under
relevant legal instruments and frameworks or by relevant global,
regional, subregional or sectoral bodies. Article 29 provides speci ﬁc
rules for interaction with EIA processes under other relevant
regimes, while article 51(3)(c) –(d) directs the CHM to provide
links to relevant clearing-house mechanisms, repositories and
databases and when establishing regional and subregional mecha-
nisms under the global mechanism, to build on global, regional and
subregional clearing-house institutions where applicable ( United
Nations, 2023 ). Institutional coexistence is therefore not merely a
policy preference; it is part of the Agreement ’sl e g a ld e s i g n
(Bodansky, 2024 ; Langlet and Vadrot, 2023 ).
The implementation question is therefore more speci ﬁc than
whether international institutions should simply ‘cooperate.’ The
practical challenge is how to connect biodiversity information,
ﬁsheries measures, vessel identity, port action and regulatory
feedback in a way that preserves institutional competence. Recent
scholarship has examined BBNJ–RFMO interaction, ﬁsheries under
the BBNJ regime, the CHM, transnational maritime environmental
crime and implementation of the PSMA ( Qu and Liu, 2022 ;
Sebuliba et al., 2025 ; Tian and Guo, 2025 ; Gaebel et al., 2025 ;
Lothian, 2024; Stefanus and Vervaele, 2021 ; Wang and Xue, 2026 ).
Within Frontiers in Marine Science, Miao (2026) develops institu-
tional complementarity for ABMT networks, Jiang et al. (2026)
analyze BBNJ–ISA disconnection in EIA implementation, Yuan and
Zhang (2026) review PSMA enforcement, and Wu (2026) examines
governance pathways for high-seas MPAs. These studies establish
the importance of cross-institutional implementation but address
particular instruments, sectors or conservation tools.
The distinct question addressed here is comparative and archi-
tectural: which institutional arrangement can most effectively con-
nect BBNJ implementation with ﬁsheries and maritime-
administration systems while preserving legal competence, account-
ability, information integrity and equitable participation, and under
what conditions? The article compares four governance architec-
tures spanning BBNJ bodies, RFMOs, ﬂag and port States, FAO/
PSMA mechanisms, IMO-related systems and national administra-
tions. The research question is deliberately neutral as to centraliza-
tion: centralized integration is treated as a genuine design option
and evaluated on the same pre-de ﬁned criteria as the other
three architectures.
This article addresses that question as a marine policy problem.
Rather than presupposing a preferred architecture, it compares four
institutional design options and evaluates them through a trans-
parent evidence-to-assessment protocol. The six criteria cover legal
compatibility, ecological decision-support effectiveness, administra-
tive feasibility, information governance, distributional equity and
adaptability. The subsequent policy recommendation is derived
only after the options have been assessed against those criteria
and against a non-compensatory legal-competence threshold. In the
federated model examined here, institutions retain their own
mandates and legal tests while using limited interfaces —common
identiﬁers, minimum data ﬁelds, referral protocols, provenance
rules and feedback arrangements. The objective is not to make
every institution see or control everything, but to determine
whether relevant information can reach the authority legally com-
petent to assess it in time to matter.
The argument contributes to the Marine Affairs and Policy
literature in three ways. First, it treats interoperability as a problem
of ocean governance rather than a purely technical exercise in
information sharing. Second, it distinguishes legal authority from
informational connectivity: stronger connectivity does not require
stronger centralization. Third, it integrates distributional equity into
institutional design by treating capacity-building as part of system
architecture, not as an afterthought. These points are directly
relevant to SDG 14 on conservation and sustainable use of oceans
and to SDG 17 on partnerships, because institutional coherence is a

2 Review design and evidence-to-
assessment protocol
2.1 Review design and source hierarchy
This is a Policy and Practice Review based on doctrinal legal
analysis and structured institutional comparison rather than a
systematic review or an empirical evaluation of enforcement out-
comes. The documentary cutoff is 31 August 2026. Evidence was
organized through a source hierarchy. First, treaty texts and of ﬁcial
United Nations, FAO, IMO and institutional documents were used
to establish legal mandates, formal procedures, treaty status and the
existence or planned operation of information systems. Second,
implementation materials were used to identify operational prac-
tices, data ﬂows and administrative constraints. Third, peer-re-
viewed scholarship was used to in terpret regime interaction,
institutional fragmentation, ﬁsheries governance, transparency,
distributional effects and policy feasibility. Primary sources control
where a secondary source and an of ﬁcial text diverge. The review
does not use publication counts as evidence of policy performance
and does not claim exhaustive literature coverage.
Documents and studies were included when they bore directly
on at least one element of the comparison: allocation of legal
competence; BBNJ –RFMO or BBNJ –sectoral interaction; ABMT
or EIA coordination; PSMA or ﬁsheries information exchange;
vessel identity or maritime-administration interfaces; information
governance; capacity asymmetry; or adaptive implementation. This
purposive inclusion rule is appropriate to a Policy and Practice
Review, but the scope of inference is correspondingly bounded: the
analysis evaluates institutional design plausibility and does not
estimate causal effects on IUU ﬁshing, biodiversity outcomes or
State behavior.
2.2 Units of analysis and construction of
the four options
The unit of comparison is the governance architecture rather
than an individual State, RFMO or enforcement event. Four ideal-
type architectures are speci ﬁed before the criteria are applied.
Option 1, autonomous implementation, leaves each regime to
develop its own information and compliance processes with min-
imal formal connection. Option 2, centralized integration, consol-
idates cross-regime data and decision support in a common global
platform or central coordinating authority. For clarity, Option 2
does not refer to the Article 51 Clearing-House Mechanism (CHM)
as such. Article 51 establishes a centralized platform for BBNJ
functions while also providing for links to relevant global, regional,
subregional and sectoral clearing-house mechanisms, repositories
and databases and, where possible, the use of existing institutions.
Option 2 instead denotes a broader cross-regime architecture that
would consolidate external ﬁsheries, vessel, port and compliance
information, and potentially decision-support functions, within a
common system. Because the BBNJ Agreement does not itself create
a general cross-regime ﬁsheries authority, Option 2 is treated as a
hypothetical design that would require a governance allocation
identifying who administers the platform, what decisions it may
support, and how authority is divided between the platform and
existing competent bodies ( United Nations, 2023 ). Option 3,
sectoral liaison, preserves separate institutions but relies on mem-
oranda of understanding, focal points, recurring consultation and
bilateral or sector-speci ﬁc exchange. Option 4, federated interop-
erability with phased implementation, preserves separate systems
and legal mandates while introducing shared identi ﬁers, minimum
metadata, structured referrals and staged technical standards.
The four options are ideal types used to expose trade-offs. Real
arrangements may combine them. In particular, a federated system
is likely to depend on sectoral liaison agreements for its legal and
administrative interfaces, while even a centralized platform would
still depend on competent authorities for legally consequential
decisions. The comparison therefore evaluates the institutional
logic of each option rather than assuming that any architecture
would exist in a pure form.
2.3 Selection and operationalization of the
six criteria
The six criteria were selected because cross-regime interopera-
bility must satisfy six distinct governance requirements that cannot
be reduced to technical ef ﬁciency alone. Legal compatibility tests
whether an architecture respects treaty-based allocations of author-
ity and the BBNJ non-undermini ng rule. Ecological decision-
support effectiveness asks whether biodiversity, spatial and impact
information can reach competent management bodies in time to
inform prioritization or management; it does not purport to
measure ecological outcomes. Administrative feasibility addresses
the degree of institutional change, duplication and recurring coor-
dination burden. Information governance covers provenance,
access control, con ﬁdentiality, correction, cybersecurity and the
risk that information is detached from its evidentiary status.
Distributional equity asks whet her lower-capacity States and
small-scale ﬁsheries can participate without disproportionate tech-
nological or compliance burdens. Adaptability addresses modular-
ity, versioning, feedback and the ability to revise interfaces as law,
science and institutional practice change. These dimensions re ﬂect
concerns identi ﬁed in the ﬁsheries-governance and regulatory-
pluralism literature, including t ransparency and accountability
(Davis and Hanich, 2022 ), institutional complexity ( Lindley and
Techera, 2017), distributional effects ( Song et al., 2020 ; Alonso and
Marschke, 2023 ), and BBNJ regime interaction ( Langlet and
Vadrot, 2023; Tian and Guo, 2025 ). Table 1 deﬁnes the observable
basis and decision rule for each criterion.

qualitative constrained optimization
The appraisal uses a two-stage evidence-to-assessment protocol.
In the ﬁrst stage, each option is examined against each criterion and

the relevant legal or institutional basis is recorded in an option-by-
criterion evidence matrix. A ﬁnding is not entered solely because
the author considers an option desirable or undesirable; it must be
connected to an observable institutional feature and to an identi-
ﬁable source category, with primary legal or institutional material
used whenever the proposition concerns authority, procedure or

The decision logic is non-compensatory rather than additive.
No numerical weights are assigned and a favorable ﬁnding on one
criterion cannot offset a structural conﬂict with legal competence or
accountability. An architecture is carried forward for policy devel-
opment only if it does not depend on a transfer of legal authority
inconsistent with the Agreement ’s coexistence rules and if its
remaining administrative, informational and distributional condi-
tions are capable of being speci ﬁed and tested. ‘Constrained
optimization ’ is therefore used in a qualitative policy-design
sense: it means identifying a comparatively workable arrangement
under binding legal and institutional constraints, not mathemati-
cally maximizing a score or claiming a scienti ﬁcally proven opti-
mum. An option is carried forward to the implementation-design
stage only when three threshold conditions are met: (1) it avoids a
structural con ﬂict with the allocation of legal competence; (2) it
provides a routine cross-regime pathway by which relevant infor-
mation can reach the legally competent body, rather than relying
solely on ad hoc contacts; and (3) it permits incremental and
reversible implementation without requiring wholesale replacement
of existing institutional systems. This rule also distinguishes Option

3 The coordination problem after
BBNJ entry into force
The BBNJ Agreement alters the information environment of
ocean governance. ABMT processes can generate spatially speci ﬁc
conservation objectives, scienti ﬁc advice, monitoring information,
and review material. EIAs can generate baseline and cumulative-
impact information. The CHM is designed to facilitate access to
information and opportunities for cooperation, while capacity-
building and technology-transfer provisions can expand the ability
of Parties to participate in implementation. Yet none of these
functions automatically converts biodiversity information into a
ﬁsheries restriction, a vessel inspection, or a sanction. Those
decisions remain governed by other legal regimes and domestic law.
This separation is normatively important. The BBNJ
Agreement’s relationship with competent bodies is not a technical
inconvenience to be engineered away; it re ﬂects negotiated bound-
aries of authority. Article 5 (2) establishes the general rule of non-
undermining and coherence, article 8 requires cooperation and
encourages Parties to promote the Agreement ’s objectives in other
relevant bodies, article 22 protects the competences of other bodies
in ABMT decision-making, article 29 structures interaction with
other EIA processes, and article 47 (6) (c) directs the COP to
promote cooperation and coordination through appropriate pro-
cesses ( United Nations, 2023 ; Scanlon, 2018 ). RFMOs possess
ﬁsheries expertise and adopt measures for speci ﬁed stocks and
areas. Flag States authorize and regulate vessels. Port States act
under the PSMA and domestic law. Market States may apply catch
documentation, import controls or due-diligence requirements.
IMO instruments address vessel identi ﬁcation, safety, training and
other maritime matters rather than ﬁsheries legality. Effective
implementation must therefore distinguish between information
that can be shared and legal conclusions that cannot simply be
transferred from one regime to another.
3.1 The 2026 –2027 implementation
window
At the documentary cutoff of 31 August 2026, the institutional
setting was transitional. DOALOS is carrying out secretariat functions
under the Agreement until the treaty secretariat commences its
functions. United Nations Noti ﬁcations 2026 –001 and 2026 –002
further conﬁrm that, until the CHM becomes operational, noti ﬁca-
tions and information required under relevant provisions of the
Agreement are to be transmitted to DOALOS for circulation and
publication as appropriate ( United Nations, 2026b , 2026c). Near-
term interoperability proposals must therefore work through interim
procedures rather than assume the existence of a mature CHM.
The Preparatory Commission reported that the ﬁrst meeting of
the COP is to be convened at United Nations Headquarters from 11
to 22 January 2027 ( United Nations, 2026a). It also prepared a draft
decision for that meeting on arrangements to enhance cooperation
with relevant legal instruments, frameworks and global, regional,
subregional and sectoral bodies, explicitly recognizing such coop-
eration as crucial to effective implementation ( United Nations,
2026d). This creates a concrete policy window: interface mapping
and data-inventory work can occur before the ﬁrst COP; the COP
can establish cooperation, provenance and mandate-protection
principles; and technical pilots can follow as the secretariat, CHM
and subsidiary arrangements become operational.

The second is institutional fragmentation. Information is held

Auld et al. (2025) show that regional cooperation against IUU

general need for cooperation into repeatable procedures rather than
relying on personal contacts or exceptional cases.
The third is temporal fragmentation. Ecological risk, ﬁshing
activity, port arrival, landing, market entry and enforcement review
occur at different points in time. By the time veri ﬁed information
reaches a downstream authority, the vessel or catch may have
moved. The PSMA partially addresses this problem by requiring
advance information and enabling port-based action. FAO ’s Global
Information Exchange System (GIES) extends that logic by sup-
porting the exchange of advance requests for port entry (ARPEs),
inspection information and relevant port-State reports. Its public
dashboard expressly presents cumulative ARPE and inspection
submissions since operationalization, demonstrating that special-
ized global compliance-information infrastructure already exists
and can be reused rather than duplicated ( Food and Agriculture
Organization of the United Nations, 2026a ). The policy question is
how to connect such ﬁsheries information systems to wider envi-

either as legally dispositive of the other.
Maritime safety instruments create a related but distinct inter-
face. The revised STCW-F Convention and mandatory STCW-F
Code entered into force on 1 January 2026, and the 2012 Cape
Town Agreement is scheduled to enter into force on 24 February
2027 after its entry-into-force conditions were met in February 2026
(International Maritime Organization, 2026a , 2026b). Together,
these instruments strengthen personnel-certi ﬁcation and ﬁshing-
vessel safety inspection frameworks and may improve the reliability
of certi ﬁcation and inspection information. They do not, however,
transform safety de ﬁciencies into evidence of IUU ﬁshing. A sound
policy model should permit veri ﬁed factual information to be
referred across agencies without collapsing the legal tests of ﬁsher-
ies, safety, labor, customs, or environmental law.
Capacity asymmetry cuts across all three forms of fragmenta-
tion. High-capacity States can combine satellite data, vessel mon-
itoring systems, electronic reporting, port inspection, corporate
information, and specialized le gal expertise. Many developing
States cannot. Song et al. (2020) and Alonso and Marschke
(2023) also show that anti-IUU policies can impose collateral
burdens on small-scale ﬁshers and workers when regulatory
design assumes industrial-scal e data and compliance capacity.
Interoperability that requires expensive digital infrastructure or
universal data production can therefore widen governance
inequality. BBNJ capacity-building provisions are relevant not
only to scienti ﬁc participation but also to the legitimacy of any
information architecture that links biodiversity governance with
ﬁsheries administration.
4 Policy options and implications
The four options differ in how they allocate connectivity,
administration and decision authority. The comparison below
does not treat the ﬁnding categories as scores and does not infer
actual policy performance from institutional design alone. Instead,
the text explains the source-linked reasons for each ﬁnding in
Table 2 and identi ﬁes which conditions would have to be satis ﬁed
before an option could be implemented or scaled.
4.1 Option 1: autonomous implementation
Under autonomous implement ation, BBNJ institutions,
RFMOs, PSMA mechanisms, IMO systems, and national agencies
continue to develop largely independent information processes.
Coordination occurs when a speci ﬁc treaty or domestic rule
requires noti ﬁcation or when of ﬁcials make case-by-case contact.
This option has the advantage of legal clarity. Each institution
controls its own information and decisions, and the risk of juris-
dictional overreach is low.
The weakness is that autonomy reproduces the very fragmen-
tation that reduces the value of new biodiversity information.
Separate systems can create multiple versions of vessel identity,
inconsistent spatial references, duplicated reporting, and delayed
referrals. The approach also increases the burden on lower-capacity
administrations because of ﬁcials must manually translate data
among systems. Autonomous implementation is therefore legally
conservative but administratively burdensome and limited in its
capacity to connect ecological information to downstream decision
points. It is best understood as the baseline against which more
connected models should be assessed, not as a desirable end state.
4.2 Option 2: centralized integration
Centralized integration seeks to solve fragmentation by creating
a common global platform, conso lidated database, or central
coordinating authority that combines biodiversity, ﬁsheries,
vessel, port and compliance information. This analytical option is
broader than the Article 51 CHM: it concerns consolidation of
information and potentially deci sion-support functions across
BBNJ and external sectoral systems, whereas the CHM can also
operate as a linking and information-access mechanism without
absorbing the mandates or databases of competent ﬁsheries and
maritime bodies. For analytical purposes, this option assumes that
such a platform would require an identi ﬁed global administrator —
potentially a new entity, a COP-authorized mechanism, or an inter-
institutional arrangement—and a rule specifying what functions are
merely informational and what functions, if any, can affect deci-
sions. That governance allocation is not supplied automatically by
Nam 10.3389/fmars.2026.1986237
Frontiers in Marine Science frontiersin.org08
the BBNJ Agreement. Technica lly, a common platform could
standardize identi ﬁers, reduce duplication and support cross-
regime analytics, but its institutional feasibility depends on whether
it remains a service layer or accumulates decision authority that
existing treaties assign elsewhere.
The principal constraints are therefore legal and institutional
rather than purely technical. First, centralization risks confusing
information aggregation with legal competence. Article 5(2) and the
more speci ﬁc competence rules in articles 22 and 29 point toward
coordination with existing bodies rather than an implied transfer of
their mandates ( United Nations, 2023 ). Second, data concentration
increases con ﬁdentiality, cybersecurity and access-control conse-
quences. The GIES itself differentiates access because ﬁsheries forms
can contain personal, compliance and commercial information.
Third, a central platform would require rules for administration,
correction, liability, funding and dispute over data quality. Fourth, a
new global data infrastructure could duplicate specialized systems
already performing de ﬁned functions. Centralized integration is
therefore not excluded a priori , but under the present legal archi-
tecture it faces a structural competence problem if it is designed as a
decision authority and signi ﬁcant conditional requirements even if
limited to information services.

institutional agreements
A third option is to preserve institutional separation while
expanding coordination through memoranda of understanding,
focal points, joint working groups, and recurring consultation.

4.4 Option 4: federated interoperability
with phased implementation
Federated interoperability combines institutional autonomy
with standardized interfaces. Each institution retains its own
database, mandate, access rules, and legal tests. Connectivity is
created through a limited set of common components: persistent
vessel identi ﬁers where available; shared geographic references;
minimum metadata describing source, time, con ﬁdence, and legal
status; standardized referral categories; machine-readable or other-
wise structured exchange formats; and explicit rules identifying the
authority responsible for veri ﬁcation and action. Under this model,
the Article 51 CHM can function as a gateway and linking layer for
BBNJ-related information while specialized ﬁsheries, vessel, port
and maritime systems remain under their respective competent
institutions. The model therefore resembles a federation of special-
ized systems rather than a single cross-regime platform.
The legal advantage is that the model separates ‘can be informed
by’ from ‘is bound by. ’ BBNJ information can inform ecological
prioritization without determiningﬁsheries illegality. RFMO compli-
ance ﬁndings can inform port risk assessment without automatically
dictating a domestic sanction. A maritime-safety inspection can
conﬁrm identity or certi ﬁcation facts without becoming proof of a
ﬁsheries violation. This architecture operationalizes the idea that facts
can be shared while legal judgments remain regime-speciﬁc.
The administrative advantage is incremental implementation.
Institutions do not need to replace existing systems. They can begin
with a small number of high-value interfaces, such as vessel identity,
spatial boundaries, designated contacts, and port-referral metadata.
More advanced functions —automated exchange, risk analytics, or
cross-regime dashboards —can be added later where capacity and
legal safeguards permit. This is particularly important because

The equity advantage is that minimum interoperability can be

Operationally, Option 4 requires a thin common governance
layer rather than a new institutional hierarchy. Subject to COP
decisions and the mandates of existing bodies, common rules would
deﬁne the minimum metadata, referral categories, versioning con-
ventions and access conditions needed for systems to interoperate.
The authoritative record would remain with the institution that
created or legally maintains it. The CHM or another agreed
interface could support discovery and routing, while the originating
institution would remain responsible for source integrity and access
status and the receiving competent body would remain responsible
for veriﬁcation and any legal consequence. Recurring inter-institu-
tional consultation would be used to resolve interface problems,
correction requests and changes in technical standards. This allo-
cation of functions is central to the federated model because it
creates a traceable cross-regime pathway without relocating sub-
stantive decision authority.
Article 22(4) provides a concrete use case for this architecture.
Article 22(4) provides that, where the achievement of the objectives
and the implementation of Part III so requires, the COP may
consider and, subject to Article 22(1) and (2), may decide, as
appropriate, to develop a mechanism regarding existing ABMTs,
including marine protected areas, adopted under relevant legal
instruments and frameworks or by relevant global, regional, subre-
gional or sectoral bodies. The provision does not prescribe the
institutional form of that mechanism. Within a federated model, it
could therefore be designed as a coordination protocol rather than a
new regulatory authority. Illustratively, one possible design could
perform the following functions: index an existing ABMT with a
link to its authoritative source; record its geographic scope, objec-
tives, legal status, effective dates and current version; identify the
adopting body and designated contact point; establish a consulta-
tion and referral process where an existing measure overlaps with,
complements or creates an apparent gap in relation to a BBNJ
measure; route scienti ﬁc or technical questions to the relevant
competent body and, where appropriate under the Agreement, to
BBNJ scienti ﬁc processes; and record subsequent amendments,
reviews or withdrawals so that outdated spatial information is not
propagated across systems. The mechanism proposed here would
not automatically incorporate an existing ABMT into BBNJ law,
alter its legal effect, or transfer ﬁsheries-management competence to
the COP. Its function would be to make existing measures discov-
erable, comparable and consultable across regimes while preserving
the authority of the institution that adopted them.
This Article 22(4) mechanism also illustrates why phased
implementation is important. A ﬁrst stage could establish a limited
registry-and-linkage function using authoritative source links, min-
imum spatial metadata and institutional contact points. A second
stage could pilot consultation and referral work ﬂows with a small
number of willing RFMOs or other relevant bodies, including
procedures for correcting outdated boundaries and clarifying the
status of overlapping measures. A review stage could then assess
metadata accuracy, response time, duplicate reporting, unresolved
competence questions and user uptake before any broader stan-
dardization. Expansion would occur only where the pilot demon-
strates that the mechanism improves visibility and coordination
without creating legal ambiguity, excessive reporting burdens or a
de facto transfer of decision authority.
Applying the decision rule in Section 2.4, centralized integration
is not carried forward as the default architecture because a central
decision authority would con ﬂict with the distributed allocation of
competence, while a purely informational central platform would
still require substantial governance and data-concentration safe-
guards. Autonomous implementation preserves competence but
fails to provide a dependable routine cross-regime pathway.
Sectoral liaison remains legally useful and is retained as an enabling
layer, but its bilateral or sector-speci ﬁc structure does not by itself
satisfy the scalability and routine-pathway threshold. Federated
interoperability is therefore carried forward for policy development
not because it receives an aggregate score, but because it can satisfy
the three threshold conditions: preservation of distributed compe-
tence, establishment of a routine cross-regime information path-
way, and incremental and reversible implementation. This remains
a conditional institutional inference, not an empirical ﬁnding that
the model will outperform alternatives in practice; the roadmap in
Table 3 is designed to generate the implementation evidence needed
for later validation.
5 From information sharing to
governance infrastructure
The phrase ‘information sharing ’ is too broad to guide imple-
mentation. In practice, institutions need to know what information
is being shared, in what format, with what provenance, for what
purpose, under whose authority, and with what consequences.
Federated interoperability therefore requires governance rules as
much as technical standards.
The ﬁrst building block is reference integrity. Cross-regime
governance depends on stable references for the objects, measures
and institutions to which information relates. For vessels, a persis-
tent identiﬁer—such as the IMO Ship Identi ﬁcation Number where
applicable—can reduce fragmentation across changes of name, ﬂag
or operator, while FAO, RFMO and national identi ﬁers remain
necessary supplementary ﬁelds. The same principle applies to
spatial measures: an ABMT, ﬁsheries closure or management rule
should be linked to an authoritative source and version. The policy
objective is not to create a universal identi ﬁer for every regulatory
Nam 10.3389/fmars.2026.1986237
Frontiers in Marine Science frontiersin.org11
object, but to ensure that different institutions can determine
whether they are referring to the same vessel, area, measure
or decision.
The second building block is spatial interoperability. BBNJ
ABMTs and EIA information are spatially structured, while ﬁsher-
ies measures may also de ﬁne closed areas, seasonal restrictions, or
management zones. These systems need common geospatial refer-
ences and version control. A risk alert is only useful if the receiving
authority can determine which measure applied at the relevant time
and whether the vessel activity occurred inside the relevant area.
Spatial metadata should therefore identify the source, legal or policy
status, effective dates, and version of the boundary or measure.
The third building block is provenance. Not all data have the
same evidentiary status. Satellite detections, AIS anomalies, ob-
server reports, inspection ﬁndings, RFMO listings, self-reported
catch data, and scientiﬁc model outputs differ in reliability and legal
relevance. Interoperability should make these differences visible
rather than ﬂatten them into a single risk score. Minimum metadata
should identify the source, timestamp, method of collection, con-
ﬁdence or veri ﬁcation status where appropriate, and whether the
information is public, restricted, or legally sensitive.
The fourth building block is a referral taxonomy. A receiving
institution needs to know why information is being sent and what, if
anything, is being requested of it. A limited set of categories —
ecological priority, identity inconsistency, authorization concern,
possible interaction with a spat ial measure, safety relevance,
market-traceability concern or request for veri ﬁcation—can make
handoffs repeatable across regimes. Importantly, the referral should
distinguish a request to verify from a conclusion that a violation
occurred. This preserves the difference between informational
connectivity and legal authority and reduces the risk that probabi-
listic or context-dependent information migrates across institutions
as adjudicative fact.
The ﬁfth building block is a feedback loop. Ocean governance
should not end when a vessel is inspected or a port decision is made.
Veriﬁed ﬁndings can inform future RFMO compliance review,
BBNJ monitoring of environmental measures, national risk assess-
ment, and capacity planning. Feedback can also reveal false posi-
tives, gaps in spatial rules, inconsistent vessel records, or burdens on
compliant ﬁshers. This is where interoperability supports ecosys-
tem-based management: enforcement information becomes part of
learning about whether conservation measures are implementable
and effective, rather than remaining isolated within case ﬁles.
6 Distributional and regional
implications
A global interoperability policy must accommodate regional
diversity. The Asia-Paci ﬁc is used here as an illustrative stress test
rather than as a representative case or causal comparison. The
region was selected for analytical, not statistical, reasons: it com-
bines large distant-water ﬂeets, archipelagic and small-island juris-
dictions, multiple RFMO and subregional arrangements, major
seafood markets, wide variation i n administrative and digital
capacity, and active regional monitoring, control and surveillance
cooperation (Fujii et al., 2021 ). These characteristics place simulta-
neous pressure on all six criteria —especially competence, informa-
tion exchange, feasibility and distributional equity —and therefore
provide a demanding setting in which to examine whether an
interoperability design remains plausible under institutional het-
erogeneity. The region also illustrates the tension between stronger
control and distributional effects. Regulatory reforms can improve
transparency and reduce IUU activity, but compliance costs and
surveillance practices can affect migrant workers, small-scale ﬁshers
and communities whose ﬁshing practices do not ﬁt industrial
monitoring models ( Alonso and Marschke, 2023 ). No claim is
made that the region is globally representative or that observed
regional outcomes validate the proposed interoperabil-
ity architecture.
