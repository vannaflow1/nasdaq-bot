Abstract
Retirement portfolios under regular withdrawals face sequence-of-returns risk:
early losses can exhaust capital even when average returns are adequate. We
examine whether regime-switching dynamic asset allocation mitigates this risk
for a 60/40 stock-bond portfolio, and whether deep learning identiﬁes regimes
better than classical econometrics. Five models (a Markov-switching model, a
multivariate and a univariate Gaussian hidden Markov model, an LSTM, and an
encoder-only Transformer) are compared under identical data, tuning, and walk-
forward validation. Their signals, net of transaction costs, feed 10,000 stationary-
bootstrap retirement paths in three withdrawal scenarios. At 4.0% the MSM, the
univariate HMM, and the LSTM cut the depletion rate from 3.30% to 0.57%,
0.60%, and 0.60% with disjoint Wilson intervals; at 6.0% only the LSTM reduces
it (17.01% against 33.04%), the univariate ﬁlters raising it to about 49% as
their return sacriﬁce becomes the dominant ruin cause, and the multivariate
HMM raising it in every scenario. Only the econometric models turn defensive
in all ﬁve crisis windows; neither network identiﬁes regimes more accurately,
and the LSTM holds the highest median terminal capital by staying invested
rather than by timing regimes. Classiﬁcation quality and search diagnostics do
not predict decumulation value. Regime-switching allocation in decumulation is
a state-contingent tail hedge: model choice determines the protected risk proﬁle,
and above a critical withdrawal rate the premium exceeds the payout.

1 Introduction
Pay-as-you-go pension systems in aging societies face sustained demographic pressure,
and a growing share of retirement income must be ﬁnanced from individually accu-
mulated capital (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2024).
Market risk thereby shifts into the decumulation phase, in which households withdraw
from a portfolio instead of contributing to it. Under regular withdrawals, portfolio
outcomes become path dependent: two return sequences with an identical mean can
produce sharply diﬀerent terminal wealth and ruin probabilities, because losses real-
ized early in retirement interact with continued withdrawals and permanently shrink
the capital base from which any recovery must compound (Pfau, 2013; Soni, 2024).
This sequence-of-returns risk (SORR) is invisible to mean-variance summary statistics
and only partially addressed by withdrawal-side rules such as the 4% rule of Bengen
(1994). On the allocation side, the 60/40 stock-bond portfolio is the conventional refer-
ence allocation for retirement decumulation. Its risk containment, however, rests on a
negative stock-bond correlation that is itself regime dependent: during the 2022 mon-
etary tightening both asset classes lost value simultaneously, and the diversiﬁcation
underlying the 60/40 design failed precisely when it was needed (ˇSiki´c, 2025).
A static allocation cannot respond to such state changes by construction; it holds
the same risk exposure in a calm bull market and in the early stage of a drawdown
that may be irreversible for a decumulation portfolio. Regime-switching models oﬀer
a data-driven alternative. Following Hamilton (1989), returns can be described as
realizations of a small number of latent market regimes, and regime inference matters
for portfolio choice (Ang & Bekaert, 2004). Regime-switching dynamic asset allocation
(DAA) operationalizes this idea: a model estimates the probability of a bear regime,
and the portfolio moves into a risk-free asset whenever this probability exceeds a
threshold. Economically, such a strategy acts as insurance against bear phases arriving
early in retirement, paying a premium in transaction costs and missed recoveries in
exchange for a thinner ruin tail.
Whether this insurance works, and which model class provides it best, sits at the
intersection of three literatures that rarely meet. The SORR literature concentrates
on withdrawal rules and partial annuitization while keeping the allocation essentially
static (Bengen, 1994; Clare et al., 2021; Soni, 2024). The regime-based allocation
literature documents drawdown reduction from regime and trend signals (Li & Mulvey,
2021; Shu et al., 2024), and the deep learning literature nonlinear forecasting gains
(Fischer & Krauss, 2018; Taneva-Angelova & Granchev, 2025), but both evaluate on
single historical backtests, without withdrawal dynamics or ruin measures. To our
knowledge, no study has compared econometric and deep learning regime models under
identical conditions using decumulation-native outcome metrics.
2
This paper addresses that gap along two research questions. RQ1: Does regime-
switching DAA reduce the ruin probability of a 60/40 portfolio in the decumulation
phase? RQ2: Do deep learning models identify regime switches more accurately than
classical econometric models, and does higher classiﬁcation quality translate into better
decumulation outcomes? Ruin is measured by the depletion rate, the share of simulated
paths on which capital is exhausted, with maximum drawdown (MDD) as a secondary
metric.
We compare ﬁve regime models under fully identical conditions: a Markov-
switching model (MSM), a multivariate Gaussian hidden Markov model (HMM), a
univariate HMM included as an ablation, a long short-term memory network (LSTM)
(Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997), and an encoder-only Transformer (Vaswani et
al., 2017), the latter two trained on Pagan–Sossounov bull/bear labels (Pagan &
Sossounov, 2003). All models emit daily bear-regime probabilities for a 60/40 S&P
500/long-term Treasury portfolio over 1990 to 2026 and are validated in a walk-forward
design (10-year training and 12-month test windows; 26 folds spanning 25.7 out-of-
sample years), with hyperparameters tuned on development folds only. The resulting
allocation signals, net of transaction costs, feed a retirement simulation of 10,000
stationary-bootstrap return paths (Politis & Romano, 1994) in each of three with-
drawal scenarios, evaluated with Wilson conﬁdence intervals on depletion rates and
paired Wilcoxon tests on path-wise outcomes.
Three ﬁndings emerge. First, regime-switching DAA reduces ruin risk condition-
ally on the withdrawal rate rather than on the model class: at 4.0% the univariate
econometric ﬁlters cut the benchmark’s depletion rate by a factor of ﬁve to six, at
6.0% they raise it by about half, as their return sacriﬁce becomes the dominant ruin
cause. Second, the two networks separate: the LSTM lowers the depletion rate in all
three scenarios and holds the highest median terminal capital, but by staying invested
rather than by timing regimes, whereas the Transformer reacts late or not at all in the
recent crisis windows and exceeds the benchmark’s depletion rate at the two higher
rates. Third, classiﬁcation quality and search diagnostics do not predict decumulation
value: the model with the ﬁeld’s best-equal precision-recall ranking and its best search
diagnostics ranks second worst on depletion. Regime-switching DAA is therefore a
state-contingent tail hedge bounded by the withdrawal rate.
The paper makes four contributions. First, it provides the ﬁrst systematic compar-
ison of econometric and deep learning regime models in a decumulation setting under
identical conditions. Second, it delivers distribution-level evidence, based on 10,000
bootstrap paths per scenario with formal inference, for the withdrawal-rate dependence
of the tail-versus-median asymmetry of regime-based allocation. Third, the univariate
HMM ablation disentangles architecture eﬀects from feature-set eﬀects and overturns
the intuition that richer multivariate inputs improve regime detection; it also shows
the two univariate speciﬁcations to be one piece of evidence rather than two, agreeing
on 99.75% of out-of-sample days. Fourth, the complete pipeline, from data ingestion
to evaluation, is released as an open-source artifact for replication and extension.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work
on regime identiﬁcation, deep learning for ﬁnancial time series, and SORR. Section 3
describes the data, the ﬁve models, the walk-forward and tuning protocol, and the

retirement simulation. Section 4 reports classiﬁcation quality, crisis-window perfor-

implications, and limitations, and Section 6 concludes.
2 Related Work
Econometric regime models form the established reference paradigm for regime-based
asset allocation. Building on the Markov-switching framework of Hamilton (1989) and
the hidden Markov model with its standard estimation machinery (Rabiner, 1989), a
substantial body of work shows that latent bull and bear states can be estimated reli-
ably enough to yield economically exploitable allocation signals (Arimond et al., 2026;
Fons et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020). Collin-Dufresne et al. (2020) ﬁnd that actively
timing volatility and liquidity regimes dominates static strategies, and Erlwein-Sayer
et al. (2020) show that ﬁlter-based HMM portfolios can beat buy-and-hold bench-
marks when time-varying correlations are modeled explicitly. Closest to our allocation
design, Shu et al. (2024) report a reduction of the maximum drawdown from 55%
to 27% relative to a passive benchmark using regime signals from a statistical jump
model. The limits of the paradigm are equally well documented: parametric distribu-
tional assumptions, delayed reactions to abrupt nonlinear breaks (Agal et al., 2025;
Jung et al., 2022), and unstable transition-probability estimates in turbulent phases
(Dua & Tuteja, 2021). Throughout this literature, performance is measured with
accumulation-oriented metrics on single historical backtests.
The deep learning literature follows a trajectory from recurrent to attention-based
architectures. LSTM networks established the case for deep learning in ﬁnancial pre-
diction (Fischer & Krauss, 2018), and recent variants combine them with diﬀerentiable
risk-budgeting layers, reporting drawdown reductions of around 40% against classi-
cal methods (Agal et al., 2025). Comparative evidence is mixed, however: Taguchi
et al. (2022) ﬁnd that econometric regime models capture market structure better
while recurrent networks predict returns more accurately. Transformer architectures
are the current focus. Taneva-Angelova and Granchev (2025) report the lowest
volatility-forecasting errors and the strongest generalization across structural breaks
for Transformer models, and Song et al. (2025) ﬁnd that an adaptive Transformer
framework outperforms ARIMA and GARCH benchmarks by a wide margin. Two
caveats recur: deep models risk memorizing historical idiosyncrasies rather than gen-
eralizable regime structure (Kumar et al., 2026; Rahaman, 2026), and results are
sensitive to hyperparameter choices (Agal et al., 2025). A smaller hybrid strand com-
bines both paradigms, for example recurrent networks that learn no-trade zones around
HMM allocations (Li & Mulvey, 2021), HMM-based allocation within a generative
forecasting framework (Oliveira & Costa, 2025), and jump models combined with
gradient boosting for crash protection (Shu et al., 2025). These are isolated model
combinations, though: systematic comparisons of the econometric and deep learning
paradigms under identical data, labels, and backtest protocols remain rare, and none
of the cited studies evaluates regime signals in a decumulation setting.
The SORR literature, by contrast, engages directly with the decumulation problem
introduced in Section 1 but treats the allocation as essentially given. Its instruments
4
are withdrawal rules and insurance products: revisions of the 4% rule showing that
static withdrawal rates raise ruin risk under changed market conditions (Leggio et al.,
2024), dynamic withdrawal schemes that match observed retiree behavior (Kieren &
Weber, 2022), and partial annuitization as a buﬀer against early losses (Soni, 2024).
Clare et al. (2021) are an exception on the allocation side, showing that trend-following
ﬁlters reduce drawdowns and stabilize sustainable withdrawal rates, extended to the
full life cycle in Clare et al. (2025). Common to these approaches is that they respond
to SORR through time (glide paths), rules (withdrawal rates), or products (annuities),
without using the current market state as an input. Jung et al. (2022) provide the
only regime-based exception, an HMM framework for surplus optimization in occupa-
tional pension plans, which targets institutional funding ratios rather than household
ruin risk. Model-based regime identiﬁcation combined with withdrawal dynamics and
formal inference on ruin probabilities is, to our knowledge, absent from this strand.
A ﬁnal issue cuts across the supervised branch of the comparison: bull and
bear regimes have no unique ground truth. Rule-based dating algorithms such as
Pagan and Sossounov (2003), which adapt the Bry-Boschan business-cycle dating
(Bry & Boschan, 1971) to ﬁnancial prices, impose minimum phase lengths and
amplitudes; threshold-based criteria such as Lunde and Timmermann (2004) trigger
regime changes on cumulated returns; business-cycle chronologies like the NBER’s
date the real economy rather than the market, and even their real-time reproduction
is nontrivial (Chauvet & Piger, 2008; Harding & Pagan, 2002). The choice among
these constructs determines which crises exist in a supervised training signal at all;
minimum-duration ﬁlters, in particular the four-month minimum phase used below,
can exclude short, V-shaped crashes by construction. This construct dependence is
directly relevant for the deep learning results reported in Section 4.
Relative to these strands, the present study diﬀers in three respects. First, it com-
pares ﬁve regime models spanning both paradigms under fully identical conditions:
the same data, the same label deﬁnition for the supervised models, the same walk-
forward protocol, tuning budget discipline, and cost assumptions. Second, it evaluates
the resulting strategies with decumulation-native metrics, at the level of the outcome
distribution and with formal inference, rather than with accumulation metrics on the
single realized history. Third, it links classiﬁcation quality, signal dynamics, and port-
folio outcomes within one design, including an ablation that separates architecture
eﬀects from feature-set eﬀects. The study thereby connects the regime-allocation, deep
learning, and SORR strands whose intersection the literature has left unoccupied.
3 Data and Methodology
3.1 Data
The empirical basis consists of daily adjusted closing prices from Yahoo Finance,
retrieved through the yfinance interface, version 1.5.1 (Aroussi, 2026), and covering
January 2, 1990 to July 31, 2026, or 9,211 trading days after cleaning. The universe is
restricted to the two instruments of the benchmark introduced in Section 1, the S&P
500 index (ˆGSPC) for equity and the Vanguard Long-Term Treasury Fund (VUSTX)
for long-duration bonds, with the 13-week Treasury bill rate (ˆIRX) as the risk-free
5
rate and the return on cash, and the CBOE Volatility Index (ˆVIX) and the 10-
year Treasury yield (ˆTNX) entering the feature set only. The feature frame supplied
to the models begins on October 16, 1990 and holds 9,012 rows, because the 200-
day moving average consumes the earliest observations. The sample spans multiple
complete market cycles, including the ﬁve crisis windows of Section 3.5; the two-asset
design isolates the eﬀect of the regime signal on the allocation decision.
Daily log returns are computed on adjusted closes, rt = ln(At/At−1). The bench-
mark return is r60/40 t = 0.6 rGSPC t + 0.4 rVUSTX t with constant weights, and the cash
position accrues at rcash t = ln (1 + IRXt/(100 · 252)). From these series, six model fea-
tures are derived: the 60/40 log returns, a 10-day rolling mean return as momentum,
the normalized distance to the 200-day simple moving average, the 20-day rolling
standard deviation of returns, the VIX, and the term spread, deﬁned as the 10-year
minus the 3-month yield. Pairwise Pearson correlations stay below |r| = 0.6 for all
feature pairs except realized versus implied volatility (r = 0.80), and the term spread
is nearly orthogonal to all other features (maximum |r| = 0.14). The implications of
the collinear volatility block for the multivariate HMM are taken up in Section 4.

3.2 Regime identiﬁcation models
All ﬁve models solve the same task: at each trading day t, they map the informa-
tion available up to t to a probability pt = P (St = bear | Ft) for a binary regime
variable St ∈ {bull, bear}. The two-regime space is deliberate. It matches the binary
risk-on/risk-oﬀ character of the allocation decision in decumulation and avoids the
fragmentation of ﬁner regime partitions. Each probability stream is converted into an
allocation signal by a model-speciﬁc threshold θ: the portfolio switches to the defen-
sive position when pt ≥ θ. The threshold is treated as a hyperparameter and tuned
jointly with each model (Section 3.3) rather than ﬁxed at 0.5, which places models
with diﬀerently calibrated probability scales on an equal footing.
The Markov-switching model follows Hamilton (1989) as a two-state speciﬁcation
with switching mean and switching variance, estimated on the univariate 60/40 return
series:
r60/40 t = µSt + εt, εt ∼ N ( 0, σ2 St ) , P (St = j | St−1 = i) = pij . (1)
The switching variance is essential given the volatility clustering documented in
Section 3.1. Within each training window, the bear regime is identiﬁed as the state
with the larger estimated variance, using training parameters only. Out-of-sample
probabilities are ﬁltered probabilities P (St | r1, . . . , rt) from the forward recursion;
smoothed probabilities are never used, so the signal at day t contains no look-ahead.

multivariate observation vector with Gaussian emissions (Rabiner, 1989):

The two deep learning models are trained as supervised classiﬁers on rule-based
bull/bear labels generated by the Pagan and Sossounov (2003) dating algorithm from
the S&P 500 price path (8-month turning-point window, 4-month minimum phase, 16-
month minimum cycle, with the standard 20% amplitude override). Pagan–Sossounov
is an ex-post algorithm whose centered window revises recent labels once later prices
arrive. Labels are therefore recomputed at each fold cutoﬀ from the history avail-
able through it and aligned to the training window, so no test-period price enters a
training target; this changes 628 fold-training rows relative to one label series com-
puted on the full sample. Six candidate schemes (MSM- and HMM-implied states,
Pagan–Sossounov, peak-to-trough, Lunde–Timmermann, and the NBER chronology)
were compared ex post on the full sample, as a descriptive diagnostic rather than as
the training construction. The three rule-based schemes agree closely with each other
and with the NBER chronology (Pagan–Sossounov κ = 0.56 on 91% of days, peak-
to-trough 0.57 and 91%, Lunde–Timmermann 0.50 and 90%), so the choice within
this near-congruent block does not drive the outcome; Pagan–Sossounov was taken
from it as the established market-dating algorithm with explicit duration and ampli-
tude criteria. HMM-implied labels were rejected for fragmentation (64%, κ = 0.21);
Appendix B reports the full matrix. Both networks receive the same six features of
Section 3.1, scaled by median and interquartile range ﬁtted on the respective train-
ing window only; the sequence length is a tuned hyperparameter and diﬀers between
them (100 trading days for the LSTM, 220 for the Transformer). Both are trained
with a class-weighted binary cross-entropy whose positive-class weight

The LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) serves as the recurrent baseline,
in the role it has established in ﬁnancial prediction (Fischer & Krauss, 2018): two
stacked LSTM layers of 32 and 256 units with dropout of 0.25, followed by a dense
sigmoid head. The Transformer implements an encoder-only architecture (Vaswani et
al., 2017): a linear projection of the six-dimensional input into an embedding space of
dimension 128, sinusoidal positional encoding, four encoder layers with eight attention
heads and a feed-forward width of 32, dropout of 0.05, and a linear head on the
representation of the ﬁnal time step. All architecture sizes, window lengths, learning
rates, and thresholds are outcomes of the hyperparameter optimization described in
Section 3.3; search spaces and ﬁnal conﬁgurations are reported in Appendix A.
3.3 Allocation rule, backtesting, and hyperparameter
optimization
The allocation rule is binary. On a defensive signal (pt ≥ θ), the portfolio holds 100%
cash accruing rcash t ; otherwise it holds the full 60/40 mix. Signals are executed with a
delay of one trading day, so the position on day t+1 reﬂects only information available
at t. Each switch incurs an assumed transaction cost of 10 basis points of portfolio
value; the sensitivity of all results to this parameter is examined over a range from
8
0 to 200 basis points in Section 4.4. Out-of-sample evaluation follows a rolling walk-
forward protocol (Figure 1): a 10-year training window, the subsequent 12 months as
the test window, and a 12-month step, yielding 26 non-overlapping test windows from
October 2000 to July 2026 (6,484 trading days, 25.7 years). All ﬁve models, including
their labels, scalers, and bear-state identiﬁcation, are re-estimated in every fold on
the respective training window (Section 3.2). The deep learning models additionally
warm-start from the previous fold’s weights for ten epochs and average the predicted
probabilities of ﬁve independently seeded trainings per fold, which dampens retraining
variance; each fold then trains under a model-speciﬁc epoch budget (30 for the LSTM,
50 for the Transformer) with early stopping on validation loss at a patience of ﬁve.

Hyperparameters are tuned with Optuna (Akiba et al., 2019) in an inner cross-
validation that reuses the exact fold generator of the ﬁnal evaluation. The fold
structure makes one departure from common practice necessary: 20 of the 26 test win-
dows contain no bear-market day at all and only two a complete Pagan–Sossounov bear
phase, so a fold-wise median of any metric is nearly blind to crisis behavior. Each trial
therefore concatenates the daily net out-of-sample returns of its folds into one pooled
series and maximizes its Martin ratio, the compound annual growth rate divided by
the Ulcer index (Martin & McCann, 1989). The concatenation is chronologically con-
tinuous: the lagged signal, the portfolio position, and the transaction cost are carried
across fold boundaries, so the pooled evaluation is identical to a single backtest on
the concatenated out-of-sample signal, veriﬁed as bit-identical for all ﬁve models. The
objective builds its equity path as the exponential of the cumulative pooled log returns,
so neither the growth rate nor the Ulcer index inherits a volatility drag from the return
convention. Because the Ulcer index integrates both depth and duration of drawdowns,
9
the rare crisis segments drive the selection. The low-dimensional econometric spaces
(the signal threshold, plus the covariance parametrization for the multivariate HMM)
are searched exhaustively on a grid of 36, 108, and 36 conﬁgurations, so the selected
conﬁgurations are global optima within their grids. The seven- and eight-dimensional
deep learning spaces are searched with a multivariate tree-structured Parzen estimator
under a ﬁxed seed, with 300 trials for the LSTM and 400 for the Transformer, of which
the ﬁrst 50 and 60 are random exploration. Trial pruning is disabled: a median pruner
would eliminate crisis-protective conﬁgurations on the chronologically early, mostly
bullish folds. The LSTM study completed 300 trials and the Transformer 400, none
pruned. The search observes only folds whose test window ends by December 2016,
a development period that includes the dot-com and global ﬁnancial crises, while the
ﬁnal walk-forward run applies the frozen conﬁgurations to all 26 folds. The COVID
crash and the 2022 rate shock therefore remain selection-free and test generalization
to structurally diﬀerent crises.
The search is accompanied by four validity diagnostics: a convergence review
combining fANOVA hyperparameter importances with an edge-of-range check, with
bounds widened and re-run wherever an optimum sat on a boundary; an objective-
sensitivity analysis across the candidate metrics (Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar, Ulcer,
maximum drawdown); deﬂated Sharpe ratios (Bailey & L´opez de Prado, 2014)
together with the probability of backtest overﬁtting, estimated by combinatorially
symmetric cross-validation (Bailey et al., 2017); and a multi-seed re-evaluation of the
top deep learning conﬁgurations. Their outcomes are reported alongside the robustness
results in Section 4.4; search spaces and ﬁnal conﬁgurations are listed in Appendix A.
3.4 Retirement simulation
The walk-forward backtest yields one realized out-of-sample path per strategy. For
SORR, this single path is insuﬃcient: ruin is a tail event whose probability cannot be
inferred from one realization. For each strategy, the 25.7-year series of daily net out-of-
sample returns (transaction costs included) and the accompanying regime signals are
resampled jointly with the stationary bootstrap of Politis and Romano (1994), which
concatenates blocks of geometrically distributed length with a mean of 20 trading days.
Blocks of on average roughly one calendar month preserve the short-range autocor-
relation and the volatility clustering documented in Section 3.1 while still producing
diverse multi-decade histories, and the joint resampling of returns and signals keeps
the two aligned, which the withdrawal mechanics below require. Per strategy and sce-
nario, 10,000 paths of 7,560 trading days (30 years at 252 trading days) are drawn. All
scenario-strategy combinations share one common random stream (common random
numbers), which underpins the paired path-wise comparisons of Section 3.5.
Each path evolves a capital account. Capital compounds with the resampled daily
return, and every 21 trading days a ﬁxed nominal withdrawal is deducted. An assumed
liquidity fee of 0.1% applies to withdrawals taken while the portfolio is invested, reﬂect-
ing the liquidation of fund shares. Capital is absorbing at zero: once exhausted, a path
remains ruined. The three scenarios in Table 2 span the relevant risk space. Standard
anchors on the 4.0% withdrawal rate of the safe-withdrawal-rate literature; Aggressive
10
keeps the same capital at a stressed 6.0% rate; Low Capital combines a reduced capi-
tal base with an intermediate 5.0% rate. The 30-year horizon is the standard of that
literature (Bengen, 1994; Cooley et al., 1998), which keeps the 4.0% anchor directly
comparable and makes ruin observable at plausible withdrawal rates rather than only
at an overstretched one. The withdrawals are deliberately non-adaptive: ﬁxed nominal
amounts isolate the eﬀect of the allocation from the withdrawal-rule eﬀects studied in
the literature of Section 2, and the sensitivity to the withdrawal rate itself is examined
in Section 4.4.

The primary outcome of the simulation is the depletion rate, the share of the
10,000 paths on which capital is exhausted before the end of the 30-year horizon.
It operationalizes the ruin probability of RQ1 at the level of the outcome distribu-
tion rather than of the single historical realization, and it is the object on which the
inference of Section 3.5 operates. The second outcome, the path-wise maximum draw-
down, is measured on the capital path after withdrawals. A depleted path therefore
registers −100% by construction, and the higher the withdrawal rate, the more the
mechanical outﬂow dominates the market component. This decumulation drawdown
is not a pure market-risk statistic, which is why it can rank strategies diﬀerently from
the maximum drawdown of the realized backtest path in Section 4.1. As a robustness
check, the entire simulation is repeated with the ﬁxed-length moving block bootstrap
of K¨unsch (1989), which draws blocks of constant length of 20 trading days. Across
the 18 scenario-strategy cells, the depletion rate changes by at most 3.88 percentage
points between the two resampling schemes, the sign of every regime model’s depletion
diﬀerence against buy-and-hold is preserved in all 15 model-scenario comparisons, and
the ranking by depletion rate is identical in two of the three scenarios; the stationary
bootstrap remains the primary speciﬁcation.
3.5 Evaluation framework
The two research questions of Section 1 are formalized as one-sided hypotheses at a
signiﬁcance level of α = 0.05. Both are evaluated in all three withdrawal scenarios and
reported in full, with the Aggressive scenario retained as the pre-registered headline
cell, because the scenario grid spans the range over which the ﬁnding changes sign. H1
operationalizes RQ1: regime-switching DAA reduces SORR relative to the buy-and-
hold benchmark. It is evaluated on two levels. At the tail, depletion rates are compared
11
through 95% Wilson conﬁdence intervals (Wilson, 1927), which remain calibrated for
proportions near zero where Wald intervals degenerate; disjoint intervals serve as a
conservative suﬃcient criterion for a signiﬁcant diﬀerence. At the distribution body,
one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Wilcoxon, 1945) compare the path-wise
maximum drawdowns of each model against buy-and-hold on the 10,000 common-
random-number paths of Section 3.4; these are the decumulation drawdowns deﬁned
there, not market drawdowns, and the paired design removes the between-history
variance component from the comparison. H2 operationalizes RQ2: the attention-
based architecture translates its representational capacity into better decumulation
outcomes. It is tested by one-sided paired Wilcoxon tests on path-wise terminal cap-
ital, comparing the Transformer separately against each competing active strategy
(MSM, multivariate and univariate HMM, and LSTM). Nonparametric tests are cho-
sen because terminal wealth and drawdown distributions are heavily skewed and, on
ruined paths, carry a point mass at zero.
Beyond the hypothesis tests, the evaluation reports three groups of descriptive met-
rics. At the backtest level, annualized performance and risk ﬁgures (CAGR, volatility,
Sharpe ratio (Sharpe, 1966), Sortino ratio (Sortino & van der Meer, 1991), max-
imum drawdown, Calmar ratio, Ulcer index, and time to recovery for drawdowns
exceeding 5%) summarize the single realized out-of-sample path; the Sharpe and
Sortino ratios are computed against a zero risk-free rate, since the cash yield already
enters each strategy’s return series through the allocation rule. Signal-dynamics statis-
tics (number of regime switches, share of whipsaws, deﬁned as reversals within ﬁve
trading days, and cumulative transaction costs) quantify the economic cost of sig-
nal instability. At the classiﬁcation level, precision, recall, and F1 scores against the
NBER recession chronology (National Bureau of Economic Research, 2023) provide a
model-independent external reference for regime identiﬁcation. The chronology enters
through the FRED series USREC of the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, which is a
monthly 0/1 indicator; it is forward-ﬁlled to daily frequency and reindexed onto the
trading-day calendar, so every recession day counted in Section 4.1 and Appendix C
is a forward-ﬁlled monthly value, and the positive class is aligned to month bound-
aries rather than to daily turning points. Together with the fact that the chronology
dates the real economy rather than the market (Section 2), this is why it serves as
a common yardstick across models rather than as an absolute accuracy measure. At
the crisis level, cumulative return and maximum drawdown are evaluated within ﬁve
historical windows chosen to span distinct crisis morphologies, complemented by the
episode-based recovery durations of Section 4.2: the slowly unfolding dot-com bear
market (2000 to 2002), the global ﬁnancial crisis (2007 to 2009), the externally trig-
gered EU debt crisis episode with its fast recovery (2011), the V-shaped COVID crash
(2020), and the 2022 rate shock, in which equities and bonds fell jointly. Because crisis
metrics are computed on the out-of-sample result path, the dot-com window is trun-
cated at the start of that path, October 16, 2000. The ﬁrst three windows fall into
the development period of the hyperparameter search, whereas COVID and the 2022
episode lie in the selection-free holdout (Section 3.3); crisis behavior in the last two
windows therefore measures generalization to unseen crisis types.
