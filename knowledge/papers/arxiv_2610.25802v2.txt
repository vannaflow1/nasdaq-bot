Abstract
Statistical arbitrage trades on an estimate of the state of a residual
spread, and conventional practice discards the uncertainty of that
estimate. We keep the uncertainty and use it in the trading decision.
A Neural Filter Estimator (NFE) is trained on simulated paths of a
mean-reverting process whose innovation law is selected by test-
ing candidates against real residuals; once trained, it returns the
posterior over the latent state of every spread in a cross-section in
one forward pass. Three results follow. On simulated paths with
known truth, every practical estimator, ours included, falls in one
narrow band of accuracy well below an estimator given full in-
formation, so the posterior gains no accuracy over a point esti-
mate; its contribution is a correct account of its own uncertainty.
On Korean equities, net of costs, the conventional point estimate
forecasts about a thousand basis points per holding period more
than it realizes, and the amortized methods cut that gap by a fac-
tor of roughly two to eight. Sizing positions from the posterior
on the same learned factors as the end-to-end Attention Factor
Model matches the Sharpe ratio of those factors under the base
rule within the period’s noise, at no more risk.

the animal and describes a different beast. Economics and finance
pose the same difficulty. It is nearly impossible to say where the
market standsat the moment, and certainty arrives only after the

fact. A familiar example is the business cycle, whose recessions are
dated by the NBER many months after they begin [21]. The term
nowcastingarose from this predicament [8]. Rather than wait for
the whole animal to come into view, we estimate thepresentstate
from the part within reach. A forecast means little without know-

bitrage, where knowing what is happening now matters more to
the next action than predicting what happens next.
Conventional statistical arbitrage [1, 6] handles this problem
with a hand-crafted pipeline. One regresses returns on factors, ac-
cumulates the residuals, fits a mean-reversion model on a trailing
window, and uses the fitted point estimates to decide when to en-
ter and exit. The procedure treats the assumed process as if it were
true, and despite the namestatisticalarbitrage it discards the uncer-
tainty of the estimates, so that a genuine opportunity and an opti-
mistic misreading of the data become indistinguishable. In practice,
moreover, the procedure is applied to a large universe of candidate
spreads: the candidates are ranked by these estimates and the top-
ranked ones are traded. However, such ranking compounds the
problem, because the candidates with the most favorable estima-
tion errors are precisely the ones selected; this is the winner’s curse
of post-selection inference [3]. More recent work learns the factors
end to end [4, 11]. This improves the construction of the residuals
but leaves their use unchanged: the trading decision is still made
from a point estimate, with no estimate of the latent state and no
measure of its uncertainty.
Despite these differences, the methods above share one struc-
ture. Each compresses a panel of returns, one day’s cross-section
of many assets, into a few factors, and trades what the factors
do not explain. They differ only in how the factors are obtained.
Fama–French [5] fix them in advance as characteristic portfolios;
Avellaneda–Lee [1] extract them from the data by principal com-
ponents and trade the residual spread as a mean-reverting pro-
cess; Epstein et al. [4] learn them end to end from characteris-
tics with a trading objective. Outside finance, tabular foundation
models [16, 23] perform the same compression with a network pre-
trained on a synthetic prior rather than fitted to each dataset. This
paper takes the next step in that sequence. It keeps the residual
spread and its mean-reversion state from Avellaneda–Lee, the task-
driven factor learning of Epstein et al., and the synthetic-prior pre-
training of the tabular models, and it adds what none of them has:
a dynamic model of the latent state, with the uncertainty of its
filtered estimate. We call the resulting estimator the Neural Filter
Estimator (NFE).
Our claim concerns how the regime is defined and estimated.
Most statistical-arbitrage strategies already condition on some no-
tion of regime, but in practice that notion is a heuristic, such as a

volatility threshold or a trailing return, or a label assigned ex post,
once the outcome is known. We instead define the regime as a la-
tent variable of an explicit generative model of the spread: whether
the spread is currently converging to its level or diverging from it,
and how fast. We then show that a single amortized filter can esti-
mate this regime for every spread in a cross-section from past data
alone, with a calibrated probability attached, and that the trading
decision can use that probability in place of a point estimate.
Two choices in this definition need justification: the number of
states and the use of a probability. In the regime-switching liter-
ature [12], a latent state is called a regime when a change in it
changes the distribution of the observed process, for example its
mean, its variance or its speed of mean reversion. For a trader, only
the changes that alter the trading decision matter, so we define the
regime relative to the decision. For a contrarian strategy held over
a fixed horizon this leaves two states: one in which enough of the
deviation reverts within the horizon for a position taken against it
to pay, and one in which it does not, so that the same position earns
nothing but its costs. Acting on this regime requires a judgment of
how certain its assignment is, because the two states call for differ-
ent actions and a mistaken assignment is costly. A point estimate
assigns the spread to one state outright and gives no measure of
that certainty, which makes the assignment difficult to act on; a
posterior assigns a probability to each state, which the decision
can weigh against the cost of being on the wrong side. Conven-
tional practice lacks this measure not out of neglect but because
its plug-in estimates do not provide it.
The rest of the paper supports this claim with three measure-
ments. The first checks the simulator. The filter is trained only on
simulated spreads, so the simulated process must resemble the real
residuals before anything learned from it can be trusted on real
data. We test this directly: a classifier is trained to distinguish simu-
lated windows from real ones, and a candidate process passes only
if the classifier largely fails. The Gaussian noise of the classical
model is rejected. The only candidate that passes on both cross-
sections has both time-varying volatility and asymmetric shocks;
fat tails alone are not enough.
The second measures how well the regime can be identified
at all. On simulated spreads the true regime is known, so every
method can be compared with the best possible classifier, one that
knows the true model, level and volatility. Every practical method,
ours included, lands in one narrow band far below that ideal. The
shortfall comes not from the estimator but from the window: the
level of the spread must be estimated from the same window as
its reversion, and that alone accounts for most of the loss. Better
inference cannot close this gap; only more information can.
The third asks what the posterior changes in trading. Under
fixed trading rules and after costs, posterior sizing earns about
the same as the point estimate; the posterior adds no alpha. What
changes is the strategy’s own forecast of its return. The point esti-
mate predicts far more than it realizes, a direct consequence of the
winner’s curse above; the posterior-based strategies reduce this
gap several-fold. The mean-reverting structure in the data is real,
but it is smaller than the cost of trading on it, and the value of the
posterior lies in saying so.
2 Related Work
Factor models and statistical arbitrage.The line of work this paper
extends begins with static characteristic factors: Fama–French [5]
explain the cross-section of returns with a few factor portfolios
fixed in advance. Dynamic factor models let the factors evolve as
a latent linear state [7, 26]. Statistical arbitrage trades the residu-
als that the factor models leave unexplained. Gatev et al. [6] trade
pairs selected by the distance method, and Avellaneda and Lee [1],
in what has become the standard treatment, fit a mean-reversion
model to cumulative factor residuals and trade its point estimates.
Recent deep-learning work trades factor portfolios instead of se-
lected pairs and learns the trading policy from residual histories
end to end [11], and Epstein et al. [4] learn conditional factors by
attention over characteristics, jointly with an after-cost trading ob-
jective. In all of these methods the trading decision rests on a point
estimate, and the uncertainty of the latent state plays no part in it.
Tabular foundation models.Outside finance, a parallel line of
work arrived at the same computation. Prior-data fitted networks
[20] train a transformer on draws from a synthetic prior, so that
one forward pass approximates a posterior predictive. Hollmann
et al. [13] make the approach practical for small tables with
TabPFN [14], Qu et al. [23] scale it with a column-then-row en-
coding, and Kong and Das [16] combine these in TabFM, which
alternates attention across columns and rows, compresses each
row to one vector, and predicts from those vectors in context. A
factor model also compresses each cross-section into a few num-
bers; in this sense the conditional factor of Epstein et al. [4] is the
finance special case of the learned row embedding. These models
are not built for financial panels: their in-context stage attends
over rows as an exchangeable set, and a time-ordered panel is not
one. This paper closes part of that gap by placing a latent-state
dynamic model under the row embedding and inferring the state
from the past alone.
Filtering and amortized inference.In state-space models, filter-
ing refers to the estimation of a latent state from past observations
alone [15]. Hamilton [12] introduced its regime-switching form to
economics, and modern deep variants learn the state-space model
itself [17] or amortize the filter with a variational inference net-
work [19]. When the latent state can be simulated together with
the observations, a different approach becomes possible. Neural
posterior estimation trains a conditional density estimator on sim-
ulator draws and then evaluates the posterior in one forward pass
[22]; it is the core of simulation-based inference [2], and its cali-
bration can be checked by simulation [27]. The NFE applies neu-
ral posterior estimation to the spreads of a time-ordered panel,
restricts the prior to a dynamic process whose innovation law is
tested against real data, and amortizes the filtering posterior over
the state.

3.2 Data-Generating Process
The filter is trained by neural posterior estimation (Section 3.5):
on simulated draws in which the latent state is known, it learns
the map from an observed window to the posterior over the state,
and it is then applied to real windows without further fitting. This
amortization requires a process to simulate from, and the posterior
is worth only as much as that process resembles the data, so the
data-generating process is the object on which the method rests
and must be stated explicitly. Conventional strategies assume one
implicitly when they expect the spread to revert to a stationary
level, and the end-to-end approaches leave theirs in the training
distribution.

write the law of the increment conditional on where the spread

3.3 Neural Filter Estimator
Once the process is fixed, the question is what information to give
the filter at time𝑡. A single spread’s window identifies its state
only partly, since the level must be estimated from the same win-
dow; the rest of the panel, the same day’s returns and characteris-
tics of the other assets, enters through the cross-sectional step. As
shown in Figure 1, the panel is a table with days as rows and as-
sets as columns, and the NFE processes it along both axes: a cross-
sectional step across the assets of one day, which removes what
the assets share and leaves each asset’s own spread, and a time-
series step along each asset’s window, which reads the spread’s dy-
namics. The tabular foundation models [16, 23] organize in-context
learning on tables the same way, and the cross-sectional step cor-
responds to their row embedding (Section 2), with one deliberate
difference: those models treat rows as exchangeable, which a time-
ordered panel is not, so the NFE’s time-series step is a recursion
that keeps the order of days, while its cross-sectional step, a factor
model, is indifferent to the order of assets.
Cross-sectional step.The classical construction uses returns
alone: the factors are the leading principal components of the
recent return covariance, and each asset’s residual is what they
leave. The Attention Factor Model (AFM) [4] instead computes
the factor weights each day by attention over the characteristics,
so that assets with similar characteristics are pooled into the
same factor portfolio and the factors adapt as the characteristics
change. In either construction a factor model maps returns and
characteristics to one residual return per asset,

3.4 Decision layer
The decision layer is pre-specified: the rules below were fixed be-
fore any run and apply with the same conventions to every strat-
egy, and the filter is never fitted to realized profit, so the posterior
is the only interface between the filter and the portfolio. If the rule
were learned, as in AFM’s end-to-end trading network, a difference
in performance could come from the rule as much as from the state
estimate; with the rule fixed, the strategies differ only in the resid-
ual they trade and in whether the position is sized from a point
estimate or from the posterior. Three components are selected on
the training split: the threshold rule’s entry level and AFM’s char-
acteristic set, by their training-split Sharpe ratios, and the per-asset
prior below, from the training split’s half-lives.

level appears in𝑑as a window estimate and is not penalized, the
omission Section 4.2 finds to be the largest source of identification
loss.
The training prior over𝜏is deliberately wide, flat inlog 2𝜏over
[3,400]days, so that one network serves every spread. At deploy-
ment it is replaced, for each stock, by a narrower Gaussian prior
inlog 2𝜏centred on that stock’s own half-life, estimated on𝐿-day
blocks of the training split and shrunk toward the panel mean by
empirical Bayes, so that a stock with few or noisy blocks is pulled
further toward the mean. Because the head is trained under a flat
prior, its output is likelihood-shaped, and the new prior is applied
by reweighting the posterior draws used in (7); the network is not
retrained.

Real data never reveal the latent state, but simulated draws in-

filter is trained as in neural posterior estimation [22], by maximiz-

moment-matched values (Table 5). Each window then carries one
half-life, so𝜃reduces to𝜏, and the flat prior gives the likelihood-

Figure 2(b) uses the training draws themselves. No real returns en-

its calibration is measured on simulated draws, where the truth is
known (Section 4.2), and it cannot have been fitted to the outcomes
it is later scored on. Real data enter the filter only through the

the per-asset prior, the split-conformal layer [24] on every learned
predictive distribution of Section 4.2, and the direct variant’s target

5 Conclusion
This paper nowcasts the state of a residual spread instead of fore-
casting its next move. It builds on the classical and the end-to-end
approaches to statistical arbitrage, trains its filter on a simulated
process whose innovation law was chosen by testing candidates
against real residuals, and uses the uncertainty of the estimate in
the trading decision. Three measured results follow. Within them,
four findings have passed a confirmation run: the overshoot find-
ing, the panel-form pooling contrast, the per-asset prior and the
ordering of the forecast–realized gaps.
First, the regime is identifiable, but only marginally: every prac-
tical estimator and the strong reference detectors fall in one nar-
row band near0.61–0.66AUC, against0.93for a fully informed
detector, and the largest loss comes from estimating the level from
the same window as the reversion. Second, a calibrated posterior
gives an accurate account of its own error. A point estimate’s fore-
cast exceeds its realized return by roughly a thousand basis points
per hold and gives no interval; the amortized methods reduce that
gap by a factor of roughly two to eight, and since a point forecast
rescaled by one constant reaches the same gap, the reduction is
a uniform shrinkage, and the interval, which the point estimate
lacks, is what the posterior adds. No method shows skill against

an unconditional baseline, with returns alone or with fundamen-
tals added. Third, in the portfolio evaluation the residual construc-
tion matters more than the trading policy. Posterior sizing matches
AFM’s Sharpe, within the period’s noise, at no more risk. No pre-
specified decision-layer configuration adds return of the size the
residual construction adds; the gain from a longer window is un-
stable across periods and not claimed; and the fully learned cross-
sectional step does no better than the random benchmark.
One methodological lesson runs through all three results: a
label-free diagnostic needs a null model and a positive control
before its output can be interpreted. Without them several of
ours lacked power or were biased upward, and we withdrew the
conclusions drawn from them, the250-day gain among them. A
simulator can pass a two-sample test that has almost no power
against the kernel it appears to validate: changing the kernel
moves the AUC by at most0.014(Appendix A).
Since the largest identification loss comes from the level esti-
mated from the same window, the natural next step is the panel
form of the NFE: one causal network over the cross-section and
time, pretrained on a panel-level generative model, that pools the
cross-section inside the network and returns its posteriors jointly,
with a selective state-space encoder [10] as its time-series step. On
truth-known draws with a shared factor in the levels, the panel
form already improves the half-life posterior over the per-asset fil-
ter, in part through capacity, and stays calibrated (Section 4.2); on
real data the two cross-sectional routes tried here, a learned cross-
sectional step and a level learned from characteristics, did not help,
so its value there is untested. Whether on real data the information
is worth more than the cost of acting on it is the question this work
leaves open.

B Experiment Details
Data.Both universe filters use end-of-sample information (liquid-
ity ranking at the sample end; delisted stocks dropped). The six
characteristics are point-in-time: each becomes available on the
first trading day after disclosure, flows are trailing-twelve-month,
capitalization is split-consistent, and a missing stock-day receives
a neutral rank.
Definitions.
Weights and costsdefined on the residual portfolios of (5)
[4]; the implied factor-hedge legs are not separately traded
or costed.
Turnoverthe mean daily absolute change in these weights as
a fraction of gross.
ΔSRthe annualized Sharpe ratio of the daily return difference
between a strategy and the random benchmark (bootstrap
SE0.41–0.42); the benchmark redraws its random positions
every𝐻days at the same gross, hence its higher turnover.
Not modeledthe Korean transaction tax and short-sale re-
strictions in force during part of the window; levels are
therefore not implementable returns.
Estimation notes.
FF3needs size and B/P on the same stock-day, with joint cov-
erage only from2017, so part of its weakness, in the earlier
period especially, reflects limited coverage.
Conformal layerwidths are multiplicative per quantile; the
outcome-fitted methods fit on the first80%of the training
split and calibrate on the remaining20%after an𝐿-day gap,
simulator methods on the full training split.
Shrinkage controlkeeps the point estimate’s positions and
rescales only its forecast by one constant fixed on the train-
ing split; its KRX run uses a panel rebuilt under the same
rule from a second vendor’s adjusted prices, on which Ta-
