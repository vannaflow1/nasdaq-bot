Abstract
This study uses a high-frequency dataset from a stock exchan ge to examine
how forecasting ability and liquidity provision relate to i nstitutional traders’
proprietary intraday proﬁtability. We discover persisten t forecasting abilities
among traders. Speciﬁcally, we demonstrate that hypotheti cal ﬂow-following
strategies based on the execution ﬂows of these skillful tra ders yield annualized
Sharpe ratios ranging from 16 to 63 across various trading fr equencies. We
match buy and sell executions into positions, linking reali zed proﬁt and loss
(P&L) to horizon-matched forecasting proxies and the liqui dity roles of both
executions. Our ﬁndings indicate a direct correlation betw een improved fore-
casting ability and proﬁtability, where a one-standard-de viation increase in the
forecasting ability proxy is associated with 1.02 – 1.19 cur rency units higher
P&L per matched position. Additionally, we measure liquidi ty-provision inten-
sity within a matched position by its number of passive execu tions: zero, one,
or two. In these regressions, P&L is 1.28 – 1.31 currency unit s higher for one
passive execution than for none, and a further 3.79 – 3.82 hig her for two than
for one, underscoring the signiﬁcant ﬁnancial beneﬁts of li quidity provision.

1 Introduction
An important question about trading in ﬁnancial markets is where pr oprietary intra-
day trading proﬁts come from. High-frequency traders are fast intermediaries who
trade for their own account and whose proﬁts can reﬂect severa l economic chan-
nels, including liquidity provision, information processing, order-ﬂow prediction, and
speed-based rents ( Menkveld; 2016). The answer matters because these channels have
diﬀerent implications for market quality: proﬁts from intermediation can compen-
sate traders for supplying immediacy and bearing inventory or adve rse-selection risk,
whereas proﬁts from prediction or anticipation can reﬂect informa tional rents earned
from other traders. Existing evidence shows that high-frequenc y trading (HFT) prof-
its can be large and persistent ( Baron et al. ; 2019), but understanding their source
requires measuring proﬁts and candidate mechanisms at the same e mpirical unit.
This measurement requirement is demanding at high frequency. Ret urn pre-
dictability is informative for both aggressive trading and passive mar ket making, but
translating predictability into dollar proﬁts depends on order-book dynamics, ﬁlls,
fees, rebates, and price impact ( A ¨ ıt-Sahalia et al.; 2025). Similarly, distinguishing liq-
uidity provision from information-based trading requires knowing no t only whether an
execution supplies or demands liquidity, but also how the resulting pro ﬁt is matched
to the trader, the holding interval, and the market state. We ther efore focus on two
central and observable channels emphasized by the literature, pr ice-forecasting ability
and liquidity provision.
We use a high-frequency data set from a stock exchange that rec ords all limit
order book events for ﬁve stocks from November 22 to November 26, 2010, including
opening and closing call auctions. For each limit order book event, ou r data provides
detailed information such as the time of the event, the type of even t, the number of
shares, the price, the identity of both the liquidity taker and the liqu idity provider,
and the trading capacity indicating whether the order was for a clien t or the trader’s
own proprietary trading. These ﬁelds allow us to study proprietary institutional
trading through two linked measurements. First, for each of 26 tim e horizons ranging
from 1 second to 30 minutes, we construct a horizon-speciﬁc price -forecasting ability
measure for a trader by the correlation between the normalized ex ecution ﬂow of the
trader in an interval with length being the given horizon and the volum e-weighted
average price (VW AP) change in the subsequent time interval. Seco nd, we consider a

proﬁt-and-loss (P&L) of a trader at the ﬁnest granularity that c omprises a buy (resp.,
sell) execution of the trader and a subsequent sell (resp., buy) ex ecution of the same
trader. We match proprietary executions into ﬁrst-in-ﬁrst-out (FIFO) buy-sell or

We use these measurements to address two questions. First, do s ome institutional
traders display persistent price-forecasting abilities at various int raday time horizons?
Second, how does a proprietary intraday P&L carried out by a trad er vary with
the trader’s price-forecasting ability at the time horizon of the P&L and with the
passiveness of the two executions constituting the P&L? Our ﬁrst question relates to
research on trading predictability and the persistence of trading s kill. The second
question provides an empirical accounting of how a P&L covaries with two central
mechanisms in the existing literature, rather than a structural mo del of all possible
proﬁt sources. The related literature falls into several strands, each measuring part
of this problem at a diﬀerent horizon or accounting unit.
The ﬁrst strand of related research studies the predictive conte nt of daily stock-
level aggregate institutional net trading ﬂows and fund-level tra ding skill within a
quarter. Campbell et al. (2009) infer daily stock-level institutional net trading ﬂows
from TAQ transactions and quarterly 13F holdings data. They ﬁnd t hat these ﬂows
predict subsequent daily stock returns, with generally positive coe ﬃcients on the
25-day-half-life measure of lagged ﬂows, and anticipate ﬁrm-level earnings surprises
and post-earnings announcement drift. Puckett and Yan (2011) use institutional
transaction data to measure fund-level intra-quarter trading s kill as the diﬀerence
between benchmark-adjusted returns on buys and sells from exe cution to quarter-
end.
At shorter trading horizons, studies examine traders’ anticipatio n of local price
paths or order ﬂow and estimate investor-speciﬁc trading skill ove r order-execution
horizons. Fishe et al. (2019) use millisecond-level order-book data from WTI crude-oil
futures to identify traders who anticipate “local price paths,” who se average dura-
tion across all identiﬁed paths is about 102.8–115.6 seconds, depen ding on direction.
These anticipatory traders trade early in the direction of an emerg ing path or before
a local reversal and need not be the fastest participants. Hirschey (2021) studies
a diﬀerent anticipatory channel, ﬁnding that aggregate net marke table buying by

predicts cumulative non-HFT net marketable buying over the followin g ﬁve minutes.

Our ﬁrst question examines whether the aggregate execution ﬂow of traders iden-
tiﬁed by horizon-speciﬁc price predictability continues to predict pr ice changes at
the same horizon in a subsequent period. Related evidence concern s persistence in
fund-level interim trading performance, anticipatory trading str ategies, and individ-
ual HFTs’ ability to predict non-HFT order ﬂow. We complement evide nce on this
by testing the persistence of forecasting ability at 26 prediction ho rizons ranging from
1 second to 30 minutes. Puckett and Yan (2011) ﬁnd that fund-level interim trading
performance persists over the subsequent four quarters. Fishe et al. (2019) compare
the retention of intraday anticipatory trading skills in 2014 among ac counts iden-
tiﬁed in 2011 as trading early in the direction of local price trends or a nticipating
price reversals, ﬁnding greater retention for the latter, althoug h account attrition and
changes in account identiﬁers limit tracking. Hirschey (2021) ﬁnds persistent diﬀer-
ences in individual HFTs’ ability to predict order ﬂow across months. Groups formed
on this ability also diﬀer in how their aggregate net marketable buying p redicts NBBO
midpoint returns in the postsort month.
A second strand measures HFT proﬁtability and its components usin g aggregate
measures, such as daily ﬁrm-level P&L, rather than individual matc hed buy-sell or
sell-buy P&L observations. Baron et al. (2019) calculate daily mark-to-market P&L
for each HFT ﬁrm and each stock from cash ﬂows and end-of-day in ventory values, and
document large and persistent proﬁts. Menkveld (2013) decomposes one particular
HFT account’s average gross trading proﬁt per trade over 200 tr ading days into net
spread revenues and positioning gains or losses, showing that pass ive spread revenues
can oﬀset inventory-related positioning losses.
Related evidence examines how HFT trading revenues vary with liquidit y de-
mand and supply and with the direction of institutional order ﬂow. Brogaard et al.
(2014) estimate aggregate trading revenues of HFT ﬁrms per stock-da y separately for
liquidity-demanding and liquidity-supplying trades. They show that HF T liquidity-
demanding marketable orders trade in the direction of permanent p rice changes and

The HFT proﬁtability studies above report ﬁrm-stock-day trading revenues, aver-
age per-trade proﬁt components for a single HFT, or revenues ag gregated across HFT
ﬁrms over stock-days or institutional meta-order execution wind ows. By contrast, our
question concerns how realized P&L from a single FIFO-matched pair of executions in
opposite direction covaries with a horizon-matched measure of the trader’s forecasting
ability and with the passiveness of its two constituent executions.
To address this question, we construct a FIFO-matched P&L fram ework for pro-
prietary institutional intraday trades that brings forecasting, e xecution, and prof-
itability measures together at the individual P&L observation level. S peciﬁcally,
the framework links each FIFO-matched buy-sell or sell-buy P&L ob servation to
a horizon-matched measure of the trader’s forecasting ability, th e passiveness of its
two constituent executions, and trade and order-book controls . This observation-level
linkage allows us to estimate how realized proprietary intraday P&L co varies with the
trader’s price-forecasting ability and to compare the P&L across le vels of liquidity-
provision intensity, deﬁned here by the number of passive executio ns comprising the
P&L.
To address the ﬁrst question, we measure a trader’s price-forec asting ability at a
particular time horizon, e.g., 1 second, by the Pearson correlation b etween the trader’s
normalized execution ﬂow in each time interval and the volume-weighted average price
(VW AP) changes in the next time interval. A trader with statistically s igniﬁcant
positive correlation at a time horizon is classiﬁed as having price-fore casting ability
at that time horizon. We consider 26 time horizons ranging from 1 sec ond to 30
minutes. To test the persistence of traders’ price-forecasting ability, we split the ﬁve-
day sample into two periods comprising the ﬁrst two days and the sub sequent three

the group of traders consisting of those with price-forecasting a bility at that time
horizon (Group 1), while the remaining traders constitute Group 2. The subsequent
three-day period serves as the out-of-sample validation window to test whether the
traders in Group 1 are able to predict subsequent price movements persistently.
We construct group execution ﬂows by summing standardized exec ution ﬂows of
all traders within each group, so traders with diﬀerent execution v olume are compa-
rable. We measure the price-forecasting ability of each group by th e Group Measure
(GM), deﬁned as the correlation between group execution ﬂow and subs equent price
movements. From Table 9, Group 1’s GM is signiﬁcantly positive in the validation
period at all tested horizons from 1 second to 2 minutes.
To gauge the economic signiﬁcance of the forecasting ability of trad ers in Group
1, we simulate three zero-cost trading strategies during the last- three-day validation

At the end of each δ-length time interval, the strategy 1 takes a directional position

ability of traders in Group 1. Similarly, the strategy 2 trades with pos ition size equal

Group 2. The positions of the strategies are held for time δ and adjusted at the

We also check whether the trading value of Group 1 accounts for on ly a small share
of total trading value, which helps distinguish persistent predictab ility from mechan-
ical price pressure. Across most horizons, the execution ﬂow of G roup 1 accounts for
less than 15% of the total trading value per stock and exhibits lower absolute volumes
than those of Group 2, thereby ruling out self-generated price mo mentum as a driver
of the observed persistence.

of proprietary trading and traders’ price-forecasting ability and liquidity provision.
We deﬁne a P&L at the ﬁnest granularity as the proﬁt or loss resultin g from a pair of
a buy (resp., sell) execution and a subsequent sell (resp., buy) exe cution of the same
number of shares by the same trader in the same stock. We use a FI FO matching
algorithm to obtain all the P&Ls of each trader from the trader’s pr oprietary execu-
tions, including executions during opening and closing calls. These auc tion sessions
account for more than 12% of total trading value in the data set an d are therefore
important for accurate matching. The regression sample retains P &L observations
whose two executions occur during continuous trading on the same day.
Building on this foundation, for each matched P&L of a trader, we at tach to it the
trader’s forecasting ability measure at the horizon of the P&L and t he passiveness of
the two executions constituting the P&L. Speciﬁcally, for each tra der n and horizon
δj, we calculate ρjn, the correlation between the trader’s execution ﬂow in an interval
of length δj and the price change in the subsequent interval across 26 horizon s from
1 second to 30 minutes. For each P&L P Li carried out by trader n, we use three
alternative approaches to ﬁnd the horizon δk among the 26 horizons that is the closest
to the horizon of the P&L, which is deﬁned as the duration between t he two executions
constituting the P&L. Under each approach, we then deﬁne the pr oxy of forecasting
ability of the trader for this particular P&L by fi = ρkn.
We deﬁne two dummies SLi and LLi for P Li, indicating that exactly one and both
of the two executions constituting the P&L are passive, respectiv ely. The omitted
category consists of P&L observations in which both executions ar e aggressive.
We then regress P Li on fi, SLi, and LLi, together with trade characteristics,
order-book controls, and trader ﬁxed eﬀects, with stock ﬁxed e ﬀects added in the
pooled regressions, so the comparison is made within a common accou nting unit and
conditional on the state of the order book. These estimates ask w hether a P&L is
higher when the trader has stronger forecasting ability at the par ticular horizon of
the P&L and when the trader uses passive limit orders to carry out t he executions.
In the pooled regressions, conditional on the other regressors, a one-standard-
deviation increase in fi is associated with 1.02 – 1.19 currency units higher P&L per
matched position across the three horizon-matching approaches . The execution-type
coeﬃcients also exhibit a consistent ordering in P&L, holding the othe r regressors
ﬁxed. Relative to positions with no passive executions (MM), position s with exactly
one passive execution (SL) have 1.28–1.31 currency units higher P& L, whereas posi-

Our empirical analysis yields four key ﬁndings. First, the execution ﬂ ow of the
group of traders whose execution ﬂow can forecast future price changes in the ﬁrst
two-day time period continues to have statistically signiﬁcant positiv e correlation
with future price changes in the second three-day time period. The results hold at 22
time horizons ranging from 1 second to 2 minutes. This group-level e vidence supports
persistence in the price-forecasting ability captured by the initial c lassiﬁcation.
Second, hypothetical trading strategies that take positions bas ed on the execution
ﬂow of the group of traders whose execution ﬂow has statistically s igniﬁcant positive
correlation with future price change in the ﬁrst two-day period ach ieve high annualized
Sharpe ratios in the second three-day period. The results hold at 2 3 time horizons
ranging from 1 second to 5 minutes, further illustrating the econom ic signiﬁcance of
the identiﬁed price-forecasting ability and its persistence.
Third, a P&L consisting of a pair of executions by a trader in opposite direc-
tions is positively associated with the price-forecasting ability of the trader at the
particular time horizon that is commensurate with that of the P&L. T his links the
proxy of forecasting ability to proﬁts from actual proprietary ex ecutions, conditional
on execution roles and the other controls.
Fourth, holding the other regressors ﬁxed, a P&L is higher with mor e executions
constituting the P&L being passive. This evidence on liquidity provision is consistent
with prior work showing that liquidity suppliers and market makers can earn returns
for providing immediacy ( Jylh¨ a et al.; 2014; Hendershott and Seasholes ; 2007). A
close HFT comparison comes from Brogaard et al. (2014), who decompose stock-
day HFT revenues and show that liquidity-demanding marketable ord ers have an
informational advantage suﬃcient to overcome the bid-ask sprea d and trading fees.
By contrast, liquidity-supplying nonmarketable limit orders are adve rsely selected,
although spread-related revenues and exchange liquidity rebates can more than oﬀset
the associated adverse-selection costs. Our execution matching analysis therefore
provides complementary evidence from a broader proprietary-tr ader sample and a
diﬀerent empirical unit. It preserves the liquidity roles of both exec utions in each P&L
observation, allowing us to distinguish supplying liquidity on one side fro m supplying
it on both sides, conditional on the forecasting proxy and the othe r regressors.

2 Data
The data set contains the information of all limit order book events, including those
occurring in opening and closing calls, for ﬁve stocks, labeled by Stoc k-1, Stock-2,
Stock-3, Stock-4, and Stock-5, during November 22, 2010 to No vember 26, 2010.
There are three types of limit order book events: limit order submiss ion, limit order
cancellation, and execution. Each execution is caused by the match ing of an incoming
market order or marketable limit order, which is submitted by a liquidity taker, with a
limit order on the order book, which was previously submitted by a liquid ity provider.
The information for each limit order book event includes the time of th e event, the
type of the event, number of shares, price, whether an order is a hidden order or
a displayed order, identity of the trader for order submission or ca ncellation, and
the identity of the liquidity taker and that of the liquidity provider for execution.
Notably, the data set contains the trading capacity of each limit ord er and market
order submitted by a trader, which indicates whether the order is s ubmitted on behalf
of the trader’s client or for the trader’s own proprietary trading.
The data set has three distinctive features. (i) The data set prov ides explicit iden-
tity of the trader for each limit order and market order, enabling pr ecise tracking of
individual trading behavior. (ii) The trading capacity of each limit orde r and market
order is provided, distinguishing traders’ proprietary trading beh avior from that for
the traders’ clients. Because clients’ trading volume is almost half o f the total trading
volume, it is essential to exclude orders from clients in order to stud y traders’ pro-
prietary trading. (iii) The data set contains information of all execu tions occurring
during opening and closing call auction sessions, unlike conventional datasets that
only include information of continuous trading sessions. Such inform ation is impor-
tant because the trading value during opening call and closing call ac counts for more
than 12% of the total trading value in our data set. This allows us to c alculate proﬁt
and loss of traders more accurately by taking into account the exe cution of traders

during opening call and closing call.

2.1 Opening and closing calls
The stock exchange has three sessions during a trading day: open ing call, continuous
trading, and closing call. During the opening call, order entry and full order man-
agement are allowed but no matching of buy and sell orders takes pla ce. At the end
of the opening call, there is an uncross taking place at 9:00, which last s a short time,
usually a fraction of a second. During the uncross, crossing order s are executed at the
equilibrium price. 1 Unexecuted orders remaining after the opening uncross constitu te
the initial limit order book for the continuous trading session. During the continuous
trading, there is automatic matching of orders; each new coming or der is immediately
checked for execution against limit orders on the opposite side of th e limit order book.
Continuous trading is followed by the closing call, which is similar to the op ening call.
During the closing call, order entry and full order management is allow ed but there
is no automatic matching. At the end of closing call, an uncross takes place between
17:29:30 and 17:30:00. During the closing call uncross, crossing orde rs are executed
based on the equilibrium price of orders in that phase. Unexecuted o rders remaining
after the closing call uncross are canceled.

3 Traders’ ability of forecasting intraday price move-
ments
In this section, we study traders’ forecasting ability of future pr ice changes at a
spectrum of time horizons ranging from 1 second to 30 minutes. We s plit our ﬁve-
day data set into two time periods: the ﬁrst period is the ﬁrst two da ys and the
second period is the last three days. We ﬁrst construct a measure of a trader’s ability
of forecasting future price change at a given time horizon in Section 3.1. We then
document the distribution of traders’ forecasting ability during th e ﬁrst time period
in Section 3.2, and examine the persistence of this ability over the second time per iod
in Section 3.3. To gauge the economic magnitude of the traders’ forecasting ab ility,
we evaluate the performance of three hypothetical trading stra tegies in Section 3.4.
Furthermore, we address potential price impact concerns by ana lyzing the execution
ﬂow share of diﬀerent trader groups in Section 3.5. Finally, Section 3.6 presents
robustness checks.
