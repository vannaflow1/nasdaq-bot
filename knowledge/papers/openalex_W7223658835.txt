Abstract 

The reliability of intrinsic and post-hoc explanations cannot be assessed independently of the perturbation under 
which they are evaluated. Using purged walk-forward validation, this study forecasts five-day realized volatility 
for 20 equities across three markets from 2016 to 2025 and evaluates five model families paired with intrinsic 
or post-hoc explanation methods. We admit only models that outperform step-5 persistence out of sample to the 
main interpretability analysis. We assess explanation reliability under three perturbations: retraining with 
different random seeds, temporal shift, and input jitter, using matched-stock paired inference. Integrated 
Gradients applied to a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network and to TabNet differ by 0.52 in temporal 
rank agreement, showing that explanation stability depends on the model –representation–explainer 
configuration. When the same trained TabNet network is explained using its intrinsic attention mask and post-
hoc Integrated Gradients, the two methods are similar under temporal shift but diverge under input perturbation 
and retraining. The intrinsic mask is more robust to input noise but less reproducible across retraining. Ablation-
based faithfulness performs no better than random feature ordering, while fixed-depth top-k agreement is biased 
by feature-space size. The Heterogeneous Autoregressive model of Realized Volatility (HAR-RV) achieves the 
best median forecasting accuracy and is statistically indistinguishable from the spline-based Generalized 
Additive Model (GAM). Overall, explanation reliability is perturbation-specific and should be evaluated 
empirically for each model–representation–explainer configuration. 

Keywords: Intrinsic interpretability; post-hoc explainability; volatility forecasting; explanation stability.

1 Introduction 
Financial time-series volatility is often considered 
easier to forecast than returns. However, this remains 
difficult because of volatility clustering, heavy tails in 
return distributions, non-stationarity across market 
regimes, and asymmetric responses to positive and 
negative shocks [1 –3]. Although machine learning 
(ML) models are able to deal more effectively than 
traditional methods such as GARCH or HAR with 
nonlinear relationships, high-dimensional 
interactions, and dynamics that depend on the market 
regime, they are not always better [2, 4]. The need for 
large amounts of data hinders their performance, 
increases the risk of overfitting, raises the 
computational costs linked to the repeated re-
estimation using rolling windows, and results in 
inconsistent improvements out of sample when 
compared to simpler benchmark models [1, 5]. The 
accuracy of the forecasts also relies heavily on how 
the features are constructed, particularly in terms of 
choosing the predictors, representing the different 
market regimes, and regarding the level of 
redundancy or collinearity among the variables [6 –
10]. Another limitation is interpretability: whereas the 
coefficients of classical linear models have fairly 
clear statistical and economic interpretations, many 
machine learning models function as opaque "black 
boxes", which hampers their acceptance by 
regulators, their use in risk management, and the 
ability to identify model failures during periods of 
extreme market conditions [11–13]. This issue has led 
to the adoption of explainable artificial intelligence 
(XAI), which includes both intrinsic and post hoc 
methods. Intrinsic methods build interpretability into 
the model itself, examples being generalized additive 
models (GAMs) [14], Explainable Boosting 
Machines (EBMs) [15], and TabNet [16]. On the 
other hand, post-hoc methods provide explanations 
after training and can be used with more complex 
architectures. Common techniques include SHAP and 
TreeSHAP [17, 18], counterfactual explanations [19], 
and Integrated Gradients [20]. Together, these 
methods offer complementary ways to increase 
transparency while maintaining the predictive 
flexibility of current machine learning models. 
In fact, the vast majority of the explainability tools 
employed in both industry and academic research are 
of a post-hoc nature: SHAP, LIME, and 
counterfactual explanations can be used with any 
model that has already been trained without 
necessitating any modifications to the model, and it is 
for this reason that they are so attractive since they do 
2 

not place any extra demands on the model selection 
process [21 –25]. Rudin [26] argues that this 
convenience is also the source of the problem: a post-
hoc explainer approximates a model which it has not 
taken part in constructing, and such an approximation 
might misrepresent the very reasoning that it claims 
to describe — a risk which she regards as 
unacceptable in the case of important decisions, such 
as those involving finance. Laugel et al. [27] provided 
direct empirical evidence of this problem in 
counterfactual explanations, showing that many 
classifier predictions lie in regions of the input space 
that are not continuously connected to real training 
data of the same class. The authors term these regions 
“unjustified” because explanations generated there 
may reflect artifacts of the model’s decision 
boundary—such as isolated pockets from an unstable 
ensemble or extrapolated regions from an overly 
flexible classifier —rather than patterns genuinely 
supported by the data. Even though their study is 
focused on counterfactual explanations rather than the 
feature-attribution methods that are the subject of this 
paper, the fundamental concern is directly applicable: 
the output of a post-hoc explainer can be affected by 
the idiosyncrasies of the trained model rather than by 
real structures in the data, and there is nothing in the 
post-hoc method itself that guarantees this will not 
occur. 
Yet the debate remains unresolved, even though 
Rudin [26] and Laugel et al. [27] have drawn a 
distinction. Wiratsin et al. [28] and Haque et al. [29] 
claim that neither intrinsic nor post-hoc explanations 
have consistently shown faithfulness, that is, that an 
explanation truly matches the model’s real reasoning. 
Regarding intrinsic methods, genuinely interpretable 
architectures remain rare in practice, and many 
models that are supposed to be interpretable still 
include black-box components. For instance, 
attention weights can be obtained from 
representations already transformed by uninterpreted 
encoders and thus may not indicate the true 
importance of the input. More generally, assertions 
about intrinsic interpretability are often accepted 
without adequate validation, attention being a clear 
case of potentially unfaithful explanations as 
discussed by Jacovi & Goldberg [30]. Post-hoc 
methods also suffer from similar issues. Feature-
attribution techniques can differ greatly, and their 
faithfulness usually depends on the particular task and 
model rather than being generally applicable. Recent 
theoretical research has even indicated that it may be 
impossible to guarantee generally faithful post-hoc 
importance measures, as shown by Bilodeau et 
al.[31]. Likewise, similar limitations have been found 
in relation to neuron-level visualizations and probing 
methods, since explanations that appear convincing 
do not necessarily correspond to the model’s actual 
internal mechanisms. Some literature therefore 
questions whether the distinction between intrinsic 
and post-hoc approaches should remain the main 
framework for explainability. Rather than assuming 
one paradigm is inherently trustworthy, this study 
treats faithfulness, stability, and consistency as 
empirical properties to assess for each model –
representation–explainer configuration. 

The idea put forward by Rudin [26] that intrinsically 
interpretable models should be given preference over 
post hoc explanations of black-box models remains 
disputed. She maintains that the supposed trade-off 
between accuracy and interpretability is generally 
misleading, noting that interpretable models can 
achieve predictive performance competitive with 
other models. Yet in many practical situations, post 
hoc explanations are still needed, since high-
performing models may already be in use, may be 
proprietary, or may be too expensive to replace with 
intrinsically interpretable ones. In these 
circumstances, post hoc methods might be the only 
reasonable way to achieve transparency. However, 
their reliability is not guaranteed. Intrinsic models 
share a similar drawback: their explanations are 
trustworthy only if the public model structure truly 
reflects the reasoning behind the predictions. Rather 
than taking this property for granted, the present study 
assesses it empirically. 
The existing XAI benchmarks generally do not tackle 
the question of whether explanations are intrinsic or 
post-hoc and instead evaluate different kinds of 
explainers (for instance, SHAP with XGBoost rather 
than attention in a transformer) — in this way they 
determine which model is more interpretable rather 
than which explanation method is more reliable, since 
the differences between the base models interfere 
with any differences that are observed. To properly 
assess Rudin’s recommendation, we need both an 
intrinsic explanation and a post-hoc explanation for 
the same trained model, so any discrepancy between 
the two can be attributed to the explanation method 
rather than to one model being easier to interpret than 
the other. To our knowledge, no previous study has 
carried out such a comparison in the context of a 
financial forecasting task, nor has it examined it under 
conditions that are realistic for that domain — 
specifically, non-stationary markets, walk-forward 
evaluation, and a skill gate that makes sure the model 
being explained actually has predictive ability from 
the beginning. 
The paper addresses that gap directly. The paper 
compares intrinsic and post-hoc explanations on the 
same model under controlled conditions: TabNet 
produces an intrinsic explanation via its attention 
masks, and the same trained TabNet model is then 
given a post-hoc explanation using Integrated 
3 

Gradients. Therefore, any difference between the two 
methods must come from the explanation technique, 
not the model. This comparison is part of a more 
comprehensive cross-model benchmark that includes 
five different model families — XGBoost, an LSTM, 
TabNet, a spline-based GAM, and an EBM — each 
being paired with the explainer appropriate to its 
architecture and each being evaluated in the context 
of 5-day-ahead realized volatility forecasting for 20 
equities from the US, European, and Japanese 
markets, using a leakage-safe walk-forward 
procedure. Rather than relying on a single 
interpretability criterion, we assess each model –
representation–explainer configuration using three 
complementary metrics: faithfulness, stability (with 
respect to input noise and over time), and consistency 
across independent explainers for the same model. 
Accordingly, this study makes three methodological 
contributions. First, it isolates explanation effects 
through a same-model comparison in which intrinsic 
TabNet masks and post-hoc Integrated Gradients 
explain the same fitted network. Second, it decomposes 
explanation stability into input-noise, temporal, and re-
estimation perturbations under leakage-safe walk-
forward evaluation. Third, it evaluates explanation 
quality with randomized faithfulness controls and size-
corrected agreement measures so that apparent 
reliability is not inferred from metric artifacts. 

2 Related Work 
2.1 Explainable Artificial Intelligence in Financial 
Time Series 
Arsenault [32] examined the use of XAI over a five-
year period in financial time series forecasting and 
developed a comprehensive taxonomy, distinguishing 
explainability from interpretability —two notions 
often conflated but used differently in practice. This 
distinction underpins the current study’s comparison 
between intrinsic interpretability, which applies to 
models designed to be interpretable (e.g., GAMs and 
EBMs), and post-hoc explainability, which refers to 
techniques applied after training to explain black-box 
models (e.g., TreeSHAP and Integrated Gradients). 
Gunnarson et al. [12] carried out a systematic review 
of AI and ML methods for predicting realized and 
implied volatility indices. They concluded that these 
methods are highly promising and often achieve 
results equal to or better than those of traditional 
econometric models, with neural networks that use 
memory mechanisms such as LSTM and GRU among 
the best performers. Nevertheless, even with these 
findings, very few studies use XAI to support their 
empirical results, pointing to a major gap: as more 
complex models become increasingly common in 
volatility forecasting, their decision-making 
processes remain largely unexplained. 
Černevičienė and Kabašinskas [33] divided the 
FinXAI methodological landscape into three 
dimensions: the time at which the explanation is given 
(with inherently interpretable models such as 
LassoNet and decision trees being contrasted against 
post-hoc methods like SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM, 
which are used with pre-trained models), the scope of 
the explanation (this being either global, which shows 
the overall decision logic, or local, which explains a 
single prediction), and the applicability (with methods 
that are specific to the model and depend on its 
internal architecture, for example those based on 
attention mechanisms, versus model-agnostic 
methods that can be used with any black-box model). 
In financial time series, feature selection helps 
identify relevant market indicators, lagged returns, 
volatility measures, macroeconomic variables, or 
technical indicators that contribute to forecasting 
price movement, risk, or market regimes. Industry 
portfolio indices thus gain intrinsic interpretability 
without sacrificing the ability to model non-linear 
relationships. Giudici et al. [38] emphasized that 
time-series XAI must account for temporal 
dependence, since feature importance changes over 
time and static explanation methods can lead to 
incorrect conclusions. Mohamed et al. [34] went 
beyond applying XAI to regression-based forecasting 
and used it for pattern-recognition tasks, predicting 
five different kinds of market trends. Sharbati et al. 
[35] combined LSTM (to capture temporal 
dependencies), XGBoost (for non-linear regression), 
and SHAP (for explanation) using Transformer-style 
encoders in a hybrid framework that balances 
predictive accuracy and transparency. Suprihadi et al. 
[36] applied a similar LSTM-SHAP approach to the 
forecasting of REIT returns. Ranjan et al. [22] found 
that a simpler 1D-CNN architecture achieved both 
interpretability and competitive predictive 
performance compared with ARIMA and LSTM in 
tests on S&P 500 data, showing that the trade-off 
between complexity and interpretability is not 
necessarily irreducible. Freeborough and van Zyl [37] 
showed that existing XAI methods do not transfer 
easily to RNN-based financial forecasting and that 
temporal dependencies require special handling. 
Taken together, the literature indicates that the 
challenges encountered in XAI for financial time 
series differ from those in tabular or image-based 
applications. Because of autocorrelation and temporal 
dependence, explanations must capture dynamic 
patterns rather than static feature-output relationships, 
and because the data are non-stationary, predictor 
importance can vary across market regimes. The low 
4 

signal-to-noise ratio also raises the possibility that 
explanation techniques will assign importance to 
noise, and mixed-frequency data —such as daily 
market prices alongside monthly macroeconomic 
indicators—make it difficult to build a consistent 
explanatory framework. Furthermore, most XAI 
methods offer only correlational rather than causal 
explanations, which limits their value for financial 
decision-making. Current approaches include 
intrinsically interpretable models like LassoNet, post-
hoc methods such as SHAP, and hybrid architectures 
such as LSTM-XGBoost, all aiming to balance 
predictive accuracy and transparency. Nevertheless, 
temporal dependence, regime shifts, and high noise 
remain major challenges. XAI therefore needs to 
develop explanation methods specifically designed 
for time-dependent data and extend evaluation across 
a wider range of financial contexts, market regimes, 
and asset classes before it can be considered a reliable 
part of financial decision-making. 
2.2 Intrinsic Interpretability vs. Post-Hoc 
Explainability: An Ongoing Debate 
Within the area of Explainable Artificial Intelligence 
(XAI) there exists a basic division concerning the way 
in which interpretability should be achieved. On one 
side, there is the view that intrinsically interpretable 
models should be used —meaning models in which 
interpretability is built right from the start—while on the 
other side it is argued that post-hoc explanation methods 
should be employed, involving the initial training of a 
high-performance "black-box" model and then the 
application of various tools to explain the decisions of 
that model. Madsen et al. [38] describe these as two 
distinct approaches: the intrinsic approach, which holds 
that only models intentionally designed to be 
interpretable can be interpreted, and the post-hoc 
approach, which asserts that black-box models can also 
be explained. This section examines the main 
arguments, empirical evidence, and the developing 
consensus, focusing on their relevance to significant 
financial decisions. It concludes by examining methods 
that go beyond this simple dichotomy. 
The idea of intrinsic interpretability is to build 
interpretability directly into the model from the start, 
rather than adding it on afterwards. Zschech et al. [39] 
refer to this as "inherently interpretable machine 
learning", meaning that the decision logic can be 
understood on the basis of the architecture itself and 
not with the aid of any external tools. Models in this 
category include linear and logistic regression, 
decision trees, rule lists, Generalized Additive 
Models, and LassoNet. Shu et al. [40] demonstrate 
that an intrinsically interpretable model for predicting 
the shear strength of reinforced concrete beams can 
achieve accuracy comparable to black-box methods. 
The main benefit of intrinsic models is their 
faithfulness. Because the decision logic is built 
directly into the model’s structure, there is no need to 
translate or approximate the model’s behavior, and the 
explanation is complete rather than only local. Yet 
this disadvantage shows itself when models are 
employed for complicated tasks such as forecasting 
financial time series, since simple models generally 
fail to capture non-linear patterns and long-range 
dependencies, resulting in the usual compromise 
between accuracy and interpretability. However, 
Rudin [26] challenges this view, arguing that the 
accuracy-interpretability trade-off is often a false 
dilemma; in many high-stakes situations, carefully 
designed interpretable models can match the accuracy 
of black-box models. Her main point is that 
researchers should develop inherently interpretable 
models rather than generating explanations afterward, 
especially since unreliable post hoc explanations in 
fields such as medical diagnosis, criminal justice, or 
financial risk management can have serious 
consequences. 
Post-hoc explainability is based on the idea of 
"predict first, then explain": once a high-performance 
black-box model has been used, a variety of 
explanation techniques can show how the model 
reaches its conclusions. This includes methods like 
feature attribution (for example, SHAP, LIME, and 
Integrated Gradients), gradient- and propagation-
based techniques such as Saliency Maps [41] and 
Grad-CAM [42], surrogate models [43] which either 
locally or globally approximate the black box, 
counterfactual explanations, and rule extraction. The 
main benefit of these methods is their flexibility: they 
can be combined with any black-box model, allowing 
researchers to use deep learning representational 
power for tasks such as financial forecasting without 
sacrificing accuracy at the design stage, at least in 
theory. 
Another limitation is that feature attribution should 
not be taken as implying causation. Fu et al. [44] show 
this difference by combining explainable AI with 
causal inference techniques in an investigation of 
environmental drivers, demonstrating the advantage 
of supplementing attribution-based explanations with 
approaches that examine causal relationships. This 
point is especially important in high-stakes 
applications, since a predictor that has a significant 
influence and is identified by an XAI method does not 
have to be seen as one whose change would alter the 
predicted outcome. 
The second point is instability, since minor alterations 
to the input can result in large changes to the 
explanation output. Thirdly, it is possible to generate 
spurious or unjustified explanations [27], which 
5 

demonstrate that counterfactual explanations can be 
disconnected from the true data distribution and 
cannot be derived from the actual data points by a 
continuous path; the authors regard this risk as 
’substantial’ in a number of datasets and stress that it 
actually leads to the creation of mathematically 
plausible but meaningless ’phantom explanations’. 
Fourthly, evaluation is difficult: although Munoz et 
al. [45] have suggested six explainer-independent 
metrics for measuring explainability, the need to 
propose them shows that the community has not yet 
reached consensus on evaluation standards. 
The situation is especially serious when the stakes are 
high. Laugel et al. [27] argue that black-box models, 
even with post-hoc explanations, should be avoided 
when decisions affect people’s lives, freedom, or 
financial security, since such explanations might not 
accurately reflect how the model behaves, could fail 
to show how it goes wrong, or might reveal spurious 
correlations. As a result, auditability and 
accountability weaken in such sensitive applications. 
Mersha et al. [46] make the same point, emphasizing 
that XAI should promote transparency, 
accountability, and fairness rather than simply serving 
as a technical add-on. Yet empirical evidence shows 
that neither intrinsic nor post-hoc methods are always 
the better option. Ai and Ramasamy [47] find that 
intrinsic explanations are generally more informative 
and reliable for product search. In contrast, Madathil 
et al. [48] think intrinsic methods are better for 
assessing process robustness in smart manufacturing, 
while post hoc methods better explain complex 
nonlinear relationships. Chakraborty et al. [49] come 
to the same conclusion, saying the right approach 
depends on the specific task and context. 
They also discuss the alleged trade-off between 
predictive accuracy and interpretability. Laugel et al. 
[27] argue that this compromise is generally 
overemphasized and mainly stems from researchers’ 
preference for black-box models rather than from any 
inherent flaw in interpretable models. Meanwhile, 
critics note that areas such as financial time series 
involve nonlinear dynamics, temporal dependence, 
and long-range interactions that simpler models may 
struggle to handle. In such cases, performance gains 
from architectures like LSTMs and Transformers 
could be real. The key point is not whether 
interpretability or accuracy should always take 
priority, but how to balance them given the 
application’s specific requirements. In fields that are 
regulated, such as credit scoring, transparency may be 
essential, while in quantitative trading, even small 
improvements in predictive performance can have 
significant economic value [23, 25, 50]. 
As a result, the debate between intrinsic and post-hoc 
interpretability should not be seen as a simple choice 
between transparency and accuracy. A more useful 
question is whether the extra predictive power of a 
more complex model warrants the associated loss of 
direct interpretability and the extra uncertainty 
introduced by post hoc explanations. This matters 
especially in credit risk assessment, where 
transparency, justification, and auditability are 
essential to the decision-making process, even though 
more sophisticated nonlinear models can still provide 
predictive benefits. The growing body of evidence 
thus supports a comparative approach rather than 
making a priori assumptions: intrinsically 
interpretable and post-hoc explainable models should 
be compared not only on the basis of their predictive 
performance but also with regard to the nature, 
complexity, and reliability of the explanations they 
offer [23, 25, 33, 50, 51]. This kind of approach goes 
beyond the idea that greater model complexity 
automatically means either better predictive 
performance or worse practical interpretability and 
instead treats the relationship between predictive 
ability and interpretability as an empirical issue. 
2.3 Benchmarking and Metrics for Explanation 
Quality 
A major gap in the explainable AI (XAI) literature is 
the lack of standardized benchmarks for comparing 
explanation techniques. To address this gap, several 
recent efforts have emerged. Chakraborty et al. [49] 
developed a comprehensive benchmarking 
framework used to study both intrinsic and post hoc 
XAI methods on three typical 3D datasets. They 
evaluate explanations quantitatively using the 
Correctness (AOPC), Completeness (AUPC), and 
Compactness criteria, applying them consistently 
across datasets. Most importantly, the results indicate 
that explanation quality varies greatly across methods 
and domains, with no single approach performing 
best across all domains, underscoring the need for 
multi-metric evaluation. Statistical tests (Kruskal –
Wallis and Mann –Whitney U) show significant 
differences in method performance. This aligns with 
the approach adopted in the present study, which 
assesses the explainers using three complementary 
metrics—faithfulness, stability, and consistency —
rather than relying on a single criterion. 
Metsch and Hauschild [52] have developed 
BenchXAI, a new package for benchmarking XAI 
methods that enables detailed evaluation of 15 
different XAI techniques on biomedical tasks. Their 
evaluation found that Integrated Gradients, DeepLift, 
DeepLiftShap, and GradientShap performed well on 
all three tasks, while methods like Deconvolution and 
Guided Backpropagation did not. This again shows 
6 

that XAI performance varies by task and that 
systematic benchmarking is essential. 
Bodria et al. [53] apply this benchmarking approach 
to tabular, image, and text data and express the 
quantitative comparisons in terms of faithfulness, 
stability, robustness, and computational time. Their 
experiments with tabular data are especially pertinent 
to assessing feature-attribution methods. Although 
the local surrogate methods generally achieved high 
fidelity in approximating the underlying black-box 
models, the findings on faithfulness and stability 
differed considerably. SHAP showed greater 
faithfulness than LIME, and none of the methods 
examined was consistently stable when inputs 
changed slightly. The authors note that no existing 
objective metric can identify a single best explainer, 
since different metrics reflect different desirable 
characteristics. This difference matters because an 
explanation can accurately approximate the model’s 
outputs yet remain unstable under small 
perturbations, showing that fidelity and stability 
measure related but not equivalent aspects of 
explanation quality. 
Belaid et al. [54] have proposed Compare-xAI, a 
benchmark that combines a variety of functional 
testing approaches for post hoc XAI algorithms. This 
benchmark illustrates the difficulty of evaluating XAI 
methods through a hierarchical scoring system 
designed for three groups of end users: researchers, 
practitioners, and the general public. The tests are 
divided into five categories —fidelity, fragility, 
stability, simplicity, and stress tests —with an overall 
comprehensibility score at the top level. This multi-
dimensional evaluation method aligns with the 
present study’s use of complementary metrics. 
Bragança and Souto [55] have introduced the Unified 
Time Series Classification Framework for 
Explainable Artificial Intelligence (UTS-XAI), which 
combines a traditional classification pipeline with 
comprehensive, time-aware XAI evaluation metrics 
such as faithfulness, robustness, sensitivity, stability, 
and localization. The framework supports several 
explanation methods —specifically SHAP, LIME, 
and Saliency Maps —and offers a way to 
systematically compare them using adapted versions 
of well-established XAI metrics reinterpreted for 
temporal data. 
Consequently, explanation evaluation has gradually 
shifted from focusing on specifying individual 
desirable properties to developing systematic 
benchmarking procedures. The faithfulness criterion 
used in this study follows the approach of Samek et 
al. [56] which assesses whether the model’s output 
deteriorates as expected when important features are 
removed. The stability criterion is based on the 
principle put forward by Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola 
[57] that similar inputs should yield similar 
explanations; their analysis shows that many 
commonly used interpretability methods violate this 
requirement. Lastly, consistency assesses whether 
independently generated explanations provide 
compatible descriptions of the same model behavior, 
drawing on the disagreement framework introduced 
by Krishna et al. [58] and further supported by 
empirical work by Brughmans et al. [59]. 
Building on this foundation, explanation quality is 
defined by measurable criteria for faithfulness, 
stability, and consistency, and by specific acceptance 
thresholds based on empirical research on the latest 
methods. The suggested framework combines 
quantitative assessments with user-centered criteria, 
enabling both researchers and practitioners to judge 
whether an explanation meets the established 
standards. This formalization is particularly relevant 
to the present study, which takes faithfulness, 
stability, and consistency as its three main evaluation 
dimensions. 

3 Data and Methodology 
Fig. 1 summarizes the study design. Two features 
distinguish this work from existing explainer 
benchmarks: the same-model contrast in stage 3, 
which holds the trained network fixed while varying 
the explanation, and the decomposition of stability 
into three perturbations in stage 5, which holds the 
explanation fixed while varying what it must 
withstand. 
Five-day realized volatility is forecast for 20 equities 
across three markets (stage 1) from two feature 
representations chosen to contrast correlation 
structure rather than width — the nominally narrower 
panel spans more effective dimensions (stage 2). We 
estimate five model families under a purged rolling 
walk-forward split, each paired with the explainer 
appropriate to its architecture (stage 3). One arm is 
treated differently: we evaluate a single trained 
TabNet network using both its intrinsic attention 
mask and post hoc Integrated Gradients. Hence, any 
divergence between the two is attributable to the 
explanation method rather than to the model, a 
premise verified by checking that no attribution call 
alters the network’s state. A cell enters the 
interpretability analysis only if it improves on step-5 
persistence out-of-sample (stage 4). We then evaluate 
admitted explanations for faithfulness against a 
random-order control, stability under three 
perturbations that differ in what each fixes, and 
agreement between two explainers of the same 
network, with all comparisons made using matched-
stock paired inference (stage 5).

The technical indicator (technical) panel is presented 
on a scale-free basis; the moving averages are shown 
as the ratio of price to the average rather than the 
average itself. The MACD and ATR are adjusted to 
the price at the same time, and signed volume flow is 
used as a rolling standardized score rather than a 
cumulative total over the whole sample. This method 
matters because it standardizes features within each 
training window. Such representations are well 
established in the literature on return and volatility 
prediction and are not arbitrary choices. In large-scale 
cross-sectional studies —for example, those carried 
out by Krauss et al. [60] —lagged-return panels are 
used as the only predictor set, and in the LSTM 
sequence models of Fischer and Krauss [61] as well, 
both trained on the entire S&P 500 cross-section. 
Technical indicator panels are among the main 
features in broader machine-learning asset-pricing 
studies, such as those of Gu et al. [62]. In the present 
study, Christensen et al. [1] use machine learning to 
forecast realized volatility using lagged 
autoregressive predictors, similar to those used in the 
price-lags representation described here. 
A precedent alone is not enough to explain why the 
two panels differ by about a factor of two in nominal 
width. This decision is deliberate and departs from the 
goal of maximizing forecasting accuracy. The main 
aim is to examine explainer behavior rather than attain 
state-of-the-art predictive performance. To this end, 
the two panels contrast a narrow, nearly orthogonal 
representation with a nominally wider but highly 
collinear one. 

The following three limitations of this framework 
must be made clear. Firstly, the two panels vary not 
only in width and correlation structure but also in the 
types of features used: raw autoregressive lags versus 
engineered, mutually correlated indicators. Since 
these different aspects are mixed, none of the results 
below can be attributed solely to the number of 
features. Given that effective dimensionality is 
inversely related to nominal width, the comparison is 
always made in terms of the correlation structure 
rather than dimensionality. Second, the two panels are 
fed into separately trained models, so cross-panel 
comparisons reflect differences between two model –
representation combinations rather than a single 
model assessed under two different data views. Only 
in the within-panel comparisons, where two 
explainers are applied to a single trained network, is 
the model fixed. Thirdly, the price-lag panel contains 
only signed return lags and excludes absolute returns, 
squared returns, or lagged realized variances. This 
omission is necessary because lagged realized 
variances are highly autocorrelated and would 
introduce the collinearity this part of the study is 
intended to avoid. As a result, the panel provides only 
limited direct information on volatility. As shown in 
Section 5, an HAR-RV benchmark based on the 
excluded lagged realized variances attains the highest 
median accuracy in the study. The design therefore 
sacrifices some predictive information to enable a 
controlled comparison of correlation structures, and it 
makes no assertion about state-of-the-art accuracy.

3.3 Models and Explanation Methods 
We evaluate five model families and two post-hoc 
explainers, pairing each model with the explainer or 
explainers appropriate to its architecture. Three of the 
five—the GAM, the EBM, and TabNet —have 
intrinsic explanations, and only TabNet is 
additionally paired with a post-hoc explainer, making 
it the only architecture on which we can compare an 
intrinsic and a post-hoc account of an identical trained 
network. This is a deliberate scope restriction, not a 
claim that the additive models admit no post-hoc 
explanation: both the GAM and the EBM could be 
explained by kernel-based Shapley attribution of the 
fitted object, which would yield three same-model 
contrasts instead of one. We record the omission as a 
limitation in Section 6.7 and as an immediate 
extension in the future directions in Section 6.7. 
3.3.1 Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) 
XGBoost is a gradient-boosted decision-tree 
algorithm that sequentially constructs trees to 
minimize prediction error while controlling model 
complexity through regularization. In this study, 
XGBoost was configured with 300 trees, a learning 
rate of 0.03, subsampling of 0.8 for observations and 
features, L2 regularization, and a minimum child 
weight of 5. Histogram-based tree construction was 
used to improve computational efficiency. 
3.3.2 Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) 
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is a recurrent 
neural-network architecture designed to capture 
temporal dependencies through gated memory 
mechanisms. The model used in this study consisted 
of a single LSTM layer with 32 hidden units followed 
by a linear regression output layer. Models were 
trained for 100 epochs using Adam optimization with 
a learning rate of ૚૙−૜and a batch size of 256. Input 
sequences were constructed using rolling 20-day 
windows from the standardized feature data. 
3.3.3 Generalized Additive Model (GAM) 
GAM is an intrinsic model of the form ݕ(݃ ̂) = ߚ0 +
݂1(ݔ1) + ݂2(ݔ2) + ⋯ + ݂௣(ݔ௣), where each is a 
smooth, learnable shape function, often splines or 
regression trees, and is a link function. Because the 
model simply adds separate contributions from each 
feature, we can plot and inspect each feature’s learned 
curve directly, with no additional explanation needed. 
This transparency is the hallmark of intrinsic 
explainability, but it doesn’t automatically capture 
feature interactions. We built a regression model that 
draws a smooth, slightly flexible curve for every 
single input feature. We restricted the curves so they 
can bend a little bit (6 basis splines) but applied a 
mathematical penalty (λ = 0.6) to keep th em from 
getting too jagged. Because the model simply adds up 
these individual curves, we can look at a graph of any 
single feature and see exactly how it drives the 
prediction. 
3.3.4 TabNet 
TabNet is a neural-network architecture for tabular 
data that uses sequential attention to perform 
instance-specific feature selection. The model was 
configured with decision and attention dimensions of 
࢔ࢊ࢔= ࢇ= ૡ , three decision steps, and a relaxation 
parameter of =ࢽ ૚. ૜ and was trained for 50 epochs. 
We standardized the regression target during training 
and transformed it back to its original scale for 
prediction. TabNet serves as the primary same-model 
comparison in this study: we explained each trained 
network using both its intrinsic attention mask and 
post-hoc Integrated Gradients, enabling us to assess 
differences in explanation behavior while holding the 
predictive model fixed. 
3.3.5 Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM) 
EBM extends the GAM by using gradient boosting to 
learn each univariate shape function, and in its general 
form can also learn a small number of automatically 
selected pairwise interaction terms, giving g(ŷ) = β₀ 
+ Σ fᵢ(xᵢ) + Σ fᵢⱼ (xᵢ, xⱼ) . In this study, interactions are 
disabled (‘interactions = 0‘), so the fitted model is 
purely additive: g(ŷ) = β₀ + Σ fᵢ(xᵢ). Each component 
is directly visualizable as a curve, so the model 
remains intrinsically interpretable while often 
achieving accuracy competitive with black-box 
learners. We use a single outer bag to keep training 
time comparable across arms. Because it’s purely 
additive, we can examine the trees for any single 
feature to see its global effect, and we can also break 
down any individual prediction to see exactly how 
much each feature contributed to that result. 
3.3.6 TreeSHAP 
TreeSHAP is a model-specific implementation of 
SHAP for tree-based models that computes feature 
contributions using the internal structure of the fitted 
tree ensemble. Based on Shapley values, it attributes 
each prediction to individual input features relative to 
a reference value. In this study, TreeSHAP was 
applied to the fitted XGBoost models to obtain 
feature-attribution vectors for subsequent faithfulness 
and stability analyses 
3.3.7 Integrated Gradients (IG) 
Integrated Gradients (IG) is a gradient-based post-hoc 
attribution method for differentiable models. It 
estimates feature contributions by integrating the 
gradient of the model output along a path from a 
reference baseline to the observed input. In this study, 
IG was applied to both the LSTM and TabNet models.

importance scores, whereas for TabNet, IG was

4 Experimental Design 
4.1 Walk-Forward Evaluation Protocol 
Accuracy and interpretability are evaluated using a 
purged rolling walk-forward split to prevent look-
ahead leakage (Fig. 2 Each fold uses a fixed 756-
trading-day training block (approximately three 
years), of which 746 rows are fitted after purging, a 
63-day test window (approximately one quarter), and 
advances by 126 trading days. Because the target at 
time (t) depends on returns from (t+1) to (t+h), we 
remove the final (h+5=10) observations from each 
training block before fitting to ensure training labels 
do not extend into the test period. Without purging, 
five rows per fold, or approximately 0.7% of the 756-
row block, would be contaminated. We estimate 
feature-standardization parameters (μ and σ) only 
from the purged training window and apply them to 
both training and test data. This procedure yields 14 
folds per ticker for the price-lag and HAR feature sets 
and 12 for the technical-indicator set for US and 
European firms. For Japanese firms, the 
corresponding numbers are 13 and 11 because of the 
shorter Tokyo trading calendar. We therefore report fold 
counts separately for each cell. 
4.2 Accuracy Metrics and the Skill Gate 
We evaluate predictive accuracy using RMSE, 
sMAPE, and relative mean absolute error (RelMAE) 
against a naive baseline computed on the same test 
observations. For the five-day volatility horizon, the 
baseline is step-(h)persistence, 
ŷ௧ = ݕ௧−ℎ, rather than day-to-day persistence. 
Because adjacent five-day rolling volatility targets 
overlap substantially, a one-day persistence 
benchmark would have artificially low error. 
Matching the baseline lag to the forecast horizon 
therefore provides a more appropriate comparison. 
RelMAE is defined as the model’s out -of-sample 
MAE divided by the step-(h) persistence MAE on the 
same rows; thus, RelMAE < 1 indicates out-of-
sample improvement over persistence. RelMAE is 
preferred to MASE because MASE scales out-of-
sample error by an in-sample naive error, which does 
not directly establish whether the model beats the 
baseline out of sample and may yield different 
conclusions across volatility regimes.

4.3 Explanation-Quality Metrics 
We compute interpretability metrics for every cell, 
regardless of skill- gate admission, using each fold’s 
trained model and corresponding attribution vector. 
This lets us measure the skill gate’s influence on 
interpretability rankings directly. We calculate 
metrics at the fold level before averaging because 
using a single attribution vector averaged across folds 
would systematically disadvantage models whose 
explanations change over time. 
4.3.1 Faithfulness 
We measure faithfulness using area over the 
perturbation curve (AOPC) with most-relevant-first 
(MoRF) sequential ablation. We rank features by 
importance and cumulatively set them to zero, using 
the standardized training mean, starting with the most 
important feature. For the technical-indicator panel, 
we ablate entire collinear groups—trend, momentum, 
volatility, and volume—by group importance to avoid 
arbitrary rankings among near-duplicate indicators. 
RMSE is recalculated after each step. AOPC is the 
average RMSE increase over the top eight ablation 
steps, or over all six features for the price-lag panel; 
we also report a matched-depth result at (k=5). We 
additionally compare MoRF with reverse-order 
ablation and with a random-order control using the 
same model. Because a near-zero AOPC may indicate 
either an uninformative ranking or general model 
insensitivity to ablation, we interpret faithfulness 
relative to the random-order AOPC. We consider an 
explanation more faithful only when its AOPC 
exceeds this control. 
4.3.2 Stability 
Three perturbation settings are evaluated. Input-noise 
stability keeps the fitted model fixed while adding 
Gaussian noise to the test data at σ = 8% of each 
feature’s standard deviation, repeated 20 times. We 
generate independent, reproducible random streams 
deterministically from the ticker, experiment 
identifier, and fold using a cryptographic hash. 
Temporal stability compares importance rankings 
across adjacent walk-forward folds after refitting the 
model, capturing changes caused by regime shifts 
and/or estimation noise. Seed stability refits the same 
model on identical data and folds using different 
random seeds, isolating variability attributable to the 
optimization path. We assess agreement under all 
three settings using Spearman rank correlation, rank-
biased overlap (RBO), and top-(k) Jaccard overlap at 
both fixed and feature-scaled depths, interpreting 
results relative to expected agreement under 
independent random rankings. 
4.3.3 Consistency 
For TabNet, we compare intrinsic masks (E7, E8) 
with post-hoc Integrated Gradients (E9, E10) using 
the same trained model and the same agreement 
metrics applied to mean per-fold importance vectors 
for each ticker. Because E7/E9 and E8/E10 share 
identical fitted models, and attribution does not alter 
model parameters, observed disagreement reflects the 
explanation method rather than differences in 
predictive architecture. 

12 

4.3.4 Size-Corrected Agreement 
Fixed-depth top-(k) overlap is not directly 
comparable across panels with different numbers of 
features. For example, with (n=6) and (k=5), the 
Jaccard index has a minimum of 0.667 and an 
expected random value of 0.7222, whereas for the 13-
feature panel the expected value is 0.2537. To remove 
this panel-width effect, we additionally report Jaccard 
overlap at a feature-scaled depth, an excess-over-null 
normalization, (observed - expected)/(1 - expected), 
and RBO, which evaluates the full ranking with 
greater weight on top positions. Expected Jaccard 
values are derived analytically, whereas expected 
RBO is estimated by Monte Carlo simulation. Cross-
panel conclusions are based on these size-invariant 
measures; raw fixed-depth values are retained only 
for comparison. 
4.4 Statistical Testing 
The main analysis uses a matched-ticker paired 
design in which each ticker contributes one value for 
each arm within a given metric and feature panel. For 
each pairwise comparison, we include only tickers 
with results for both arms, and we evaluate paired 
differences using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and a 
two-sided sign-flip permutation test on the mean 
difference. We include the permutation test because it 
can be more reliable than the signed-rank test with 
small sample sizes. We report effect size using a 
matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation, which 
summarizes the balance of wins and losses across 
tickers. Pairing is essential because substantial ticker-
specific variation could otherwise obscure differences 
attributable to the explainers, particularly when 
comparing intrinsic and post-hoc explanations 
derived from the same fitted network. Two secondary 
analyses assess robustness to incomplete overlap 
among admitted tickers. First, because different 
explainers may qualify on different ticker sets, we 
apply a Mann–Whitney U test to all admitted cells as 
an unpaired sensitivity analysis. Second, where 
sufficient complete blocks are available —that is, 
tickers for which all arms in a comparison group are 
simultaneously admitted —we use a Friedman test 
followed by Nemenyi post-hoc comparisons as an 
additional robustness check. We report the number of 
matched pairs for every comparison, and we classify 
analyses with fewer than five pairs as underpowered 
and do not interpret them as evidence of no difference. 
For pairwise explanation comparisons, we Holm-
adjust p-values within each metric-feature-set family, 
then apply a second Holm correction across all 
families. 
5 Results 
5.1 Sample, splits, and the skill gate 
The study contains 12 explained arms (E1-E12) plus 
three unexplained reference forecasts (B0-B2). 
Because E7/E9 and E8/E10 reuse the same fitted 
TabNet predictions for two different explainers, these 
correspond to 13 unique predictive configurations, or 
260 stock-level fitted configurations across 20 
equities, but 15 arm/reference result series, or 300 
stock-level result rows. All predictive configurations 
trained without error. Walk-forward validation 
produced 14 folds per stock for the price-lag and HAR 
feature sets and 12 for the technical-indicator set on 
the US and European names, and 13 and 11 
respectively on the five Japanese names, reflecting the 
shorter Tokyo trading calendar over 2016-2025. The 
technical-indicator arms lose two folds relative to 
their price-lag counterparts because the volume-flow 
normalization requires a 252-day warm-up; fold 
counts are therefore reported for each cell. Each 
training block is purged of the h + embargo = 10 
terminal rows whose labels depend on returns inside 
the subsequent test window. Without the purge, five 
training rows per fold, approximately 0.7% of a 756-
row block, would carry contaminated labels. 
The skill gate admits a cell if its out-of-sample 
relative MAE against step-5 persistence, computed on 
identical test rows, falls below one. The gate admitted 
165 of 240 eligible cells (68.8%) (Table 3). We report 
the significance of the per-cell skill advantage rather 
than require it: the gate is a screening step separating 
models with forecasting skill from those without, not 
a confirmatory test, and both the raw and Benjamini–
Hochberg-adjusted one-sided fold-paired p-values 
accompany every cell. Requiring per-cell adjusted 
significance at q ≤ 0.05 would admit only 6% of cells 
and would eliminate both same-model comparisons 
entirely; results under a stricter q ≤ 0.10 tier are 
reported as a sensitivity analysis in 5.7. The 
persistence reference (B1) returns a relative MAE of 
exactly 1.000 on all 20 stocks by construction, serving 
as an end-to-end check that the gate’s numerator and 
denominator align on the same rows. 
Two patterns are visible. Every technical-indicator 
arm is admitted less often than its price-lag twin. The 
recurrent arm is the weakest in the study: IG/LSTM 
on technical indicators admits no stock at all. The gate 
admits US cells at roughly half the rate of the other 
two markets (Table 4). Because of where the gate is 
defined, the admitted sample is close to balanced 
across markets —59 US, 52 European, and 54 
Japanese cells —whereas the universe was designed 
with ten US names against five in each of the other 
two regions.
