Introduction: Electron beam (eBeam) technology is increasingly being adopted 
in biomedical research and industry as an alternative to cobalt-60-based gamma 
irradiation. However, a comprehensive understanding of how eBeam energy and 
dose influence viral inactivation and the preservation of viral surface antigens 
remains limited. This study compared the inactivation kinetics of human rotavirus 
(RV), human respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and influenza A virus (IAV) following 
exposure to high-energy electron beam (HEEB; 10 MeV), medium-energy elec-
tron beam (MEEB; 4.5 MeV), and gamma irradiation under conditions that were 
standardized to the greatest extent possible. Additionally, antigenic characteriza-
tion was performed for HEEB and MEEB inactivated viruses.
Methods: Human rotavirus, human respiratory syncytial virus, and influenza A 
virus were irradiated using HEEB, MEEB and gamma irradiation using incremental 
doses under standardized experimental conditions. Viral inactivation kinetics (D10) 
were determined using TCID50 assays while preservation of viral antigenicity and 
virus–host receptor interactions were evaluated by hemagglutination and ELISA 
assays. Heat and formalin inactivation were used as experimental controls.
Results: All three modalities effectively inactivated the tested viruses with compa-
rable overall inactivation efficiency. The D10 values of RV (1.23–1.29 kGy) and RSV 
(1.03–1.73 kGy) did not differ significantly (p = 0.01) among the irradiation modal-
ities. The IAV exhibited greater radioresistance, with D 10 value ranging between 
1.62–2.27 kGy. Complete inactivation by both high- and medium-energy eBeam 
was confirmed by three serial passages. Importantly, eBeam-treated viruses 
retained viral antigenicity (VP4, F-protein, and HA) at levels comparable to 
untreated controls, whereas heat and formalin treatments reduced antigenicity 
by 5.09- to 256-fold and 1.26- to 2-fold, respectively. Furthermore, eBeam-inac-
tivated viruses exhibited 1.54- to 23.11-fold greater virus-host receptor recogni-
tion than heat and formalin inactivated preparations.
Discussion: Both high-energy and medium-energy electron beam modalities 
achieved viral inactivation efficiencies comparable to gamma irradiation under 
standardized conditions, supporting their potential as non-radioisotopic alter -
natives for viral inactivation. Importantly, eBeam treatment preserved viral anti -
genicity and functional virus–host receptor interactions more effectively than 
conventional heat and formalin inactivation methods. These findings highlight 
the potential of eBeam technology as a platform for producing inactivated viral 
preparations for vaccine development and other biopharmaceutical applications.

1 Introduction
Ionizing radiation technologies are extensively employed across 
medical, industrial, agricultural, environmental and biotechnological 
sectors owing to their ability to achieve effective microbial inactivation 
(Bhatia and Pillai, 2022; Skrobarczyk et al., 2022). The high penetra-
tion ability enables pathogen elimination within sealed packages and 
complex matrices, making them ideal for medical device sterilization, 
decontamination of biologics and vaccine development (Suljovrujic et 
al., 2024). Conventional inactivation strategies including thermal-
inactivation (heat, steam), and chemical inactivation (formalin, beta-
propiolactone, ethylene oxide gas) require longer incubation time and 
often damage structural proteins essential for immunogenicity 
(Delgado et al., 2009; Skrobarczyk et al., 2022). Chemical inactivation 
with formaldehyde introduces covalent crosslinks between amino acid 
residues, altering antigenic epitopes, while thermal inactivation 
induces global protein denaturation through disruption of non-cova-
lent interactions maintaining tertiary structure (Murphy and Walsh, 
1988; Butreddy et al., 2021). In contrast, ionizing radiation preferen-
tially targets nucleic acids over structural proteins, achieving complete 
pathogen inactivation while preserving the native structural antigen 
conformation. This serves as a critical attribute for developing effective 
vaccines and immunomodulators (Buxton et al., 1988; Jonah et al., 
1990; Fertey et al., 2020; Bhatia and Pillai, 2022; Perera et al., 2025; 
Praveen et al., 2025).
Among ionizing radiation modalities, gamma and electron 
beam (eBeam) represent the two principal technologies employed 
for sterilization and pathogen inactivation. Gamma radiation is 
generated by the decay of radioactive isotopes primarily cobalt-60 
(60Co) or Cesium-137 ( 137Cs). Cobalt-60 emits gamma photons of 
two different energies of 1.17 MeV and 1.33 MeV (million electron 
volts) and emits low energy beta rays (electrons) with a maximum 
energy of 0.32 MeV ( Cleland, 2006 ; Munir and Federighi, 2020 ). 
Electron beam is generated using machine-based electron accel -
erators such as linear accelerator (LINACs) or Rhodotrons operat -
ing using commercial electricity without any radioactive isotopes. 
Both gamma and eBeam inactivates pathogen through direct 
DNA/RNA damage with energetic electrons and via indirect 
damage to nucleic acids mediated primarily by the radiolytic 
products such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH), hydrogen peroxide 
(H2O2), aqueous electrons (e −aq), and hydrogen atom (•H) ( Buxton 
et al., 1988 ; Jonah et al., 1990 ). While the underlying radiochem -
istry is shared, these modalities differ fundamentally in dose rate, 
particle energy, and penetration characteristics. Gamma photons 
exhibit deep penetration capability depending upon the product 
geometry and configuration making this modality a historical 
benchmark for sterilization of dense and heterogenous products 
in sealed final packaging ( ISO 11137-2, 2013 ). However, gamma 
processing faces inherent constraints arising from the physics of 
radioactive decay. The delivered dose is governed by source activ -
ity, source-to product distance, facility design and source configu -
ration, resulting in relatively low dose rates, typically ranging from 
0.03 to 12 Gy/s ( Tallentire et al., 2010 ). These low dose rates 
necessitate prolonged exposure time, often hours to achieve higher 
doses. Conversely, eBeam systems deliver equivalent doses at rates 
of 102–104 Gy/s, achieving complete inactivation within seconds. 
Furthermore, concerns related to radioactive source security, 
regulatory compliance, source replenishment, and long-term sus -
tainability have increasingly challenged the widespread adoption 
of gamma irradiation technology ( Bhatia and Pillai, 2022 ; Praveen 
et al., 2025 ).
Electron beam technology is available in multiple energy con -
figurations, suited for specific applications based on penetration 
ability and product geometry. High Energy Electron Beam (HEEB) 
operates at 5–10 MeV and provide higher penetration compared to 
Medium Energy Electron Beam (MEEB) that operates at 1–5 MeV 
energy and widely used for food irradiation and medical device ster -
ilization purposes. Comparatively, Low Energy Electron Beam 
(LEEB) (<1 MeV , typically 80–300 keV) achieves mostly surface 
level penetration and used for surface decontamination and mate -
rial modification applications. All eBeam modalities have shown to 
effectively inactivate microbial pathogens under specific treatment 
parameters and material configurations suitable to uniformly 
deliver doses across the samples (Predmore et al., 2015; Fertey et al., 
2020; Skrobarczyk et al., 2022 ). Electron beam modalities have sig -
nificant operational advantages over gamma such as precise dose 
control, instant switch on/ off capability, elimination of radioactive 
isotope use, positioning eBeam as a sustainable alternative 
to gamma.
An effective viral inactivation method must ensure complete 
and irreversible loss of viral infectivity ( Bonnafous et al., 2014 ). 
While chemical inactivation with formaldehyde or β-propiolactone 
achieves this goal, it has been shown to alter essential antigenic pro -
teins in respiratory syncytial virus ( Delgado et al., 2009 ), influenza 
A virus (Bonnafous et al., 2014), Japanese encephalitis virus (Fan et 
al., 2015), and poliovirus ( Wilton et al., 2014 ). Ionizing radiation 
offers a distinct advantage by achieving complete inactivation while 
preserving antigenic surface proteins. This feature has been studied 
using gamma irradiation for influenza H3N2 ( Salehi et al., 2018 ), 
rotavirus (Shahrudin et al., 2018 ), RSV (Chen et al., 2023 ), rabies 
virus ( Franka et al., 2009 ), Zika virus ( Sintara et al., 2025 ), and 
SARS-CoV-2 (Turan et al., 2021). Similarly, different eBeam modali-
ties have shown viral inactivation and antigen preservation such as 
in case of 200 keV LEEB for influenza H3N8 (Fertey et al., 2016); 10 
MeV HEEB for rotavirus ( Skrobarczyk et al., 2022 ) and HIV-2/
hepatitis A/pseudorabies/porcine parvovirus ( Schmidt et al., 2012); 
and 4.5 MeV MEEB for murine norovirus-1 and Tulane virus 
(Predmore et al., 2015 ). However, these studies differ substantially 
in experimental conditions including sample matrix, irradiation 
temperature, dosimetry systems, and pathogen type making it dif -
ficult to assess a cross-modality comparison of viral inactivation 
efficiency.
To date, no published study has directly compared eBeam modal-
ities (HEEB and MEEB) with gamma irradiation for viral inactivation

under identical experimental parameters. Such empirical data is 
essential if eBeam is to be widely adopted in any format. To address 
this critical gap, the present study systematically compared the viral 
inactivation kinetics of HEEB (10 MeV), MEEB (4.5 MeV), and 60Co 
gamma irradiation using three human viral pathogens representing 
diverse structural and genomic composition. Human rotavirus (RV , 
a non-enveloped double stranded RNA virus of family Reoviridae), 
human respiratory syncytial virus type A2 (RSV , an enveloped single 
stranded negative sense RNA virus of family Pneumoviridae) and 
influenza A/PR/8/34/H1N1 virus (IAV , an enveloped, segmented 
single stranded negative sense RNA virus of family 
Orthomyxoviridae) were used for inactivation studies. Critical 
parameters such as sample matrix (PBS), sample packaging shipping 
temperature (4 °C), and dosimetry (alanine/EPR) were maintained 
across all modalities. LEEB was excluded as alanine dosimetry is 
incompatible with low-energy electrons and appropriate biocontain-
ment during LEEB exposure was not feasible. The university’s bio-
safety guidelines did not allow for the direct exposure of the viral 
pathognes to LEEB in the laboratory setting without appropriate 
containment. Beyond inactivation kinetics, the functional character-
istics of eBeam inactivated viruses were assessed. The impact of 
HEEB and MEEB irradiation on antigenic protein preservation were 
compared to traditional heat and formalin-inactivation methods 
across all three viruses (Lelie et al., 1987; Fan et al., 2015; Hankaniemi 
et al., 2019). Antigenic preservation after gamma irradiation was not 
assessed, as this has already been well documented for various viruses 
Franka et al., 2009; Salehi et al., 2018; Shahrudin et al., 2018; Turan 
et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023; Sintara et al., 2025). We hypothesized 
that, when delivered at equivalent absorbed doses, high-energy (10 
MeV) and medium-energy (4.5 MeV) electron beam irradiation 
would achieve viral inactivation efficiencies comparable to cobalt-60 
gamma irradiation, while preserving viral antigenicity and virus–host 
receptor recognition more effectively than conventional heat- and 
formalin-based inactivation methods.
2 Materials and methods
2.1 Virus propagation
Human rotavirus (RV), human respiratory syncytial virus type A2 
(RSV) and influenza A/PR/8/34/H1N1 virus (IAV) were used in this 
study. RV , RSV and IAV were propagated in MA104 (African green 
monkey fetal kidney epithelial cells; ATCC CRL-2378.1), HEp-2 
(human laryngeal epidermoid carcinoma epithelial cells; ATCC CCL-
23), and Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK; canine renal epithelial 
cells; ATCC CCL-34) cell lines, respectively. The cells lines were main-
tained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; 
Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, NY) supplemented with 10% heat 
inactivated fetal bovine serum and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin 
(Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, NY). Virus activation and propaga-
tion were facilitated by TPCK-treated trypsin for RV and IAV at a final 
concentration of 1 μg/mL with all cultures maintained at 37 °C and 
5% CO₂ incubator. To obtain high-titer viral stocks, culture superna-
tants were harvested and concentrated using 50 kDa molecular weight 
cut-off (MWCO) filters (MilliporeSigma, Saint Louis, MO). The con-
centrated viruses were diluted in PBS to defined concentrations and 
titers were estimated by 50% Tissue Culture Infectious Dose (TCID₅₀) 
assay using Reed and Muench (1938) method. Concentrated viral 
stocks with defined titers were used for all the analyses in the experi-
mental workflow including (1) inactivation kinetics and D 10 value 
determination, (2) complete virus inactivation, (3) antigen preserva-
tion, and (4) virus–host interactions as illustrated in Figure 1.

and gamma irradiation

viral inactivation efficiency, three distinct ionizing radiation modali-
ties were employed under standard biological conditions: High energy

Defined titers of RV , RSV and IAV were packaged using a triple-

irradiation, and return to the TAMU laboratory for virus enumera-

Beam Research (NCEBR), Texas A&M University, College Station, TX 
using 10 MeV 15 kW S-band LINAC (L3 Pulse Sciences, Melbourne, 
FL). MEEB irradiation was conducted at 4.5 MeV utilizing the com-
mercial irradiation services of EBeam Services, Inc. (Cranbury, NJ) 
and gamma irradiation was performed using 60Co source (1.77 and 
1.33 MeV) at Steris AST (Libertyville, IL). The operational and physi-

summarized in Table 1. Defined titers of RV , RSV , and IAV were irra-
diated at incremental target doses of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 kGy across all 
three radiation modalities. The inactivation kinetics experiments were 
performed in two completely independent trials with triplicate sam-
ples for each target dose point for HEEB, MEEB and gamma radiation.
Absorbed doses were measured with calibrated alanine dosim -
eters (GEX, Palm City, FL) using Bruker E-Scan ESR spectrometer 
(Bruker, Billerica, MA). The dose uniformity ratio (DUR = Dmax/
Dmin) was determined for each irradiation configuration to con -
firm uniform dose delivery throughout the sample volume. DUR 
is a key parameter in radiation processing, as it indicates the uni -
formity of dose distribution throughout the entire sample and it 
is critically important in the determination of D 10 values. A DUR 
value close to 1.0 signifies that minimum and maximum absorbed 
doses across the package were nearly equivalent. Alanine/EPR 
dosimetry was selected for this comparative study due to its estab -
lished dose-rate independence, energy independence across the 
MeV range, minimal sensitivity to environmental variables, and 
long-term signal stability—properties essential for accurate cross-
modality dose comparison ( ISO/ASTM 51607, 2013 ). Post irradia -
tion, samples were aliquoted and tested with minimal delay 
between irradiation and biological analysis to study the inactiva -
tion efficiency.
2.3 Inactivation kinetics and D10 value 
determination
Inactivation kinetics were determined for RV , RSV , and IAV 
across all three radiation modalities (HEEB, MEEB, and gamma) 
to enable direct cross-modality comparison of viral radiosensitiv -
ity. Surviving infectious virus populations at each target dose 
point were quantified using the TCID₅₀ assay as described earlier 
and titers expressed as log 10 TCID 50/mL. Viral survival curves 
were constructed by plotting log 10 TCID 50/mL as a function of 
absorbed dose (kGy). The D 10 values were calculated as the nega -
tive reciprocal of the slope and reported as mean ± standard error 
(SE) derived from two independent experimental trials, each com -
prising three replicates per dose point.
2.4 Complete inactivation confirmation of 
eBeam inactivated viruses
To evaluate the complete viral inactivation, defined titers of 
viral suspensions were irradiated under both HEEB and MEEB 
modalities at lethal doses (target dose as 12, 15, 18, and 20 kGy). 
These doses were selected to exceed theoretical complete inactiva -
tion threshold calculated from D 10 values. Complete inactivation 
was verified through three consecutive serial passages on suscep -
tible cell monolayers ( WHO, 2011). About 2 mL of irradiated and 
control samples (representing ~40% of total irradiated volume) was 
inoculated onto 80–90% confluent cell monolayers of correspond -
ing cell line to monitor cytopathic effects (CPEs) using T75 cell 
culture flask. The loss of viral infectivity was tested in triplicate for 
each dose point. Regardless of CPE observation, the infected cells 
underwent a freeze–thaw cycle, after which entire culture superna -
tant was harvested, centrifuged and concentrated using 50 kDa 
MWCO (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO) filters to 200 μL and the 
entire volume passaged onto fresh cell monolayers. This serial pas -
sage process was repeated through three consecutive passages (P1, 
P2, and P3) for all HEEB and MEEB irradiated samples with three 
replicates at each dose point. Complete inactivation was defined as 
the absence of detectable CPE across all three serial passages in all 
three replicates at a given dose.
In parallel, chemical and thermal inactivation using formalin and 
heat, respectively, were performed to generate conventionally inacti-
vated viral preparations as comparative controls for subsequent analy-
ses. For chemical inactivation, formalin (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, 
MO; Cat. No. F1635-25) was used to inactivate all three tested viruses 
following established protocols. RV was treated with 2% (v/v) forma-
lin and incubated at 25 °C for 30 min, followed by 37 °C for 30 min 
(Skrobarczyk et al., 2022). RSV was inactivated using 0.025% (v/v) 
formalin at 37 °C for 96 h (Bayer et al., 2018) and IAV culture super-
natants were incubated with 0.1% (v/v) formalin at 4 °C for 16 h 
(Fertey et al., 2016). For thermal inactivation, all three viruses were 
exposed to 85 °C for 20 min (Bayer et al., 2018).

2.5 Antigenic preservation assessment of 
eBeam inactivated viruses
To assess whether eBeam inactivation preserves immunodomi-
nant surface proteins, the antigenic integrity of eBeam (HEEB and 
MEEB) inactivated viral preparations was compared against untreated 
(non-irradiated), heat inactivated and formalin inactivated controls. 
Key surface antigens- VP4 (RV), Fusion (F) protein (RSV), and hem-
agglutinin/HA (IAV) were quantified using indirect ELISA, direct 
ELISA, and hemagglutination assay (Bonneux et al., 2024; Kelleher et 
al., 2025).
2.5.1 VP4 antigenicity of rotavirus (indirect ELISA)
The integrity of VP4 outer capsid protein on eBeam inactivated 
RV was assessed by indirect ELISA. Fifty microliters (50 μL) of inac-
tivated virus preparations were mixed 1:1 (v/v) with carbonate–bicar-
bonate coating buffer (0.05 M, pH 9.6) and coated onto 96-well 
polystyrene ELISA plate. The plate was incubated overnight at 37 °C 
and subsequently washed with PBS (phosphate buffered saline) thrice 
to remove the unbound antigen. Unspecific binding sites in the wells 
were blocked with non-fat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature, fol-
lowed by three washing with PBST (phosphate buffered saline with 
0.05% Tween 20). A 100 μL solution of 1:1000 diluted rabbit poly-
clonal anti-VP4 antibody (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, United 
States; Cat. No. MBS7151952) was added to each well and incubated 
for 1 h, followed by three washing with PBST to remove unbound 
antibody. The antibody dilution was selected based on prior optimiza-
tion experiments using different dilutions (1:500, 1:1,000, 1:200) were 
tested to achieve best signal to noise ratio. HRP-conjugated goat anti-
rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Invitrogen ™, Thermo Fisher 
Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No. A16110) was added at a dilu-
tion of 1:1,000 and incubated for 1 h. After final three washes, TMB 
substrate was applied and incubated for 30 min, and the optical den-
sity was measured at 450 nm to determine VP4 antigenicity.
2.5.2 Fusion (F) protein antigenicity of RSV (direct 
ELISA)
The structural integrity of the RSV fusion (F) glycoprotein follow-
ing eBeam inactivation was assessed by direct ELISA. Fifty microliters 
(50 μL) of HEEB and MEEB inactivated viral preparations were mixed 
with coating buffer (1:1 ratio) and coated on 96-well microplates fol-
lowed by incubation at 37 °C overnight. To avoid non-specific bind-
ing, the wells were blocked with non-fat dry milk for 1 h at ambient 
temperature, followed by additional three washes using 
PBST. Subsequently, an HRP-conjugated anti-RSV-F rabbit antibody 
(Sino Biological, Beijing, China; Cat. No. 11049-R302-H) was added 
using optimized 1:2000 dilution and incubated for 60 min. Unbound 
antibodies were removed with three PBST washes, and then TMB 
substrate solution was added followed by stop solution. The optical 
density was measured at 450 nm.
2.5.3 Hemagglutinin (HA) antigenicity of IAV 
(hemagglutinin assay)
Preservation of the influenza HA protein following eBeam inacti-
vation was evaluated by the hemagglutination (HA) assay, which mea-
sures the functional capacity of HA to agglutinate erythrocytes. HEEB 
and MEEB-inactivated virus preparations (50 μL) were subjected to 
two-fold serial dilutions across 96 well V-bottom microtiter plates. 
Untreated (non-irradiated) virus served as a positive control, and PBS 
was used as the negative control. Fifty microliters of turkey erythro-
cyte suspension (0.5%) (v/v) were added to each well, and plates were 
incubated for 30 min at room temperature until the erythrocytes 
settled in the positive control well (Ali et al., 2018). The assay measures 
the ability of HA protein agglutination with the turkey’s red blood 
cells. Wells were assessed visually for the presence or absence of hem-
agglutination, and the HA titer was recorded as the reciprocal of the 
highest dilution exhibiting complete hemagglutination (WHO, 2011).
2.6 Virus-host interaction
To evaluate whether eBeam-inactivated viruses retain functional 
virus-host recognition, HEEB- and MEEB-inactivated preparations at 
lethal doses (12, 15, 18, and 20 kGy) were assessed for their ability to 
adsorb onto susceptible host cell monolayers and subsequently 
detected using virus-specific antibodies through cell-based ELISA. For 
this, 50 μL of the IAV and RSV were allowed to adsorb overnight on 
the 80–90% confluent MDCK and HEp-2 cells, respectively. Following 
incubation, non-adsorbed viruses were removed by gentle washing, 
and cells were fixed with chilled acetone (80%) to preserve cell mor-
phology and virus-receptor complexes. Fixed monolayers were 
blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature to 
prevent non-specific antibody binding, followed by three PBST 
washes. For HA detection, HRP conjugated anti-HA antibody, mouse 
monoclonal (Sino Biological Inc., Beijing, China; Cat. No. 11684-
MM03-H) was added in 1:2,000 dilution to MDCK cells. For F- pro-
tein detection, HRP-conjugated anti-RSV-F rabbit antibody (Sino 
Biological, Beijing, China; Cat. No. 11049-R302-H) was added at 
1:2,000 dilution to Hep-2 cells and incubated for 60 min. Subsequently, 
TMB substrate solution was added, and the optical density was mea-
sured to evaluate the binding of the irradiated virus to host receptors. 
For human rotavirus, HEEB and MEEB irradiated viruses were tested 
through indirect ELISA. In this procedure, 50 μL of the irradiated 
virus was plated onto MA104 cells and incubated overnight to allow 
viral attachment in the presence of TPCK trypsin (1 μg/mL). 
Afterward, the cells underwent antigen fixation, and blocking was 
performed as mentioned earlier. A primary anti-VP4 antibody 
(1:1,000 dilution) was then applied and incubated for 60 min, followed 
by three PBST washes. The HRP-labeled secondary antibody (1:1,000 
dilution) was added and incubated for an additional hour, after which 
the wells were washed three more times. Finally, TMB substrate was 
added, and the optical density was measured to determine the extent 
of viral attachment to host cell receptors.
2.7 Data analysis
The D10 values were determined from linear regression of virus 
surviving curves plotted as a function of absorbed doses and calcu-
lated as the negative reciprocal of the regression slope. Goodness of fit 
was assessed by R2 and D10 were reported as mean ± standard error 
from two independent trials each comprising of three replicates per 
dose point. For statistical comparisons across different radiation 
modalities, one-way analysis of variance (ANOV A) followed by 
Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was employed. Significance for D10 
comparisons across radiation modalities was defined at p < 0.01 to 
provide conservative interpretation of inter-modality differences.

3 Results
3.1 Virus inactivation kinetics and D10 value 
determination
To determine the effect of irradiation modalities on viral inactiva-
tion efficiency, inactivation kinetics was studied across HEEB, MEEB 
and gamma for three structurally and genomically diverse human 
viruses: RV , RSV and IAV . Linear regression analysis of log10 surviving 
fraction of viruses versus absorbed doses demonstrated first order 
inactivation kinetics for all the virus-radiation modality combination 
with R2 > 0.95 (Figure 2, SI 1). The D10 values representing the radia-
tion dose required for 90% virus inactivation across HEEB, MEEB and 
gamma radiation are summarized in Table 2.
Among the three viruses, RV was most radiosensitive with D10 
value of 1.23 ± 0.01 (gamma), 1.25 ± 0.25 (HEEB), and 1.29 ± 0.22 
kGy (MEEB), with no significant differences between modalities. 
Similarly, RSV exhibited D10 value of 1.03 ± 0.07 (gamma), 1.45 ± 0.02 
(HEEB), and 1.73 ± 0.23 kGy (MEEB) with no significant difference 
(p > 0.01) between radiation modalities. In contrast, IAV was found 
to be relatively radioresistant, with D10 values of 1.62 ± 0.003 (gamma), 
1.95 ± 0.04 (HEEB), and 2.27 ± 0.01 kGy (MEEB), with all pairwise 
comparisons significantly different for each of the radiation modality 
(Table 2). Results indicate that regardless of modality, the radiosensi-
tivity hierarchy remained consistent (RV > RSV > IAV), indicating 
that virus-intrinsic properties also contribute in radiation sensitivity. 
Modality specific parameters including dose rate and energy exerted 
a secondary and virus-dependent influence, with inter modality dif-
ference emerging only for the relatively radioresistant IAV .
3.2 Complete inactivation confirmation of 
eBeam inactivated viruses
Three consecutive cell culture passages of eBeam (HEEB and 
MEEB), heat, and formalin inactivated virus samples were performed 
to confirm the complete loss of infectivity of three viruses at each 
target dose (12 kGy, 15 kGy, 18 kGy, and 20 kGy) in triplicates. 
Complete inactivation was defined as the absence of detectable CPEs 
across all three serial passages in all three replicates at a given dose. 
Results from the HEEB and MEEB inactivation studies are summa-
rized in Table 3.
Both HEEB and MEEB achieved complete inactivation of all three 
viruses, however, the minimum dose required for complete inactiva-
tion varied depending upon the virus titer rather than modality. For 
HEEB, complete inactivation was confirmed at 18.17 kGy for RV (ini-
tial titer: 10.18 log10 TCID50/mL), 15.56 kGy for RSV (initial titer: 6.7 
log10 TCID50/mL), and 12.31 kGy for IAV (initial titer: 9.44 log 10 
TCID50/mL). For MEEB, complete inactivation was achieved at 19.81 
kGy for RV (initial titer: 9.44 log10 TCID50/mL), and 13.29 kGy for 
both RSV (initial titer: 8.74 log10 TCID50/mL) and IAV (initial titer: 
6.64 log10 TCID50/mL). Formalin and heat inactivation controls con-
firmed complete inactivation across all three passages for all viruses.
Notably, post-irradiation testing of human rotavirus (HEEB at 
12.3 kGy and MEEB at 13.29 kGy) yielded negative results in the first 
passage, however, infectivity was detected in the second passage. 
Additionally, RV samples irradiated with HEEB at 15.56 kGy and

3.3 Antigenic preservation assessment of 
eBeam inactivated viruses
Across all three viruses, HEEB and MEEB inactivation consis -
tently preserved the antigens comparable to untreated viruses, 
while substantial antigen loss was observed in case of heat and for -
malin inactivated viruses ( Figure 3 ). In RV , VP4 surface protein 
showed no significant difference between untreated controls and 
eBeam inactivated viral suspension at lethal doses. In contrast, heat 
inactivation resulted in 5.09 to 6.56-fold reduction, and formalin 
inactivation caused 1.69 to 1.72-fold reduction in VP4 antigenicity 
compared to HEEB and MEEB ( Figures 3a ,d). Similarly, surface 
fusion-protein of RSV showed no significant difference between 
untreated virus to HEEB lethal doses (15.56, 18.17, 20.73 kGy) and 
MEEB lethal doses (13.29, 16.20, 19.81, 21.65 kGy). Conversely, a 
5.79 to 7.89-fold reduction was noted for the heat-inactivated RSV 
virus, and a 1.26 to 1.72-fold reduction for the formalin-inactivated 
RSV relative to the HEEB and MEEB-inactivated samples 
(Figures 3b,e). Influenza A virus was assessed for the hemaggluti -
nin antigen integrity by its ability to bind with turkey red blood 
cells. The results showed no difference in hemagglutinin antigen 
integrity between untreated virus to HEEB lethal doses (12.31, 
15.56, 18.17, 20.73 kGy) and MEEB lethal doses (13.29, 16.20, 
19.81, 21.65 kGy). In comparison, results showed significant differ -
ences with 256-fold reduction for heat-inactivated and 2-fold 
reduction for formalin-inactivated influenza A virus suspensions 
(Figures 3c,f).
These results demonstrate that eBeam treatment (HEEB and 
MEEB) preserve the native confirmation and structural integrity of 
the key antigenic proteins even at doses substantially exceeding the 
complete inactivation threshold, while heat and formalin inactivation 
compromises the antigenic integrity across all viral surface pro -
teins tested.
3.4 Virus-host interaction
The HEEB and MEEB irradiated viral suspensions which showed 
complete inactivation and preserved antigenic proteins were further 
evaluated for their ability to be recognized by their respective host’s 
receptors. Figure 4 shows that both HEEB and MEEB inactivation 
demonstrated enhanced virus-host interaction than heat or forma -
lin-inactivated samples across all three tested viruses. The eBeam-
inactivated RV displayed significant difference ( p < 0.0001) with 
1.85-fold (HEEB) and 1.82- fold (MEEB) better virus-host recogni -
tion than heat-inactivated samples and 0.89-fold (HEEB) and 1.14-
fold (MEEB) better host recognition than formalin-inactivated 
samples (Figure 4A). However, the untreated control RV preparation 
exhibited 1.07- to 1.18-fold higher virus-host activity of the VP4 
protein compared to HEEB and MEEB inactivated samples. Similar 
results were observed for RSV , where eBeam inactivation resulted in 
1.58 to 1.54-fold and 1.67 to 1.63-fold greater virus-host interaction 
of fusion-protein (p < 0.0001) in comparison to heat and formalin-
inactivated samples, respectively, (Figure 4B). For IAV , lethal eBeam 
treatment demonstrated 23.03 to 23.11-fold and 1.29 to 1.31-fold 
better virus-host interaction of hemagglutinin protein with statisti -
cal significance ( p < 0.0001) in comparison to heat and formalin-
inactivated influenza A virus, respectively, ( Figure 4C ). These 
findings emphasize the superior preservation of functional virus-
host interactions following eBeam (HEEB and MEEB) 
inactivation.
4 Discussion
This study provides the first systematic cross modality compari-
son between accelerator-based (eBeam: HEEB and MEEB) and radio-
active isotope-based (gamma) radiation for viral inactivation. 
Previous studies investigated viral inactivation using individual radia-
tion modalities: rotavirus with HEEB (Skrobarczyk et al., 2022), RSV 
with LEEB ( Bayer et al., 2018 ), and influenza A with LEEB and 
gamma (Fertey et al., 2016), but employed different matrices, tem-
peratures, or dosimetry systems, precluding reliable cross-modality 
comparison of D10 values. Present study addresses this gap by main-
taining standard parameters such as sample matrix (PBS), unified 
alanine/EPR dosimetry system (ISO/ASTM 51607, 2013) and main-
taining the temperature (4 °C) during sample handling and transpor-
tation, while varying the radiation modality enabling direct 
comparison of inactivation efficiency between HEEB, MEEB and 
gamma irradiation.
The primary physical differences between these modalities as 
highlighted in Table 1 are dose rate (HEEB: 3.84–4.31 kGy/s; 
MEEB: 0.37–0.45 kGy/s; Cobalt-60: 1.7–5.1 kGy/h) and particle 
energy (HEEB: 10 MeV electrons; MEEB: 4.5 MeV electrons; 
gamma: 1.17 and 1.33 MeV uncharged photons). We acknowledge 
that beam current and dose rate could not be experimentally nor -
malized across eBeam modalities, as irradiations were necessarily 
performed at physically distinct facilities. Nevertheless, the con -
sistent patterns observed across all three viruses suggest system -
atic modality-dependent effects rather than experimental 
variation.

radiation sensitivity hierarchy was observed with RV > RSV > IAV 
across all the modalities. Ionizing radiation induced viral inactiva -
tion is governed by both direct ionization of viral nucleic acids and 
indirect effects mediated by reactive species generated through 
water radiolysis. Variations in electron energy, and consequently 
linear energy transfer (LET), influence the spatial distribution and 
yield of these reactive species, thereby impacting viral genome 
damage and preservation of antigenic structures ( Buxton et al., 
1988; Jonah et al., 1990 ; Fertey et al., 2016 ). Based on the classical 
target theory ( Lea, 1946 ; Pollard, 1953), inactivation probability is 
directly proportional to the radiation sensitive target volume within 
the irradiated entity. Consequently, D10 is inversely proportional to 
genome size, as larger genome presents proportionally greater 
chances of radiation induced ionization events per unit absorbed 
dose (Lea, 1946; Praveen et al., 2013 ; Feldmann et al., 2019 ). Thus, 
higher susceptibility of RV could be attributable to the relatively 
large genome size (18.5 kb ds RNA) compared to RSV (15.2 kb 
ssRNA) and IAV (13.5 kb segmented ssRNA).
The D10 value (1.95 ± 0.04 kGy) for the IAV (A/PR/8/34/H1N1) 
using PBS matrix in current study is comparable to previously 
reported 2.3 kGy for IAV (A/Chicken/TX/02/rH5N3) using HEEB 
irradiation ( Brahmakshatriya et al., 2009 ). However, rotavirus D 10 
value (1.25 ± 0.25 kGy) was found lower than the previously 
reported D10 value (2.38 kGy) with HEEB irradiation ( Skrobarczyk 
et al., 2022). This suggests that the D 10 value is influenced by factors 
beyond ionizing radiation energy or genome size, including the 
virus strain, matrix composition and other variables. However, the 
decrease in log 10 titers across all three tested viruses showed an 
inverse but linear relationship, demonstrating the efficiency of high 
and medium energy eBeam technology ( Figure 2). Both HEEB and 
MEEB demonstrated effective viral inactivation, establishing their 
suitability as alternatives to 60Co gamma radiation. Electron beam 
modalities offer significant operational advantage of achieving com -
plete viral inactivation and achieve sterility assurance level (SAL) 
in a fraction of the time without producing radioactive emissions 
(Gaidamakova et al., 2012 ).

Both HEEB and MEEB irradiation showed significantly better 
preservation of surface antigenic proteins and virus-host interaction 
compared to traditional chemical-inactivation method, which is widely 
used in vaccine development but often leads to reduced antigenic 
response due to damaged antigenic proteins ( Fertey et al., 2020 ). 
Chemical inactivation is the method used for the FDA-approved influ-
enza inactivated vaccine since 2013 in the United States, which is time-
consuming and complicates downstream assays. Across all three 
viruses, eBeam-inactivated preparations-maintained antigenicity 
equivalent to untreated virus even at doses up to 21.65 kGy—substan-
tially exceeding complete inactivation thresholds—while heat inactiva-
tion caused 5.09–256-fold loss and formalin produced 1.26–2-fold 
reduction. These results are consistent with previous reports (Fertey et 
al., 2016; Skrobarczyk et al., 2022) and reflect the fundamental mecha-
nistic advantage of radiation-based inactivation: preferential genomic 
destruction through nucleic acid targeting while leaving structural 
proteins largely intact at the doses required for inactivation.
Antigenic preservation was further validated by virus-host inter-
action assays, where eBeam inactivated viruses displayed superior 
recognition to host-receptors compared to heat and chemical-inacti-
vated viruses. Unlike the non-replicative eBeam-treated samples, 
untreated control viruses demonstrated stronger interactions, likely 
reflecting ongoing viral replication. Compared to chemical inactiva-
tion that produce narrower antibody-specific responses (Singleton et 
al., 2021; Wijewardana et al., 2023), irradiated vaccines using eBeam 
and gamma modalities can offer broader and cellular immune 
responses (Alsharifi et al., 2009; Furuya et al., 2010; David et al., 2019; 
Wijewardana et al., 2023 ; Schulze et al., 2024 ; Belay et al., 2025 ). 
Overall, these results highlight eBeam irradiation as a promising 
alternative for future vaccine development and biopharmaceutical 
applications due to its rapid inactivation and superior antigenic pres-
ervation of vaccinal target sites.
The differential effects of ionizing radiation (eBeam and 
gamma), heat, and formalin on viral infectivity and antigen pres -
ervation can be attributed to the inherent differences in how these 
three viral inactivation methodologies function. Ionizing radia -
tion functions by breakage of the phosphor-diester bonds on the 
nucleic acid molecule while formalin functions by creating cova -
lent crosslinks in and between proteins and nucleic acids whereas 
heat treatment induces protein unfolding, aggregation and other 
irreversible thermal damage ( Ward, 1988; Hoffman et al., 2015 ; 
Loveday et al., 2021 ).
5 Conclusion
This study provides the first systematic comparison of HEEB 
(10 MeV), MEEB (4,5 MeV) and Cobalt-60 gamma irradiation for 
viral inactivation under standardized conditions of sample matrix, 
shipping temperature and alanine dosimetry system. All three 
modalities effectively inactivated human rotavirus, respiratory syn -
cytial virus and influenza A virus. The inactivation kinetics studies 
demonstrated that viral inactivation efficiency achieved by eBeam 
modalities are comparable to gamma irradiation. Therefore, HEEB 
and MEEB represents sustainable nonradioactive alternatives to 
gamma radiation, capable of achieving effective viral inactivation 
in seconds compared to hours required for gamma, owing to its 
high dose rate. Critically, eBeam inactivation preserved key 
immunodominant surface proteins (VP4, F-protein and HA) at 
levels equivalent to untreated control even at lethal dose of up to 
20.73 kGy (HEEB) and 21.65 kGy (MEEB), substantially outper -
forming heat and formalin-inactivated virus preparations. Also, 
eBeam inactivated viruses maintained superior functional virus-
host receptor recognition indicating the functional integrity of anti -
genic proteins. Collectively, these findings establish eBeam 
technology as a promising platform for effective viral inactivation 
for biopharmaceutical applications, combining rapid and complete 
inactivation with superior antigenic preservation and functional 
host receptor recognition essential for immunological efficacy.
