Model Risk in European Union Allowance Futures and 
Options: From Black–76 to Institutionally Grounded 
Models—A Critical Structured Review 

Bohao Zhu* 
Beijing Jiaotong University, Weihai, China 
*Corresponding author: Bohao Zhu. 

Abstract 
Pricing models for European Union Allowance (EUA) derivatives are often assessed as statistical 
specifications even though the EU Emissions Trading System changes the payoff boundary, supply process, 
and intertemporal value of the underlying compliance asset. This critical structured review examines how EUA 
futures and options models have developed from Black–76 conventions to institutionally grounded equilibrium, 
structural, and Market Stability Reserve (MSR) specifications; how regulatory mechanisms enter risk-neutral 
valuation; and what evidence supports model robustness. Fifty -three DOI - or publisher -verified academic 
records were coded using an Institution –Model–Measure–Evidence (IMME) lens: 47 inform the model -level 
synthesis and six provide contextual triangulation. The evidence is organized into early benchmarks and 
deficiencies, institutionally grounded models, and MSR -era pricing and validation. Black –76 remains a 
transparent quotation benchmark, but evidence from physical-measure dynamics and phase boundaries limits 
its structural interpretation. Structural and equilibrium models make institutional channels explicit, yet add 
identification and numerical risks. Comparative studies provide some held -out pricing evidence, but the 
literature still lacks transaction-cost-aware hedging and regime-transport tests. The resulting model -selection 
principle is condition al adequacy: use the least complex model that is regime -aligned, measure -explicit, 
empirically identified, and validated for the intended pricing or hedging task.

1. Introduction 
European Union Allowances (EUAs) are both tradable assets and regulator-created compliance instruments. 
Their futures and options are quoted in a language inherited from conventional commodity derivatives, and 
Black–76 remains the natural reference for a E uropean option written on a futures contract [1]. Yet a 
transparent benchmark is not the same as an adequate model. Model risk arises when several statistically 
plausible specifications imply materially different prices, sensitivities, reserves, or hedge outcomes [2]. In the

EUA market, this risk is unusually consequential because the stochastic process is not determined only by 
market information: it also reflects rules that can change the survival, scarcity, and terminal value of an 
allowance. 
The institutional architecture makes cross -period transport especially difficult. Banking and borrowing 
determine whether scarcity can be shifted through time [3]; compliance calendars, penalties, allocation, and 
verified-emissions disclosure affect the payoff boundary and information set; and reforms such as the Market 
Stability Reserve (MSR) make effective supply depend on the state of the market [4–6]. Consequently, a model 
may be arbitrage -free and fit one period while remaining economically misspecified for another phase. In -
sample accuracy can also conceal a misspecified transition from the physical probability measure to the risk -
neutral measure, weak identification from sparse option quotes, or a hedge that fails when liquidity and policy 
conditions change. 
Existing books provide valuable accounts of EU ETS history, market microstructure, and econometrics [4–
6], while review-method guidance clarifies review design and reporting expectations [7,8]. Tang et al. [9] map 
quantitative ETS research across broad model classes and agent tasks, with derivatives as one subtopic; Bisiotis 
et al. [10] primarily synthesize time-series, econometric, and machine-learning forecasts. Neither provides an 
MSR-aware comparison linking institutional design to pricing-measure choice, option-surface validation, and 
implementable hedging evidence. This paper therefore asks three linked questions: how modelling evolved 
from Black–76-type specifications to institutionally grounded models; through which mechanisms EU ETS 
rules affect risk -neutral dynamics and derivative values; and how strongly the availab le evidence supports 
comparative robustness across regimes. Section 2 defines the common valuation concepts, Section 3 
synthesizes three connected strands of evidence, Section 4 proposes a conditional model -selection principle 
and identifies research gaps, and Section 5 answers the questions from the present synthesis. 
2. Theoretical Background 
Black–76 prices a futures option by discounting the expected terminal payoff under lognormal risk-neutral 
futures dynamics with constant volatility [1]. Its practical virtues are substantial: the inputs are observable or 
conventionally inferred, the formul a is transparent, and implied volatility provides a common coordinate for 
comparing strikes and maturities. The embedded assumptions nevertheless form an audit checklist rather than 
a default description of the EUA market. Continuous trading, a single diff usion, stable volatility, frictionless 
hedging, and a correctly specified terminal payoff can each fail without making the quoted Black–76 volatility 
useless. The benchmark should therefore be retained as a reference price and reporting convention while it s 
economic and statistical assumptions are tested. 
A second distinction concerns probability measures. Historical returns, realized volatility, GARCH 
estimates, and policy -shock decompositions describe dynamics under the physical measure (P). Derivative 
valuation instead requires a risk -neutral measure (Q) or another selected pricing measure under which 
discounted tradable prices satisfy the relevant no-arbitrage restriction. Jumps, liquidity, non-traded compliance 
risk, and policy uncertainty may make the market incomplete, so the physical -to-pricing-measure 
transformation and the associated volatility or jump premia are part of the model rather than technical 
afterthoughts [2]. Forecasting success under the physical measure is consequently not equivalent to option -
pricing or hedging validation. 
Institutional rules enter at both the boundary and the state -transition levels. A compliance penalty and the 
probability of aggregate shortage influence terminal allowance value, while banking, borrowing, cancellation, 
and certificate substitutability determine intertemporal and cross-asset links [3–6]. Under the MSR, the Total 
Number of Allowances in Circulation (TNAC) can trigger intake, release, or cancellation, making future 
supply state-dependent. To compare heterogeneous studies consistently, this review uses IMME as a coding 
lens with four linked dimensions: institutional regime, model specification, probability measure, and empirical 
evidence. IMME is not proposed as a standalone theory. It is an analytical device for asking, for every model 
family, when it fails, why it fails, and what evidence defines its domain of use.

3. Literature Review 
3.1 Review Design and Coding 
This critical structured review synthesizes heterogeneous theoretical and empirical studies [7]. Searches 
through 11 August 2026 covered Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, Wiley Online Library, 
Taylor & Francis Online, SIAM, Cambridge Cor e, and journal publishers, supplemented by backward and 
forward citation chaining. Search terms combined EU ETS, EUA, and emission allowance with futures, 
options, pricing, hedging, risk -neutral, jump, stochastic volatility, structural, equilibrium, MSR, and TNAC. 
Eligible sources were English -language peer-reviewed articles, academic books, and review -method papers 
relevant to allowance dynamics, derivative valuat ion, or institutional mechanisms. The retained corpus 
comprises 53 DOI - or publisher -verified records: 47 support the model -level synthesis and six provide 
contextual support, as identified in Appendix A. Original database exports and screening decisions w ere not 
preserved, so PRISMA screening counts are not reported [8]. The Institution –Model–Measure–Evidence 
(IMME) coding records institutional regimes and contract boundaries, model specifications, probability 
measures and pricing transformations, and empi rical data, calibration, validation, and hedging evidence. 
Appendix A provides the coding protocol and an example. 
3.2 Early Benchmarks and Model Deficiencies 
Across the early literature, Black –76 is better understood as a transparent benchmark than as a complete 
data-generating model for EUA derivatives. Equilibrium evidence identifies volatility that changes with time 
and the allowance price [11], while mixtur e-GARCH analysis documents conditional heteroskedasticity and 
heavy tails [12]. Markov -switching evidence further shows that EUA dynamics can change abruptly across 
market states [13]. Most importantly, the prohibition on carrying Phase I allowances into P hase II created an 
institutional discontinuity between contracts that would otherwise appear to be adjacent maturities; jump -
diffusion and convenience -yield extensions in early derivative work were therefore responses to a real 
compliance boundary rather than merely statistical embellishments [14]. 
The IMME comparison clarifies both the contribution and the limit of this strand. Under the physical 
measure, early spot and futures studies document conditional heteroskedasticity, heavy tails, and state changes 
that are inconsistent with a constant -volatility Gaussian process or a single stationary regime [11 –13]. 
Daskalakis et al. [14] additionally show that a closed -phase banking prohibition changes the terminal and 
cross-maturity structure relevant to derivatives. These findings diagnose ingredients that a pricing model may 
need, but they do not by themselves identify risk-neutral dynamics or establish option-pricing superiority. The 
appropriate conclusion is not that every jump, regime, or stochastic -volatility extension dominates Black–76. 
It is that the benchmark's domain must be stated and that features should be added only when an institutionally 
matched mechanism and a relevant validation test justify them. 
3.3 Institutionally Grounded Structural and Equilibrium Models 
The next literature strand redefines an allowance as a contingent claim produced by the compliance system. 
In incomplete-market work, information about aggregate positions can generate jumps and hedging depends 
on a selected martingale measure rather than a unique one [15]. Stochastic -control and equilibrium models 
derive prices from firms' production, abatement, trading, and terminal compliance decisions [16,17], while 
market-design analysis shows that allocation and compliance rules affect equilibrium and rents [18]. A 
tractable risk-neutral model links terminal value to the statutory penalty and the pricing -measure probability 
of non-compliance [19], and jump-diffusion formulations translate discrete information or policy shocks into 
nonlinear valuation equations [20]. These approaches make the institutional boundary explicit, but many were 
designed for non-bankable or early-phase markets. 
Structural models deepen that link by endogenizing state dynamics. Firm optimization under information 
asymmetry and intertemporal flexibility produces option values that need not resemble those from an 
exogenous lognormal process [21]. Forward-backward stochastic differential equation and partial differential 
equation systems connect demand for the polluting good, cumulative emissions, allowance prices, and 
derivatives across compliance periods [22]. The discontinuous compliance payoff can create a singula r 
terminal condition whose numerical treatment affects option values and implied volatility [23]. Market -

consistent modelling of substitutable EUA and Certified Emission Reduction certificates likewise shows that 
eligibility rules restrict joint dynamics in ways a generic correlated -assets model may miss [24]. Economic 
interpretation improves, but latent emis sions states, singular boundaries, parameter identification, and 
numerical approximation become additional sources of model risk. 
Empirical and comparative studies partly arbitrate this trade -off. EUA futures -option evidence indicates 
that jump risk can materially change prices when combined with time -series dynamics and a stated pricing 
transformation [25]. Time -varying convenience yields and risk premia complicate joint calibration across 
maturities [26], while long -memory and regime comparisons caution against transporting one volatility law 
across market states [27]. An equilibrium model treating allowances as nonlinear claims on aggregate 
emissions reproduces nonstandard spot, futures, volatility, and option behaviour, although estimation is 
demanding [28]. Most directly, Hitzemann and Uhrig -Homburg [29] compare a geometric -Brownian-motion 
benchmark with six ETS -specific reduced-form models using daily Phase II EUA futures and futures -option 
prices from January 2008 to June 2012. Monthly calibrations are tested on the following month's option prices; 
two-period models perform best overall in 2011 –2012, particularly out of sample, while added volatility 
flexibility sometimes worsens hel d-out performance. This supports institutional alignment within the tested 
Phase II models, with no transaction-cost-aware hedging or MSR-era test. 
3.4 Modern Pricing and Validation Evidence in the MSR Era 
The MSR changes the institutional baseline by making supply a feedback process rather than a fixed cap. 
Phase IV design choices and reserve thresholds alter expected scarcity and can be anticipated before 
implementation [30]. Private banking and risk avers ion interact with temporal issuance, intake, and 
cancellation, so a current surplus can imply different future supply paths under different expectations [31]. 
Multiperiod singular systems extend the theory to several compliance dates with banking, borrowin g, and 
withdrawal [32]. Fang et al. [33] compare lognormal (LN), skewness –kurtosis-modified lognormal (SKM), 
and two-component mixture-lognormal (MLN-2) models using weekly EUA call -option samples from Phase 
III (2017–2018) and Phase IV (2023). MLN-2 performs best overall across the two samples, although SKM is 
strongest in-sample in the short Phase IV window. Together with [29], this comparison supports performance 
gains within specific samples and regimes rather than universal model superiority. Its evidence remains limited 
by sparse contracts, low -liquidity quotes, and the absence of dynamic -hedging tests. Theory -guided transfer 
learning improves pricing for two Phase IV call contracts, but that narrow test set also remains preliminary 
[34]. 
A related body of evidence shows why pricing -measure claims must be separated from historical 
forecasting. Around the 2006 compliance event, physical and risk -neutral densities changed differently after 
verified-emissions disclosure [35], and realized -emissions announcements affected returns, volume, and 
volatility even when the calendar event was partly anticipated in option prices [36]. A time -varying variance 
risk premium confirms that implied variance is not a direct forecast of physical variance [37]. Forecasting 
studies themselves conflict: implied volatility contains forward information in one sample [38], whereas 
GARCH specifications perform better in another illiquid sample [39]. Jump evidence is equally conditional. 
High-frequency analysis identifies small and large jumps and questions a continuous Brownian component 
[40]; Bayesian estimation supports stochastic volatility with jumps [41]; yet systematic outlier treatment 
removes many apparent jumps [42]. Differences in sampling frequency, quote quality, liquidity, horizon, 
cleaning, and loss function explain why these findings should not be pooled into one universal ranking. 
Post-MSR work improves institutional relevance without closing the validation chain. Long -run market 
models suggest that reserve reform can raise prices and reduce cumulative emissions while making the 
effective cap endogenous [43], and a critical review documents the reserve's theoretical channels and contested 
effects [44]. Structural shock decomposition indicates that the composition of EUA price movements changed 
after the MSR, although the pandemic and energy crisis impede clean causal attribution [45] . A recent 
multistage pricing framework makes TNAC thresholds, reserve intake, release, cancellation, auctions, and 
continuation value explicit and reproduces recent price and futures-term-structure features [46]. Recent jump-
test option pricing also reinforces event sensitivity [47]. Both advances remain early: neither demonstrates a 
complete option -surface calibration together with genuinely held -out prices and transaction -cost-aware 
dynamic hedging. The missing link is thus bounded but important —MSR states have not yet been connected 
to risk-neutral futures and option surfaces and tested under one reproducible cross-regime protocol.

4. Discussion and Synthesis 
4.1 Central Synthesis 
The synthesis rejects a monotonic complexity narrative. Black –76 fails as a structural account when 
volatility, tails, phase boundaries, or state -dependent supply are material, but it remains valuable as an 
interpretable quotation and control model. Jump, stochastic-volatility, mixture, structural, equilibrium, and 
machine-learning specifications can correct identified deficiencies, yet every correction creates new exposure 
to parameter instability, latent -state identification, numerical choices, or data sc arcity. A model is therefore 
defensible only conditionally: its institutional boundary must match the contract and evaluation period, its 
pricing measure must be explicit, and its incremental mechanism must be supported by evidence relevant to 
the intended decision. 
Viewed through IMME, the most persuasive literature connects all four dimensions rather than excelling at 
one. Institutional coherence without an identified market process is not a pricing model; a sophisticated process 
under the wrong compliance regime is economically misspecified ; a physical -measure forecast without a 
pricing transformation does not validate an option value; and a low calibration error without held -out or 
hedging evidence does not establish robustness. The available direct comparisons identify benefits within their 
particular samples and model sets [29,33], but they do not establish a phase -invariant ranking. The review 
therefore proposes conditional adequacy as a model-selection principle. 
4.2 Applicability Boundaries and Evidence Hierarchy 
Model choice should follow the task. Black –76 can serve as a first benchmark for liquid, near -the-money 
quotations when a transparent implied -volatility language is needed. Historical GARCH, regime, or jump 
models are useful for forecasting and risk scenar ios, but cannot price options without a justified measure 
transformation. Jump, stochastic-volatility, and mixture models are better candidates for smiles, tails, and event 
exposure when option liquidity identifies their added parameters. Structural and equilibriu m models are most 
valuable when compliance, banking, certificate links, or MSR feedback are central, although their 
implementation requires sensitivity analysis for latent states and numerical boundaries. Data -driven models 
should be judged only under time-ordered tests, transparent training data, and strong conventional controls. 
The evidence hierarchy proposed in this review is a cumulative four -level scheme derived from recurring 
validation gaps across the three synthesis strands. Level 1 requires institutional, no -arbitrage, and pricing -
measure coherence. Level 2 adds identified and stable in-sample calibration with disclosed quote and liquidity 
filters. Level 3 requires genuinely time -ordered held-out option pricing by strike and maturity. Level 4 adds 
transaction-cost-aware dynamic hedging and transport across policy regimes, i ncluding explicit recalibration 
or retirement criteria. Greater mathematical complexity does not raise a model's level; each level adds a 
validation burden and does not substitute for lower -level coherence. In the reviewed literature, evidence is 
concentrated at Levels 1–2, with fewer studies providing Level 3 validation and very limited Level 4 evidence. 
Heterogeneous contracts, filters, samples, and losses accordingly preclude a pooled ranking by raw error. 
4.3 Future Research 
Three gaps emerge from the reviewed evidence: comparable out -of-sample option pricing, dynamic 
hedging with transaction costs, and validation across policy phases. Shared contract samples, quote filters, and 
time-ordered evaluation windows would make prici ng comparisons more informative. Hedging tests could 
then examine whether pricing improvements translate into gains after trading costs, while cross -phase tests 
would assess whether calibrated models remain useful when compliance rules or MSR mechanisms ch ange. 
These priorities would strengthen the evidence for conditional model selection and help distinguish ordinary 
parameter instability from institutional misspecification. 
5. Conclusion 
The present synthesis answers the first research question by identifying an evolution from a transparent 
Black–76 convention to empirical jump, volatility, and regime extensions, and then to risk-neutral, equilibrium, 
structural, multiperiod, MSR-explicit, and theory-guided data-driven models. The decisive differences among 
Vol. 17 (2026): Proceedings of the International Conference on Research in Accounting, Finance and Management (ICRAFM 2026) 
814 
these families are not mathematical sophistication alone. They lie in the terminal boundary, institutional state 
variables, probability-measure treatment, parameter identification, and standard of validation. 
For the second question, EU ETS rules affect derivative values through several connected mechanisms: 
banking and borrowing determine intertemporal survival; penalties and aggregate shortage shape terminal 
value; disclosure and compliance dates alter the in formation process; certificate eligibility constrains joint 
dynamics; and the MSR makes future supply depend on circulating allowances and policy thresholds. These 
channels can change both the expected futures path and the risk -neutral distribution of opti on payoffs, so 
institutional design cannot be relegated to an external policy discussion. 
For the third question, the limited direct comparisons provide relatively stronger support for institutionally 
aligned specifications within matched samples, model sets, and policy regimes; evidence for a broader ranking, 
especially in the MSR era, remains insufficient. Robustness requires coherent institutional boundaries and an 
explicit pricing measure, followed by held -out option pricing, transaction -cost-aware dynamic hedging, and 
validation across policy regimes. The resulting model-selection principle is conditional adequacy: use the least 
complex model that is regime-aligned, measure-explicit, empirically identified, and validated for the intended 
pricing or hedging task.

Appendix A. Corpus Classification and IMME Coding Protocol 
Corpus classification. The 53-record evidence matrix contains 47 records used in the model-level synthesis 
and six contextual records retained for triangulation. The latter comprise Chevallier's risk -premium analysis 
[48], Chevallier and Sé vi's realized -volatility forecast [49], Bredin and Parsons' cost -of-carry and term -
structure evidence [50], Hinz and Novikov's fair -pricing principles [51], Kollenberg and Taschini's cap-
adjustment design [52], and Perino and Willner's analysis of delayed MSR reform [53]. They support pricing-
measure, term -structure, or institutional interpretation but do not add an independent cross -model option -
validation or hedging test; none was treated as a duplicate or quality exclusion. 
Coding protocol. Institution codes record regime boundaries and policy state; Model codes identify 
processes, state variables, payoffs, parameters, and numerical methods; Measure codes distinguish P, Q, 
transformations, and incomplete-market pricing principles; Evidence codes record data, filters, validation splits, 
losses, hedging costs, and regime tests. For example, Fang et al. [33] is coded as Phase III –IV open trading 
with inter -phase banking (Institution); LN, SKM, and MLN -2 futures -option specificati ons (Model); risk -
neutral calibration to option prices (Measure); and weekly cross -sections with training/testing MSE, MAE, 
and MAPE but no dynamic-hedging or regime-transport test (Evidence). 
Illustrative evidence links. Risk -neutral modelling in [19] illustrates Level 1 coherence; option -price 
calibration in [33] illustrates a Level 2 component; and next-month pricing in [29] illustrates the chronological 
holdout component of Level 3. These links identify particular evidence requirements rather than formal ratings 
of whole studies; neither empirical example establishes the combined hedging and cross -regime validation 
required at Level 4.
