A Review of Methods for Predicting Stock Volatility: A 
Comparison of Traditional Econometric Models, Implied 
Volatility, and Machine Learning Methods 

Jinyi Liu* 
School of Finance, Nankai University, Tianjin 300071, China 
*Corresponding author: Jinyi Liu. 

Abstract 
Stock volatility forecasting serves as a crucial underpinning for derivatives pricing, asset allocation, and risk 
management. This paper examines volatility proxy variables, empirical applications, as well as forecasting 
evaluation metrics, and then review s representative models and empirical evidence across three research 
pathways. The comparison concerns the information basis and forecasting horizon, taking into account 
explanatory power, stability, and economic value. A comprehensive review of the literature suggests that 
traditional models remain a robust benchmark in scenarios with limited data, in which emphasis is placed on 
interpretability and reproducibility. Impl ied volatility can provide incremental information beyond historical 
data; however, its predictive power is influenced by variance risk premiums, liquidity, and maturity matching. 
Machine learning offers greater flexibility in high -dimensional data environments, but its advantages depend 
on rigorous time -series validation and control of information leakage. Because of inconsistencies in proxy 
variables, loss functions, sample partitioning, and market conditions, existing evidence fails to support the 
universal superiority of any single method across all tasks. Future analysis should conduct cross -market tests 
under a unified evaluation framework and integrate statistical forecasting accuracy with economic value in 
risk management and investment decision-making. 
Keywords 
volatility forecasting, GARCH-type models, implied volatility, machine learning, model comparison 

1. Introduction 
The extent of stock volatility is an indicator of the uncertainty that characterizes asset price changes and is 
an important input to derivatives pricing, asset allocation, and risk management [1]. Volatility is not directly 
observable and has clustering, persistence, long memory, and asymmetry. The factors that affect its 
fluctuations include market conditions, economic uncertainty, and investor sentiment [2, 3, 4]. To forecast 
volatility, it is necessary to identify its historical dynamics and determine the horizons and market conditions 
under which different types of information provide incremental value. 
The study of this question has been divided into three general directions. To begin with, conventional 
econometric models extract the volatility pattern based on historical returns, the range of prices, and high -
frequency realized measures, and make use of structures like GARCH, HAR, Realized GARCH, and CARR

use option prices, variance risk premiums, and trading volume to infer what markets expect the future volatility

information, and the capability to handle multi-source information, respectively. 
In the current literature, the key issue being contested is whether a model's predictive advantage persists 
under a common evaluation framework. The selection of true volatility proxies and loss measures, the forecast 
horizon, information timing, and sample partitioning influence the ranking of models [16, 17, 18]. In addition, 
a decrease in statistical error may not be translated to economic benefit in risk management or investment 
decision making [19]. The main topic of this paper is information sources, with an initial discussion on 
volatility measures, their applications, and evaluation, followed by a systematic review of the three categories 
of methods. The applicability conditions and controversies of each method are compared and contrasted in 
order to draw comparisons between them and outline the gaps in research and future testing opportunities. 
2. Measurement, Applications, and Evaluation of Volatility 
The comparability of various studies is only possible if the predicted objects and assessment criteria are 
more or less similar . In this section, the commonly used proxy variables of volatility, applications of 
forecasting outcomes in derivatives pricing and risk management, and out-of-sample loss functions and model 
comparison tests will be discussed in sequence. 
2.1 Volatility Proxies and Measurement Differences 
The most popular proxy variables are squared returns, price range, and realized volatility. Squared returns 
depend exclusively on adjacent closing prices; they are widely available over long historical periods but are 
highly noisy at the daily frequency. Price range involves the use of both high and low prices that strike a 
balance between cost of the data and intraday information content [5, 20]. Realized volatility, however, 
summarizes intraday high-frequency returns and can better characteri ze price paths, although it is vulnerable 
to bid-ask spreads, asynchronous trading, and sampling frequency [8, 20]. The errors in measurement of these 
three forms of proxy variables vary, and an identical prediction would be ranked differently with various loss 
functions. Comparisons across studies thus need standardization of proxy variables or adoption of evaluation 
procedures that are less sensitive to proxy errors [18]. 
2.2 Empirical Applications of Volatility Forecasting 
The value of volatility forecasting is ultimately reflected in specific financial decision -making. Firstly, in 
the pricing of an option, projected volatility has a direct implication on theoretical prices and risk exposure 
estimates. To illustrate, in the case of SSE 50 ETF options, model-free implied volatility combines information 
on various strike prices, and incorporates forward -looking information, not just historical volatility. As there 
is a variance risk premium between risk -neutral expectations and realised volatility, correcting this risk 
premium in implied volatility is likely to decrease the forecast bias [11, 12, 14]. In this context, the difference 
between the expected volatility and the risk compensation will also influence the forecast accuracy. 
Secondly, in market risk management, the forecasting of volatility can be viewed as an important input in 
the calculation of Value at Risk (VaR ) and determining capital buffers. Wu Xinyu et al. [19] included both 
options information and high-frequency data in the VaR measures; the findings indicated that the combination 
of forward -looking information with the recent realized volatilities would co ntribute positively to the 
characterization of risks in extreme market situations. The applications of VaR pay attention to the 
identification of tail losses as well as the evaluation of the appropriateness of the risk coverage, which makes 
it possible to test how statistical accuracy can be transformed into economic and managerial value. In 
considering the assessment of volatility models, the forecasting problem and its duration must be considered 
in the analysis together with the decision-making loss function.

2.3 Out-of-Sample Evaluation and Model Comparison Tests 
The in-sample fitting performance cannot be used to measure a model’s predictive power out -of-sample. 
In order to make the comparison results reliable, related research generally splits the data into training and 
testing intervals chronologically and uses rolling or extended windows to mimic the real -life process of the 
information being received over time [1, 15, 16 ]. Selection of evaluation metrics also affects the findings of 
model comparisons. Mean Squared Error (MSE) emphasizes measuring absolute error, but robust loss 
functions like QLIKE are able to decrease the effect of measurement errors of proxy variables on model 
rankings [18]. For predictive-accuracy testing, the Diebold-Mariano test [21] is commonly used to determine 
whether the difference between the forecast errors of two models is statistic ally significant. Giacomini and 
White [22] extended forecast evaluation to conditional predictive ability while accounting for parameter -
estimation uncertainty. When comparing a number of models simultaneously, Hansen et al. [17] suggested 
using the Model Confidence Set (MCS) to determine a set of statistically indistinguishable superior models, 
which precludes the misinterpretation of small or random model error differences as stable predictive benefits. 
3. Traditional Econometric Models Based on Historical Information 
The traditional econometric model has been developed over time in the direction of the introduction of 
intraday, long-term, and state information on the basis of historical price dynamics. These types of models also 
have a well-defined structure, simple interpretation of parameters, and are relatively easy to reproduce; they 
can be used as the foundation of volatility prediction and as the standard to compare more advanced techniques 
[1]. 
3.1 ARCH and GARCH: Volatility Clustering and Persistence 
Engle [10] suggested using the ARCH model to express conditional variance as a function of the squares 
of previous shocks, which introduces volatility clustering to a statistically estimateable form. Bollerslev [9] 
also added the lagged conditional variance term to the GARCH model in order to portray volatility persistence 
with fewer parameters. Both model classes require only daily returns and can thus be used in situations with 
low data frequency or when interpretability of the parameters is important. Nonetheless, they have mostly been 
constrained in terms of information to historical returns, such that it is challenging to directly use intraday 
price paths or consider option expectations and textual information. Subsequent developments have therefore 
focused on incorporating these additional sources of information. 
3.2 Realized GARCH and HAR: High-Frequency Information and Long Memory 
Realized measures can be directly constructed by the use of high-frequency data and intraday information 
may be included in conditional volatility models. Realized GARCH links daily returns, latent conditional 
variance, and realized volatility through a measurement equation. Research on the Chinese market has show n 
that such a framework enhances out -of-sample predictions of the CSI 300 Index [7, 23]. The HAR model is 
based on the approximation of the long-memory properties of volatility with daily, weekly, and monthly factors; 
it has a simple form that can be extended to include jumps and other high-frequency factors [8, 15]. According 
to cross-market evidence, nonlinear or very complex models do not always outperform linear benchmarks over 
all horizons [16]. Efficiency of high-frequency information is determined by sampling noise, model structure, 
and forecast horizon. 
3.3 CARR, Component Decomposition, and Macro Information 
In contrast to models based on closing returns, CARR directly captures the price range created by the high 
and low prices, thereby retaining part of the intraday information at a relatively low data cost [5, 20]. On the 
basis of this, score-driven CARR models will reflect conditional heteroscedasticity through dynamic parameter 
updates, whereas multiplicative CARR will incorporate long -term, short-term, and high-frequency measures 
[5, 6]. Another group of studies breaks down volatility into direction, magnitude, long-term trends, and market 
states. Quantile decomposition and state transitions have been found to be able to differentiate between times 
of high and low volatility [4]. Economic uncertainty direction and cycle impact lo ng-term volatility in an 
asymmetric way [3]. Although these models are more interpretable economically, the low frequency and

4. Implied Volatility Methods Based on Option Information 
Implied volatility is based on market estimates of volatility until the end of a contract and can give a 
prediction of the future beyond past returns [1, 14]. Pricing assumptions, market frictions, and risk 
compensation also affect the price of options. As such, although implied volatility is forward-looking, it might 
not necessarily be an unbiased predictor of future actual volatility. 
4.1 Additional Information Provided by Implied Volatility 
Implied volatility based on the traditional Black -Scholes (BS) is based upon certain pricing models and 
individual option quotes. Model -free implied volatility aggregates information across multiple strike prices, 
thereby reducing reliance on the assumptio ns of any particular pricing model. Evidence based on the SSE 50 
ETF options suggests that the model-free implied volatility reflects the information provided by the traditional 
BS implied volatility and much of the historical volatility information [11]. In their research on the options of 
the S&P 500 index, Jiang and Tian [24] discovered that model-free implied volatility contains information on 
both Black-Scholes implied volatility and historical realized volatility, and is more efficient in forecasting 
future volatility. Yet, its performance also depends on whether the quality of the quote, market liquidity, and 
forecast horizon are well -fitted [24]. Implied volatility measurements can have a significant level of noise 
when contracts are rarely traded, strike price distributions are scarce, or bid-ask spreads are large. 
4.2 Variance Risk Premium and Forecasting Bias 
The implied variance is made up of the expectations of future volatility and the compensation that investors 
require to cover the volatility risk; therefore, predicted and observed volatility are not entirely consistent with 
a risk-neutral measure [12, 14]. Studies by Bollerslev et al. [25] suggest that the variance risk premium, defined 
as the difference between model-free implied variance and realized variance, has asset pricing information and 
this also implies that implied measures have risk neutral estimates as well as compensating risk. Studies of the 
Chinese options market have shown that predictive and pricing information is present in the variance risk 
premium itself; detecting and controlling such a premium can help in eliminating the prediction bi as of the 
implied volatility [11, 12]. The implied volatility forecast error can be due to the market expectation bias, risk 
compensation, as well as liquidity and quotation frictions. 
4.3 Supplementary Insights from Options Trading Volume and High-Frequency 
Information 
Besides the price, the volume of options trading also indicates what kind of contracts are of interest to 
information traders. Ni et al. [26] discovered that the net demand for volatility by non -market makers within 
the options market is a predictor of the future realized volatility of the underlying stock, meaning that trading 
activity may give volatility information without the need for option prices. Research on the SSE 50 ETF 
options indicates that the prediction ability of HAR-type models can be enhanced by trading volume in deeply 
out-of-the-money options and that the direction of the effect varies across call and put trading volumes [13]. 
Option prices are indicative of market expectations, high-frequency volatility indicates recent factual volatility, 
and trading volume describes trading activity. The joint application of this data may increase the amount of 
information available to risk models [11, 19]. The independent incremental value of each of the variables 
should be determined by nested comparisons or ablation tests. 
5. Machine Learning Methods Based on Multi-Source Data 
The main application of machine learning is to filter and integrate high-dimensional variables and to capture 
underlying nonlinear connections. Their predictive benefits are then dependent on the quality of data, feature 
engineering, and validation plan.

5.1 Integration of High-Dimensional Nonlinear Relationships and Har 
Sparse variable selection can be done through lasso, random forests can do it by identifying nonlinearities 
and variable interactions, and neural networks can do it as they can fit complex time dependencies. Current 
studies usually integrate such methods w ith the help of the HAR structures to increase the functionality of 
feature processing but still maintain the daily, weekly, and monthly memory features. Research on Chinese 
high-frequency data shows that jump components, variable selection, and nonlinear fitting may enhance out-
of-sample predictions of certain samples [15]. The GAT-TCN model suggested by Lü Chengshuang et al. [2] 
considers both stock correlations and time dependencies at the same time. There was no systematic evidence 
that all nonlinear models were better than other models at all time horizons when comparing global stock 
indices [16]. Complexity of the models might imply predictive benefits only in the case that the incremental 
information is stable and the sample size is large enough. 
5.2 Text Sentiment and Macroeconomic Uncertainty 
Text and macroeconomic variables increase the information edge of volatility prediction. Lü Chengshuang 
et al. [2] used fine -tuned large language models to transform the text of the stock forum into sentiment 
indicators that were used as inputs to a forecasting network, along with price and technical features. In their 
ablation results, they found that sent iment information provides incremental predictive value in the specific 
stock sample. Uncertainty in the economy is studied more extensively in relation to long -term volatility and 
its variation in direction and cycles [3], whereas higher frequency price v ariables are mainly used to make 
shorter term predictions [15]. This finding indicates that various information sources can be associated with 
various time scales, but the most effective way to combine information from multiple sources remains unclear. 
5.3 Overfitting, Information Leakage, and Reproducibility 
The credibility of machine-learning results depends critically on the validation procedure. Financial data is 
highly time -ordered. Randomly splitting the sample or conducting standardization, variable selection, and 
hyperparameter selection on all samples will permit future information to creep into the training process. In -
sample fit could also merely indicate the memory of historical noise [2, 15]. Comparisons of models must be 
based on out -of-sample validation using chronological rolling updates and must be compared with simple 
benchmarks under the same information set, forecast horizon, and loss function. The statistical measures like 
MCS can also be used to check if the errors differ more than sampling variation [17, 18]. Until there are simple 
models competing at the level of the best -performing models, it cannot be stated that complex models have a 
steady advantage [16]. 
6. Comparison and Synthesis of Three Classes of Volatility Forecasting Methods 
These three methods have significant differences between them on the basis of their information foundation 
and processing techniques. Table 1 shows the information sources and representative methods of every 
category and compares them based on their key advantages, limitations, and areas of application. Traditional 
econometric models perform well in terms of low data requirements and interpretability; implied volatility 
derives its main value from the market's forward -looking information; and machine learning provides more 
flexibility when working with multi -source and high -dimensional systems and nonlinear systems. All three 
methods are also limited by historical information lags, risk premium, liquidity, and the risks of overfitting 
and high reproducibility costs. 
6.1 Information Sources, Forecast Horizon, and Market Conditions 
The questions that a model can answer depend on its information sources. The historical returns and the 
realized measures are well-suited to describe past dynamics in volatility. Multi -source variables might reflect 
sentiment and correlation structures that cannot be accounted for by prices, and option pri ces reflect market 
expectations associated with the maturity of the contract [1, 2, 14, 15]. There is also the impact of the forecast 
horizon on the comparative strength of different approaches. Intraday price and jump terms are mostly useful 
in predicting short-term movements, whereas macroeconomic uncertainty and long -term terms are more 
appropriate to explain mid- to long-term movements; option data is affected by time to maturity [3, 4, 8, 13]. 
Straightforward linear models could be competitive in quiet times because the parameters are stable, but in

6.2 The Trade-off Between Explanatory Power, Forward-Looking Nature, and Flexibility 
Traditional econometric model parameter structures can assist in explaining the causes of volatility 
persistence, but when structural constraints are strong, they might prevent new information from being 
considered. Implied volatility is forward -looking but needs a disentanglement between actual expectations, 
risk premium, and market frictions. Machine learning minimizes limitations on functional forms yet raises data 
demands, parameter-tuning difficulties and reproducibility costs, [1, 11, 12, 15, 16]. The selection of methods 
must be based on the goals of research. The mechanism -based explanation needs an identifiable parameter 
structure, and the extraction of short-term market expectations depends on the quality of options data, whereas 
high-dimensional forecasts need strict out-of-sample validation. 
6.3 Unified Evaluation and Integrated Research 
To achieve a proper comparison, there are at least four standardized conditions necessary, that is, the 
volatility proxy variables, the cutoff point concerning forecast information, the forecast horizon, and the out -
of-sample loss function [16, 17, 18]. The statistical and economic assessments need to be reported concurrently. 
A lower error indicates that the forecast is closer to the proxy variable. The economic worth of a model needs 
to be evaluated on the basis of observable changes in option pricing, VaR coverage, or investment decisions 
[16, 19]. Some of the studies on integration have gone beyond the limits of these three methodological groups, 
such as integrating machine learning into HAR models or using options and high -frequency data to measure 
risks together [15, 19]. The future comparisons ought to aim at determining what kinds of information can give 
steady incremental benefits depending on what conditions prevail. Following this, Table 2 presents 
representative models in terms of their research ro le, depicting the relationship between dynamic nature of 
volatility, extraction of information on its future behavior, and combination of various sources of information

7. Conclusions and Future Directions 
The paper presents three research directions in the field of stock volatility prediction according to the 
information sources. According to current knowledge, traditional econometric models have relative strengths 
in their ability to explain structures, im plied volatility provides forward -looking market information, and 
machine learning has a comparative advantage in its ability to process high -dimensional information. The 
constraints of these advantages include the quality of the data, forecast time scales, the state of the market, and 
validation plans. The performance of models is highly conditional, and the discrepancies in the outcomes of 
studies are frequently more effectively accounted for by the source of the information and the structure of the 
assessments than by the complexity of the models. 
There are four remaining drawbacks in the current literature. First, most empirical researches conducted 
domestically focuses on the CSI 300 Index, the SSE 50 ETF options, or particular phases of the market. Their 
findings are not sufficiently supported by evidence that suggests they could be applied to different markets, 
assets, and extreme market conditions. Second, various studies use different proxies of volatilities and loss 
functions, and information timing, forecasting windows, and division of samples differ greatly. The 
performance of the model can be due to both methodological differences and differences in the research design. 
Third, while many studies rely primarily on statistical for ecast errors, joint tests of economic value—such as 
option pricing, VaR coverage, and investment decisions —remain insufficient. Fourth, there is no consensus 
on whether machine learning can consistently outperform simple linear models. Existing research also rarely 
systematically decomposes whether improvements stem from new information, variable selection, or nonlinear 
structures. 
These shortcomings may be addressed by conducting future research in three directions. Initially, proxy 
measures, cut -off points of information, forecast horizons, and rolling out -of-sample strategies need to be 
standardized. Based on this, comparative studies of various markets, asset types, and levels of volatility ought 
to be carried out, and report both forecast inaccuracies as well as their uncertainty. Then, statistical precision 
and economic worth must be considered as part of one assessment system to assess if improvement of forecasts 
may always decrease the error in option pricing, improve the coverage of VaR, or raise the returns adjusted to 
risks. Lastly, multi -source integration studies involve both ablation tests and time -series validation. It is 
necessary to determine the independent incremental value of high -frequency, option, macroeconomic, and 
text-based information that will help define the applicability limits of more complicated models in comparison 
with simple ones.
