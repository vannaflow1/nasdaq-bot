Abstract
This paper proposes a deep learning-driven operator splitting scheme for high-
dimensional free-boundary problems, with American option pricing as a representative
application. In a market with proportional transaction costs, a stochastic liquidity risk
with an empirically validated nonlinear drift based on S&P 500 data, and two-factor
stochastic volatility, the pricing problem takes the form of a five-dimensional highly
nonlinear variational inequality. The proposed scheme decomposes this variational
inequality at each time layer into an elliptic continuation equation approximated by a
physics-informed neural network and a closed-form projection step enforcing the early-
exercise constraint. A conditionalℓ ∞ stability bound is derived under stated resolvent
and Lipschitz assumptions. The scheme is validated against finite-difference and least-
squares Monte Carlo benchmarks, and both the option prices and the learned exercise
boundary show close agreement with the reference solutions. Sensitivity analyses with
respect to the key model parameters are also reported.
Keywords: Free boundary problem; American options; Physics-informed neural net-
works; Operator splitting; Optimal exercise boundary.
1 Introduction
Free-boundary problems arise across a wide range of applications in mathematical finance,
and American option pricing is among the most classical of them. In this setting, the option
value and the optimal exercise boundary must be determined jointly, which poses a substan-
tial challenge to analytical treatment. A rich set of numerical methods has therefore been
developed to address this problem, including the finite-difference scheme [5], the binomial
tree method [9], and the least-squares Monte Carlo method [24].
Transaction costs are one of the most direct market frictions. An early line of research
preserves the replicating-portfolio logic of Black–Scholes but replaces continuous rebalanc-
ing with a discrete strategy, thereby absorbing the associated costs into a modified effective

volatility. Leland [23] derived this adjustment for the option writer, whereas Hoggard et
al. [20] obtained the corresponding price for the option holder, with the difference being
the sign of the transaction cost term. A second line of research adopts a stochastic-control
perspective based on utility maximization. Davis et al. [10] incorporate the investor’s risk
preference and derive the utility indifference price, which is equivalent to solving two Hamil-
ton–Jacobi–Bellman equations. The pricing approaches above were developed primarily for
European claims. For American options, the early-exercise feature already renders the prob-
lem substantially harder, and the presence of additional market frictions makes the resulting
free-boundary problem considerably more difficult.
Beyond explicit fees, liquidity acts as a pervasive implicit trading cost arising from bid-
ask spreads [1, 2], market depth and price impact [17, 25]. In option pricing, the stochastic
liquidity model of Feng et al.[13] has been widely adopted, in which the liquidity discount
factor follows a stochastic process and market liquidity is modeled as a mean-reverting pro-
cess. Feng et al.[14] later empirically demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach for
European option pricing, and the model has since been extended to more general claims and
richer market settings [6, 11, 27, 32, 34, 35]. Building on this framework, Yan et al. [33]
recently combined proportional transaction costs with liquidity risk for American options,
albeit under constant volatility. Constant-volatility models, however, cannot reproduce the
smile and term structure of implied volatility [12], so stochastic volatility is strongly moti-
vated by empirical evidence. Although single-factor stochastic-volatility models [19, 21, 31]
have become standard tools, a single variance factor is often insufficient to capture the entire
implied-volatility surface [8, 18, 29], which encodes both short-run variance fluctuations and
the slower evolution of the variance level itself. Incorporating two-factor stochastic volatility
into the framework of Yan et al. [33] would increase the dimensionality of an already chal-
lenging free-boundary problem, rendering both the analytical formulation and the numerical
solution considerably more difficult.
The choice of numerical method is therefore crucial. Neither traditional grid-based
schemes nor Monte Carlo methods are fully satisfactory here. Finite-difference and finite-
element methods scale poorly in dimension [4] and are further complicated by mixed-derivative
terms, whereas Monte Carlo methods are dimension-robust but can be sensitive to the re-
gression basis near the exercise boundary [26]. Operator-splitting methods improve on this
by addressing the complementarity structure of American options, decoupling diffusion from
early exercise [7, 22]; however, conventional implementations remain grid-based, which again
limits their applicability in high dimensions. Deep-learning methods offer a mesh-free alter-
native, such as the Deep Galerkin Method [30] and PINN [28], which target high-dimensional
PDEs. These techniques have also been applied to Black-Scholes equations [16] and, more
relevantly, to optimal stopping problems [3, 15]. Nevertheless, applying these methods to
American options is complicated by the variational-inequality structure, where the non-
smooth payoff and the moving free boundary create sharp features that are difficult to
approximate.
Motivated by these observations, this paper develops an American put option pricing
model that combines exogenous transaction fees with endogenous liquidity risk under a two-
factor stochastic volatility framework. Empirically, we document nonlinear persistence in a
market-liquidity proxy constructed from the S&P 500 index and its constituents, a finding
that motivates modeling the liquidity factor with a nonlinear mean-reverting drift scaled
by the transaction costs rate. Under discrete proportional hedging, the holder and the
2
writer satisfy distinct nonlinear pricing equations, with the transaction costs terms entering
with opposite signs. Since the holder holds the early-exercise right, the holder’s value is
determined by a variational inequality that yields the optimal exercise boundary, which
the writer’s equation then takes as given; the model therefore produces a price interval
rather than a single arbitrage-free value. To solve the resulting high-dimensional problem,
we propose a PINN-based operator splitting scheme, denoted as PINN-splitting. At each
time layer, this scheme decomposes the variational inequality into an elliptic continuation
equation approximated by a PINN and a closed-form projection step enforcing the early-
exercise constraint, thereby avoiding the exposure of the network to the non-smooth obstacle.
We then derive a conditionalℓ ∞ stability bound for this scheme under the stated resolvent
and Lipschitz assumptions.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 formulates the market
model and derives the nonlinear pricing equations for the holder and the writer accordingly.
Section 3 presents the PINN-splitting numerical scheme and reports numerical experiments,
with Section 4 concluding the paper.

empirically testable. The empirical relevance of this nonlinear feedback term is validated in
Appendix A, where we show that the estimated coefficient onL ξt is statistically significant
at the 1% level using S&P 500 market data.
Before constructing the pricing model, we first discuss how transaction costs affect the
valuation of American options, which raises two modeling issues. First, continuous rebal-
ancing is infeasible, as it would generate unbounded hedging costs, so the portfolio must be
adjusted at discrete intervals. Second, the holder and the writer face asymmetric valuation
problems, since their pricing PDEs differ in the sign of the nonlinear transaction costs term,
reflecting their opposing hedging positions. As a result, no unique option price exists under
transaction costs. The holder’s valuation provides a lower bound and the writer’s an upper
bound, together defining the viable price interval.
We begin by deriving the PDE for the holder of an American put option in the contin-
uation region. The PDE for the writer’s price can be derived following a similar procedure,
with a reversal in the sign of the transaction cost term, reflecting the opposite position held
in the hedge portfolio.

The assumptions in Eq. (8) serve a key economic purpose: they ensure that the final pricing
PDE for the target optionVremains independent of the specific choice of hedge instruments
V1,V 2, andV 3. By forcing the combined sensitivity of the hedge portfolio to each risk
factor—S,v,θ, andL—to vanish, we decouple the transaction costs, which originate from
rebalancing the hedge, from any explicit functional dependence on the auxiliary options.
This is logically consistent: the fair price of an option should be governed by its contractual
features and the dynamics of the market state variables, not by the particular derivatives
employed for risk management.
The representation in Eq. (9) admits a natural interpretation: each hedge position ∆ i
adjusts dynamically in proportion to the inverse delta of the corresponding hedge option,
scaled by a time-varying factorg i(t). This factor captures the aggregate exposure to the
ith risk factor that must be neutralized, while the explicit dependence on the form ofV i is
absorbed and does not appear in the pricing equation. Consequently, although the hedging
strategy involves the auxiliary optionsV 1,V 2, andV 3, the resulting pricing PDE forV
is self-contained. The hedge portfolio offsets the additional risks induced by two-factor
stochastic volatility and the liquidity factor without allowing the choice of hedge instruments
to influence the target option’s price.

3 Numerical methods and stability analysis
Having established the model in Section 2, we now turn to the numerical solution of the
resulting high-dimensional free-boundary problem, as no closed-form solution is available
for the pricing equations (14) and (15). This task faces two difficulties. On the one hand,
the four-dimensional state space makes classical mesh-based methods computationally pro-
hibitive, as the cost of a finite-difference grid grows exponentially with the dimension. On
the other hand, American options introduce two sources of nonsmoothness, namely the kink
in the payoff and the free boundary generated by the early-exercise constraint, both of which
are known to degrade the accuracy of standard neural PDE solvers.
These two difficulties suggest a complementary combination. Neural PDE solvers such as
the Deep Galerkin Method of Sirignano and Spiliopoulos [30] provide a mesh-free approach
that scales well with the dimension of the state space. Operator splitting, in turn, decou-
ples the smooth continuation PDE from the nonsmooth early-exercise constraint at each
time step, allowing the neural network to approximate only the smooth component while
the obstacle constraint is enforced analytically. Deep learning thus addresses the curse of
dimensionality, while operator splitting restores the smoothness required for accurate neural
approximation. This is the essence of the deep learning-driven operator splitting method
proposed in this section, whose stability we analyze below.
3.1 The deep learning-driven operator splitting scheme
At each time layer, the continuation equation is solved by a neural network, while the early-
exercise condition is enforced separately through a projection step. The operator splitting
component builds on the method of Ikonen and Toivanen [22], which decouples the com-
plementarity constraint from the PDE solve at each time step and has been shown to be
more efficient than projected successive over-relaxation in the classical Black–Scholes setting;
Chen and Shen [7] rigorously established its stability and convergence. However, existing
implementations assume a frictionless market and are designed for a single pricing equation.
In our framework, the continuation problem at each splitting step is still four-dimensional
and nonlinear, so we adopt a PINN as the inner solver. Since transaction costs separate the
holder’s and writer’s equations in our model, we additionally develop a second scheme for
the writer, whose stopping region is inherited from the holder’s exercise policy.

4.2.3 Loss convergence at the first splitting layer
Here, we examine the loss convergence of the first continuation-solving step. This step is
not warm-started by a later layer and therefore provides a clean diagnostic for the stability
of the nonlinear solver. Since the transaction-cost-adjusted equation is nonlinear and the
network must be initialized solely from the payoff function, this step represents one of the
more challenging stages in the splitting procedure. A stable loss decrease here therefore
supports the robustness of the overall scheme.

As shown in Figure 2, the loss decreases steadily during the Adam training stage, from
approximately 2.56×10 4 to below 5.62×10 −2 over 6000 epochs, a reduction of about
five orders of magnitude. The smoothed trend remains monotone and does not exhibit
visible oscillations or divergence, suggesting that the optimizer copes well with the nonconvex
landscape induced by the nonlinear transaction-cost term. Moreover, the smooth decay of the
loss indicates that the Sobol-based residual sampling does not introduce excessive noise into
the gradient estimates. This confirms that the residual of the first split continuation problem
is reduced in a stable manner. Together with the pricing comparisons and the learnedE
profiles, this provides additional evidence for the numerical reliability of the PINN-splitting
method.

the fact that the writer’s price always exceeds the holder’s price under nonzero transaction
costs, yields economically consistent comparative statics.
5 Conclusion
In this work, we propose a novel operator splitting scheme integrated with PINN to solve
high-dimensional free-boundary problems, focusing on the valuation of American options un-
der complex market frictions. This hybrid architecture decouples the early-exercise bound-
ary from the continuation PDE, enabling the neural solver to maintain high approximation
accuracy despite the curse of dimensionality and the non-differentiable payoff kink. We eval-
uate the algorithm’s robustness by pricing American options under proportional transaction
costs, stochastic liquidity risk, and two-factor stochastic volatility, a framework validated
by S&P 500 empirical evidence. Numerical results show that the PINN-splitting scheme
not only preserves theoretical stability but also agrees closely with FDM and LSMC bench-
marks. Overall, the PINN-splitting scheme offers a modular, scalable, and efficient approach
to a broad class of free-boundary problems in financial engineering and related fields, with
possible extensions to jump-diffusion processes and higher-dimensional derivatives in future
work.
