Abstract
This study examines thirty equity–bond and multi-asset exchange-traded funds (ETFs) listed
in the United States, Canada, and Australia using daily U.S.-dollar returns over 2020–2024. A
rolling-window framework compares minimum-variance and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)
portfolio optimization under long-only and long–short constraints against an equally weighted
benchmark. Performance is assessed using risk-adjusted measures, downside risk, extreme-tail
estimation, and conditional volatility models. Optimized portfolios exhibit lower empirical VaR
and CVaR than the benchmark, with long–short strategies generally providing greater downside-
risk reduction. However, the equally weighted benchmark achieves the highest reported Sharpe,
Sortino, and Rachev ratios. GARCH and FIGARCH comparisons favor GARCH based on
information criteria. A historically estimated Normal Inverse Gaussian model further gener-
ates European option prices and model-implied volatility surfaces under a martingale-corrected
pricing specification. The findings highlight trade-offs between risk reduction, risk-adjusted
performance, and practical implementation costs.
Keywords: Equity–bond diversification; exchange-traded funds; Conditional Value-at-Risk; extreme-
value theory; long-memory volatility; L´evy processes; Normal Inverse Gaussian distribution; implied
volatility.
1 Introduction
Balanced equity–bond and multi-asset portfolios have long occupied a central position in strategic
asset allocation and institutional portfolio management. The classical justification for combining
1
arXiv:2611.21897v1 [q-fin.PM] 16 Nov 2026
equities and fixed-income securities rests on imperfect correlations between growth-sensitive and
defensive asset classes, thereby allowing investors to improve risk-adjusted returns through diver-
sification (Elton & Gruber, 1997; Markowitz, 1952). Multi-asset allocation strategies extend this
diversification approach by incorporating additional asset classes with potentially different risk and
return characteristics.
Over the last two decades, exchange-traded funds (ETFs) have become important vehicles for
implementing such allocation strategies because of their transparency, liquidity, relatively low costs,
and broad market accessibility (Ben-David, Franzoni, & Moussawi, 2018; Madhavan, 2012).
Recent market developments, however, have raised important questions regarding the robust-
ness of traditional diversification strategies. The period 2020–2024 included the COVID-19 crisis,
substantial inflationary pressures, aggressive monetary tightening, and changes in global interest-
rate expectations. These developments highlight the importance of evaluating portfolio strategies
not only in terms of overall volatility and risk-adjusted returns, but also in terms of their exposure
to downside risk and extreme market losses.
Traditional portfolio optimization methods, particularly the mean–variance framework, primar-
ily characterize risk through return variance. Although such approaches provide a well-established
foundation for portfolio allocation, variance treats positive and negative deviations from expected
returns symmetrically. Consequently, portfolios constructed using variance-based optimization may
not adequately account for asymmetric losses or extreme downside outcomes. This limitation is
particularly relevant when evaluating equity–bond and multi-asset portfolios, where diversification
across asset classes does not necessarily eliminate exposure to substantial losses during periods of
market stress.
These considerations motivate the use of downside-risk-based portfolio optimization methods.
In particular, Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) provides a coherent risk measure that evaluates
expected losses beyond a specified VaR threshold (Pflug, 2000; Rockafellar & Uryasev, 2000). Unlike
variance, CVaR directly focuses on adverse outcomes in the tail of the loss distribution. Compar-
ing minimum-variance and CVaR-based portfolio strategies therefore allows us to examine how
alternative risk objectives influence portfolio allocation, downside exposure, and out-of-sample per-
formance.
Beyond portfolio optimization, distributional characteristics such as skewness and heavy tails
provide additional information about portfolio risk that may not be fully captured by variance-
based measures. Examining these features can therefore contribute to a broader assessment of
downside and extreme-tail risk across alternative portfolio strategies.
Another important challenge concerns the distributional assumptions underlying classical port-
folio theory. Empirical asset returns frequently exhibit skewness, excess kurtosis, and persistent
volatility dynamics inconsistent with Gaussian models (Cont, 2001; Mandelbrot, 1963).
L´evy processes provide a flexible framework for modeling financial returns because they can
accommodate jumps, heavy tails, and asymmetric return distributions while maintaining analytical
tractability. Among these models, the Normal Inverse Gaussian (NIG) process introduced by
Barndorff-Nielsen (1997) provides a particularly flexible specification for capturing distributional
characteristics observed in financial return data.
2
This paper develops a unified empirical framework for analyzing equity–bond and multi-asset
ETFs over the period 2020–2024, characterized by substantial market fluctuations and changing
economic conditions. Our contribution is fourfold.
First, we provide a cross-country empirical analysis of equity–bond and multi-asset ETFs span-
ning the United States, Canada, and Australia. Second, we compare variance-based and CVaR-
based portfolio optimization methods under both long-only and long–short constraints, examin-
ing downside risk, extreme-tail exposure, portfolio exposure, turnover, transaction-cost sensitivity,
and out-of-sample portfolio performance. Third, we examine volatility persistence in the portfo-
lio return series using GARCH and FIGARCH specifications. Fourth, we develop a model-based
option-pricing analysis under the NIG L´evy framework to complement the historical portfolio and
risk analysis.
The empirical analysis considers portfolios constructed from thirty equity–bond and multi-asset
ETFs representing a broad range of asset allocation strategies, including conservative income-
oriented and growth-oriented portfolios. Portfolio performance is evaluated using a 504-trading-day
rolling estimation window and subsequent out-of-sample daily simple returns. The evaluation in-
corporates multiple risk and performance measures, including Sharpe ratios, Sortino ratios, Rachev
ratios, Value-at-Risk, Conditional Value-at-Risk, and extreme-tail estimators (Lee, Shirvani, Afroz,
Rachev, & Fabozzi, 2026). Portfolio exposure, turnover, and sensitivity to proportional transaction
costs are also examined to assess the implications of portfolio rebalancing.
In addition, volatility dynamics are examined using GARCH and FIGARCH models, while
model-implied option prices and corresponding implied-volatility surfaces are generated under the
NIG specification using Fourier-transform methods.
The empirical results show that the optimized portfolios exhibit lower out-of-sample daily
volatility, maximum drawdown, VaR, and CVaR than the equally weighted benchmark. The long–
short strategies consistently achieve lower values of these risk measures than their corresponding
long-only portfolios. However, these reductions in realized risk do not consistently translate into
higher Sharpe, Sortino, or Rachev ratios. The transaction-cost sensitivity analysis further shows
that the performance of long–short strategies can be substantially affected by portfolio turnover,
particularly under higher assumed transaction-cost rates.
The analysis also identifies persistent volatility dynamics, with the GARCH specification pro-
viding an adequate representation of the observed volatility clustering. Furthermore, the fitted NIG
model captures important distributional characteristics of portfolio returns, including skewness and
heavy-tailed behavior, and provides the basis for the model-implied option-pricing analysis.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the data, return con-
struction procedures, and descriptive characteristics of the ETF universe. Section 3 presents the
portfolio optimization framework. Section 4 examines empirical portfolio risk-return performance
and extreme-tail behavior using Hill tail-index estimation. Section 5 examines volatility persistence
using GARCH and FIGARCH models. Section 6 develops the model-based option-pricing analysis
under the NIG L´evy framework. Section 7 concludes.

2 Data and Summary Statistics
This section describes the dataset, return construction methodology, and principal empirical char-
acteristics of the balanced and multi-asset ETF universe analyzed in this study. The objective
is to establish a consistent cross-country framework for evaluating portfolio diversification and
downside-risk behavior under changing market conditions.
2.1 Data Description
The empirical analysis is based on a sample of thirty equity–bond and multi-asset exchange-traded
funds listed in three developed financial markets: the United States, Canada, and Australia. The
sample consists of sixteen U.S. ETFs, ten Canadian ETFs, and four Australian ETFs. These
funds collectively represent a broad spectrum of strategic allocation profiles, ranging from conser-
vative income-oriented portfolios with relatively high bond exposure to aggressive growth-oriented
portfolios dominated by equities.
Balanced ETFs provide diversified exposure to equity and fixed-income markets within a single
tradable instrument and therefore constitute an economically meaningful laboratory for studying
portfolio diversification under realistic investment conditions. Although several ETFs in the sample
contain modest allocations to alternative assets such as real estate investment trusts, preferred
securities, commodities, or tactical allocation overlays, the dominant sources of systematic risk
remain the underlying equity and fixed-income exposures. Consistent with both academic literature
and industry classification standards, the ETFs are therefore categorized according to their primary
equity–bond allocation structure.
Daily adjusted closing prices are obtained from Yahoo Finance over the period January 2,
2020 through December 31, 2024. This sample period intentionally captures several economically
distinct market environments, including the COVID-19 market collapse, the subsequent recovery
phase, the inflation-driven monetary tightening cycle of 2022, and the later stabilization period.
These changing market conditions provide a useful setting for evaluating diversification benefits
and comparing alternative portfolio optimization frameworks.
In addition to the ETF universe, the Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) is included as
a benchmark representing broad U.S. equity market performance. The DJIA serves as a useful
reference point for comparing the behavior of balanced portfolios with concentrated large-cap equity
exposure during periods of market turbulence and recovery.
Daily ETF closing prices for funds listed in the United States, Canada, and Australia were
expressed in U.S. dollars before calculating returns. Canadian-listed ETF prices were converted
using the DEXCAUS daily exchange-rate series (Canadian dollars per U.S. dollar), while Australian-
listed ETF prices were converted using the DEXUSAL series (U.S. dollars per Australian dollar),
both obtained from the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) database maintained by the
Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (US), 2026a,
2026b). ETF closing prices and exchange-rate observations were aligned to the available U.S.
trading dates. For each date, the corresponding closing prices reported by Yahoo Finance for the
4
U.S., Canadian, and Australian markets were matched according to their respective trading dates.
Thus, observations associated with the same date represent closing prices from the respective local
exchanges rather than prices recorded simultaneously.
Differences in market closing times were not explicitly adjusted for, as the analysis was con-
ducted using daily observations rather than intraday synchronized prices. When an exchange-rate
observation was unavailable on a corresponding ETF observation date, the most recent preceding
available exchange rate was used. Under the rolling-window framework, portfolio weights were
estimated using observations through date t and applied to returns on date t + 1. Although the
exchanges operate in different time zones, the Australian and Canadian closing prices associated
with date t would ordinarily have been available by the close of the U.S. market on that date.
Thus, calendar-date alignment of the ETF closing prices does not, by itself, require future ETF
price information when weights are formed after the U.S. market close. However, the analysis does
not explicitly verify the publication timing of the corresponding daily exchange rates or account
for the timing of trade execution across exchanges.
Differences in international trading calendars resulted in missing ETF price observations on
some U.S. trading dates. These missing values were imputed using a forward-filling procedure,
whereby each missing ETF price was replaced with the most recent available price from a preceding
trading date. In total, 374 of 37, 740 ETF price observations (0.99%) were imputed, occurring across
46 of the 1, 258 dates in the sample. Because forward filling relies exclusively on previously available
price observations, the imputation procedure does not introduce future price information. However,
this approach may generate zero returns on dates when an ETF did not trade and may affect the
measured return dynamics.
Accordingly, the reported out-of-sample results should be interpreted as a daily-frequency port-
folio backtest based on date-aligned closing prices rather than as the performance of a fully syn-
chronized, executable cross-market trading strategy. The analysis does not explicitly account for
cross-market execution timing. Portfolio turnover and gross and short exposures are examined sep-
arately in Section 3.6, while the effects of proportional transaction costs are evaluated through a
sensitivity analysis in Section 4.3. However, short-selling costs, financing costs, and other leverage-
related implementation considerations are not explicitly incorporated.

3 Portfolio Optimization Framework
This section develops the portfolio optimization framework used to evaluate alternative allocation
strategies under changing market conditions. The objective is to examine how different notions
of portfolio risk behave in the presence of heavy-tailed return distributions, unstable correlation
structures, and persistent volatility dynamics. Particular emphasis is placed on the comparison
between variance-based and downside-risk-based portfolio construction methods. To account for the
evolving nature of financial markets, we employ a rolling-window estimation framework. Portfolio
9
weights are estimated using a fixed window of 504 trading days, corresponding approximately to
two years of historical observations. For each out-of-sample return date t, target weights wp,k,t
are estimated using the preceding 504 daily return observations, indexed from t − 504 through
t − 1, and are applied to the simple ETF returns realized on date t. The estimation window
subsequently advances by one trading day and the procedure is repeated throughout the sample
period. This rolling procedure updates allocations as new observations become available and allows
the estimated portfolio weights to respond to changes in historical volatility, correlations, and
downside-risk characteristics. Figures 3 and 4 present the full-sample (in-sample) efficient frontiers
obtained under the mean–variance and CVaR optimization frameworks using the 3-month U.S.
Treasury yield as a short-term risk-free-rate proxy and the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield as an
alternative reference yield.
The full-sample efficient frontiers are constructed using daily logarithmic returns calculated from
the forward-filled ETF prices expressed in U.S. dollars. These in-sample frontier calculations are
distinct from the rolling portfolio optimizations and out-of-sample wealth calculations, which use
daily simple returns. For the frontier comparisons, the annualized Treasury yields are converted to
daily log-equivalent rates using log(1+y/100)/252, where y denotes the annualized yield in percent.
The reference rate for each comparison is based on the Treasury yield observed on December 31,
2024. These reference rates are used to identify the maximum reward-to-risk portfolios along the
estimated frontiers.
Under both frameworks, the individual ETFs occupy a wide range of risk–return positions
relative to the corresponding efficient frontiers. The use of the two Treasury benchmarks also allows
the sensitivity of the optimal portfolio allocation to the choice of reference yield to be examined.
The mean–variance framework measures portfolio risk through return variance, whereas the CVaR
framework focuses on losses in the lower tail of the return distribution. The two approaches therefore
evaluate portfolio risk from different perspectives.
Under the mean–variance framework, the maximum-Sharpe portfolio is strongly concentrated
in NTSX under both risk-free-rate specifications. Using the 3-month Treasury yield, the optimal
portfolio consists of 86.882% NTSX and 13.118% PWS. When the 10-year Treasury yield is used,
the allocation changes to 90.161% NTSX and 9.839% PWS. Thus, NTSX remains the dominant
component under both specifications, while the allocation to PWS decreases when the 10-year
Treasury yield is used as the alternative reference yield.

Having examined the full-sample efficient frontiers under the mean–variance and CVaR frame-
works, we next turn to the broader empirical comparison of the portfolio strategies considered in
this study. We consider three portfolio construction approaches:
• mean–variance optimization,
• Conditional Value-at-Risk optimization,
• the equally weighted portfolio (EWP) as a benchmark specification.
The empirical analysis evaluates both long-only and long–short portfolio configurations in order
to examine how portfolio constraints affect diversification and downside-risk management. The
portfolio strategies considered throughout the paper are defined as follows:
• LO-MVP: Long-only minimum-variance portfolio,
• LO-C95: Long-only CVaR portfolio at the 95% confidence level,
• LO-C99: Long-only CVaR portfolio at the 99% confidence level,
• LS-MVP: long–short minimum-variance portfolio,
• LS-C95: long–short CVaR portfolio at the 95% confidence level,
• LS-C99: long–short CVaR portfolio at the 99% confidence level.

The mean–variance framework introduced by Markowitz (1952) remains one of the founda-

negative return deviations symmetrically. It therefore does not directly target extreme downside
losses.

The equally weighted portfolio remains an important benchmark in empirical asset allocation stud-

4.1 Portfolio Risk–Return Performance
To evaluate portfolio performance comprehensively, we consider several complementary risk-adjusted
performance and risk measures, including the Sharpe ratio, Sortino ratio, daily volatility, Rachev
ratio, and maximum drawdown. Each metric captures a different dimension of portfolio behavior
and therefore provides insight into the interaction between return generation, downside exposure,
and tail-risk sensitivity. For the risk-adjusted measures requiring a risk-free rate, the 3-month U.S.
Treasury yield is used as the risk-free-rate proxy and converted to an effective daily rate to match
the frequency of the portfolio returns.
17
The Sharpe ratio evaluates excess return relative to total return volatility and remains one
of the most widely used measures of mean–variance efficiency (Sharpe, 1966). However, because
the Sharpe ratio relies on variance as the underlying risk measure, it may provide a misleading
characterization of performance when returns exhibit asymmetry, excess kurtosis, or substantial
tail dependence.
The Sortino ratio partially addresses this limitation by focusing exclusively on downside volatil-
ity rather than total return dispersion (Sortino & Price, 1994). Nevertheless, both Sharpe and
Sortino ratios remain fundamentally moment-based performance measures and therefore may still
inadequately capture extreme downside exposure under heavy-tailed return distributions.
To incorporate tail asymmetry more explicitly, we additionally consider the Rachev ratio, which
compares expected upper-tail gains with expected lower-tail losses (Rachev, Menn, & Fabozzi,
2005). In this analysis, the Rachev ratio is computed from daily excess returns, defined as the
portfolio return minus the corresponding daily risk-free rate, with the upper and lower tails each
defined using a 5% tail probability. The denominator is expressed as the absolute magnitude of
the expected lower-tail return. Unlike traditional moment-based performance metrics, the Rachev
ratio directly evaluates the balance between favorable and unfavorable tail events and is therefore
particularly appropriate in the context of non-Gaussian portfolio returns.

4.2 Portfolio Risk Assessment: Value-at-Risk and Conditional Value-at-Risk
To further evaluate portfolio robustness under adverse market conditions, we examine both Value-
at-Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) across all portfolio strategies.1
For a given confidence level, Value-at-Risk measures the threshold loss exceeded with a specified
probability. Although VaR remains widely used in institutional risk management, it provides only
a quantile-based measure of downside exposure and therefore does not capture the magnitude
of losses beyond the VaR threshold. CVaR addresses this limitation by measuring the expected
loss conditional on the occurrence of extreme downside events (Rockafellar & Uryasev, 2000).
Consequently, CVaR provides a more informative characterization of downside exposure under
heavy-tailed and asymmetric return distributions. This distinction is particularly important in the
context of balanced equity–bond portfolios because simultaneous declines in both equity and fixed-
income markets may generate return asymmetries that are not adequately captured by variance-
based or quantile-only risk measures.
Throughout this analysis, VaR and CVaR are reported as positive loss measures computed from
the portfolio return series. Thus, larger values indicate greater downside risk.
