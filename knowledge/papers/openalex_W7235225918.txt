Abstract
We study a random-feature method for pricing and calibrating European options under jump-diffusion
(Merton, Kou) and stochastic-volatility (Heston, SABR) models. The network has one hidden layer with a
probit activation and fixed random inner weights, so fitting it to the pricing equation, the payoff and the
boundary conditions is a single regularised least-squares problem, with no iterative training. For Merton
and Kou the jump integral of each feature is explicit, so no quadrature is needed. One factorisation of the
least-squares matrix gives the price at every state and time, its Greeks, and the exact derivatives of the prices
with respect to the model parameters for gradient-based calibration. For the jump models, subtracting the
Black–Scholes price removes the payoff kink and reduces the pricing error by about two orders of magnitude.
The method is then about as accurate as a standard Crank–Nicolson finite-difference scheme at the same
run time, although a finite-difference scheme that also subtracts the Black–Scholes price is about two orders
of magnitude more accurate still. On the test problems of a recent physics-informed neural network study,
the method matches that network’s accuracy in seconds instead of about half an hour. The calibration
experiments show that a solver can fit the quotes and still return wrong parameters when its pricing error is
not small compared with the effect of the parameters on the prices: without the subtraction the solver misses
the jump parameters by up to 50%, and with it they are recovered to within 3%. For Heston and SABR,
used as an applicability study with boundary conditions that need no reference prices, pricing errors are
about one percent and the calibrated parameters are within about 15% of the true values.
Keywords: Option pricing; jump-diffusion; stochastic volatility; Heston; SABR; partial integro-differential
equations; random features; extreme learning machines; model calibration.

1 Introduction
1.1 The problem
The Black–Scholes model prices a European option in closed form, but it does not reproduce what is observed in
option markets. Implied volatility varies with strike and maturity, asset prices occasionally jump, and volatility
itself fluctuates over time. Two families of models address these features. Jump-diffusion models add random
jumps to the asset price; we consider the models of Merton [1976], with normally distributed log-jumps, and
of Kou [2002], with double-exponential log-jumps. Stochastic-volatility models let the variance follow its own
diffusion, correlated with the asset price; we consider the models of Heston [1993] and Hagan et al. [2002]
(SABR).
In each of these models the price of a European option is the expected discounted payoff under the risk-neutral
measure. By the Feynman–Kac theorem, the price as a function of the current state and the time to maturity
solves a linear partial differential equation (PDE). For the jump models it solves a partial integro-differential
equation (PIDE): a PDE with an additional integral term that accounts for the jumps. For the four models
above, European prices can also be computed quickly and accurately by Fourier methods [Carr and Madan,
1999, Fang and Oosterlee, 2008]. We use these models because such accurate references exist, which lets us
measure the error of the method. The PDE route is of most interest for products and settings where transform
methods are less convenient, for example continuously monitored barrier options under stochastic volatility, or
the repeated valuation of a portfolio along simulated paths.
In practice, pricing goes hand in hand with calibration. A model is first calibrated: its parameters are
chosen so that the model reproduces the quoted prices of liquid options, typically vanilla calls and puts. The
calibrated model is then used to price and hedge less liquid or non-standard derivatives on the same asset.
Calibration requires pricing the quoted options at many trial parameter vectors. Gradient-based optimisers,

1.2 Neural-network solvers and random features
Why use neural networks for pricing equations at all? For European options in one or two state variables, Fourier
methods and finite differences are fast, accurate and supported by convergence theory, and they are hard to
improve upon. The case for neural networks rests on problems that lie outside this setting. The most cited is a
large number of state variables, for which grids become infeasible; this motivated mesh-free methods such as
the deep Galerkin method [Sirignano and Spiliopoulos, 2018] and the deep BSDE method [Han et al., 2018]. A
second is the repeated evaluation of one pricing map, for example during calibration, where a network trained
once can then be evaluated almost instantly [Horvath et al., 2021, Hainaut and Casas, 2024]. But even in low
dimension there are two common situations in which classical methods are less convenient. Non-standard payoffs,
such as barrier or Asian options under jump or stochastic-volatility models, usually have no Fourier solution and
need a problem-specific grid. And exposure and margin calculations need prices and sensitivities of a trade at
many states and future dates along simulated paths, which calls for a price surface that can be evaluated and
differentiated anywhere. A neural network provides exactly such a surface, defined at every state and time and
differentiable everywhere. These advantages come at a price: training is a slow, nonconvex optimisation whose
result depends on the initialisation, and it offers no error control comparable to refining a grid. Systematic
comparisons bear this out. On standard test equations in one to three space dimensions, PINNs have not
matched finite elements in accuracy or solution time [Grossmann et al., 2024], and a review of machine-learning
solvers for partial differential equations found that most claims of outperforming classical methods rested on
comparisons with weak baselines [McGreivy and Hakim, 2024].
Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) [Raissi et al., 2019] solve a PDE by training a neural network so
that it nearly satisfies the equation, the initial condition and the boundary conditions at a large set of sample
points. They are flexible, but training is a nonconvex optimisation: it is slow, it can stop at different solutions
depending on the random initialisation, and each step needs derivatives of the network output with respect to
its inputs, usually obtained by automatic differentiation. For jump models the integral term must in addition be
approximated by a quadrature rule at every sample point [Bansal et al., 2026].
Random-feature methods remove the training loop. The inner weights of a one-hidden-layer network are
drawn at random and then held fixed, so the network output is a linear combination of fixed functions (the
features) with unknown coefficients (the output weights). The pricing equation is linear: each of its terms is a
known coefficient multiplied by the unknown price function, by one of its derivatives, or, for the jump models,
by its average over a jump. If the network output is substituted for the price, the equation at any given point
therefore becomes a linear equation in the output weights. Imposing it at many sample points, together with
the payoff and the boundary conditions, gives a system of linear equations for the output weights, which is
solved once by least squares. This idea, known as the extreme learning machine, has been applied to PDEs from
physics [Dwivedi and Srinivasan, 2020, Dong and Li, 2021, Chen et al., 2022, Wang and Dong, 2024] and to
option pricing [Gonon, 2023, Srinivasan et al., 2025].
1.3 Approach and contributions
We follow this random-feature approach, with features that apply the standard normal distribution function
Φ (the probit function) to a random linear combination of the state and the time to maturity, and we call
the method PIRanNN (physics-informed random neural network). Fitting random features to a linear pricing
equation by least squares is not new in itself: it is the physics-informed extreme learning machine of Dwivedi
and Srinivasan [2020], which has already been applied to the Black–Scholes and Heston–Hull–White equations
[Srinivasan et al., 2025] (Section 2.3). Our contributions are the following.
1. The jump term is exact. We use the standard normal distribution function Φ as activation. Averaging a
probit feature over a normal or a double-exponential jump gives an explicit formula (Lemma 4.3). Every
term of the pricing equation, including the jump integral of the Merton and Kou models, can therefore be
evaluated exactly for each feature, and the resulting PIDE solver needs no quadrature.
2. One factorisation serves many purposes. The matrix of the least-squares problem is factorised once, for the
price, and the factorisation is then reused. The Greeks are derivatives of the fitted price with respect to the
state, available at every point of the domain. Differentiating the least-squares solution with respect to the
model parameters gives the exact Jacobian of the solver’s prices, for diffusion and jump parameters alike
(Proposition 5.1); each parameter costs only a small fraction of the first solve, so calibration needs no offline
training over a parameter range. The current variance v0 of Heston and the current volatility α0 of SABR
only move the point at which the price surface is evaluated, so recalibrating them needs no new solve. Other

European payoffs under the same model can also be priced with the same factorisation (Section 4.2). These
properties follow from linearity, and the Jacobian formula is the classical one of Golub and Pereyra [1973];
what we add is their combination in one tool for pricing and calibration. Because the fitted price is a formula
in the state and the time to maturity, all of this is available at any point, with no interpolation on a grid.
3. For the jump models, a quadrature-free Black–Scholes subtraction removes the main source of error. The call
payoff has a kink at the strike, which a sum of smooth features represents poorly at short maturities. We
subtract the Black–Scholes price, which already contains the kink, and solve for the remainder, a classical
technique (Section 4.5). The jump integral of the Black–Scholes price is also explicit, so this step remains
quadrature-free. It is the main source of accuracy on the jump models: in the ablation of Section 6.1 it
reduces the error by a factor of about 100 for Merton and 70 for Kou, and by about 700 and 300 together
with extra features near the strike, for the same total number of features. The step is not specific to random
features; it improves a finite-difference scheme by a similar factor. For Heston and SABR, the price of the
model with the volatility of volatility set to zero, a classical approximation, can be subtracted in the same
way; in our tests it reduces the error for both models, by an amount that varies from case to case (Sections 6.5
and 6.6).
4. Solver accuracy limits identifiability. In the calibration of both jump models, the plain solve fits the quoted
prices but returns jump parameters that are off by up to 50%, because its pricing error is larger than the
change in the prices caused by the parameter error. If the calibration is repeated with a new random draw
of the inner weights, it again fits the quotes but returns different, equally wrong, jump parameters. With
the Black–Scholes subtraction the pricing error is 30 to 50 times smaller and the parameters are recovered
to within 3% (Section 6.3). This differs from the known intrinsic difficulty of separating jump and diffusion
parameters, where the quoted prices themselves do not determine the parameters whatever the pricer [Albani
and Zubelli, 2020]: here the parameters are identifiable, and the loss is caused by the solver. Indeed, when
the quotes are generated by the plain solver itself, with the same draw of the inner weights, the same solver
recovers the parameters exactly. The same consideration applies to any calibration that uses an approximate
pricer. The exact Jacobian, and repeating the calibration with new random draws of the inner weights, let
one check a calibration for this problem without a reference price (Section 5.6).
We test the method on European options in models with one or two state variables, where accurate references
exist. The jump models receive the full treatment above. There we compare it with a Crank–Nicolson finite-
difference scheme with an implicit jump term, at equal run time and with the same boundary data, which is
the kind of strong baseline that McGreivy and Hakim [2024] call for. With the Black–Scholes subtraction, our
method is about as accurate as this scheme: more accurate for run times of a few seconds and less accurate for
longer ones, because its error levels off near 10−6 (relative to the strike) while the finite-difference error keeps
falling. A finite-difference scheme that also subtracts the Black–Scholes price is about two orders of magnitude
more accurate than either. We therefore do not claim that the method is the most accurate pricer in one space
dimension. Its case rests on what one solve provides: a price that is a formula in the state and time, its Greeks
and its exact parameter Jacobian from one factorisation, a jump term without quadrature, and a formulation
that carries over unchanged to two state variables. Heston and SABR are an applicability study: we use only
simple boundary conditions that need no reference prices and, for the main results, the plain solve, which is
what one has when no suitable function to subtract is known. The method then prices and calibrates equations
whose coefficients depend on the state, at an accuracy of about one percent, partly limited by the payoff kink;
for Heston, the best ADI schemes remain more accurate in three of the four standard test cases.
The method is also transparent. For a given draw of the features, the output weights are the unique solution
of a regularised least-squares problem, whereas a trained PINN depends on its random initialisation, the optimiser
and the point at which training is stopped. The fitted price, its Greeks and its Jacobian are explicit formulas in
known functions, and this is what makes the checks of the fourth item possible.
The method keeps the mesh-free formulation and the smooth price surface of neural-network solvers but
removes the training step, so the applications of Section 1.2 are direct extensions. A continuously monitored
barrier adds a boundary condition at the barrier, and for the Merton model the jump term then remains explicit
(Section 7). An Asian option adds the running average as a state variable, and Lemma 4.2 applies unchanged
to an operator with more state variables. For exposure and margin calculations, one solve gives prices and
sensitivities at every state and time in the domain, so valuing a trade along simulated paths needs no further
solves. These extensions are not tested here.

2 Related work
2.1 Classical numerical methods
For European options under jump-diffusion and affine stochastic-volatility models, the most efficient methods
use the characteristic function of the log-price. The fast Fourier transform method of Carr and Madan [1999]
and the Fourier-cosine (COS) method of Fang and Oosterlee [2008] compute a European price from a short series
whose terms involve the characteristic function and the Fourier coefficients of the payoff. When no characteristic
function is available, or when the product is not European, the pricing equation is usually solved by finite
differences. For jump models the integral term is then approximated by quadrature at every time step [Cont
and Voltchkova, 2005]. For two-dimensional stochastic-volatility equations, alternating-direction implicit (ADI)
schemes are the standard choice [In ’t Hout and Foulon, 2010], and the BENCHOP-SLV project [von Sydow
et al., 2019] provides highly accurate reference prices for the Heston model.
Calibration with a finite-difference pricer is also well studied. The gradient of the prices with respect to
the model parameters can be computed by differentiating the discretised equation, which leads to adjoint
methods; Albani and Zubelli [2020] use such a gradient to calibrate jump-diffusion models with local volatility.
Differentiating the solution of a least-squares problem with respect to parameters on which its matrix depends is
also classical [Golub and Pereyra, 1973]. An exact parameter gradient of a PDE pricer is therefore not new in
itself. What is new here is obtaining it for a mesh-free, quadrature-free solver at a small fraction of the cost of
the price itself.
2.2 Physics-informed neural networks for pricing and calibration
PINNs [Raissi et al., 2019] have been extended in many directions, for example to periodic activation functions,
which have also been used to fit PDE residuals [Sitzmann et al., 2020]. In option pricing, Bansal et al. [2026]
apply PINNs to the Merton and Kou PIDEs, approximating the jump integral by the composite trapezoidal
rule, and compare with finite differences; Bansal et al. [2025] extend the approach to weather derivatives. For
stochastic volatility, Hainaut and Casas [2024] train a PINN on the Heston pricing equation over a range of
option specifications and model parameters, so that new prices require no retraining. Mrad and Iassamen
[2025] combine supervised learning on simulated payoffs with a PDE penalty for Heston with time-dependent
parameters. Bae et al. [2024] solve the Black–Scholes equation with a given local volatility surface by a PINN,
and check the result by recovering the surface from the trained network through Dupire’s formula.
Physics-informed networks have also been used for calibration, in two main ways.
• Inverse PINNs treat the unknown model parameters as additional trainable variables and fit them together
with the network weights, as in the parameter-identification examples of Raissi et al. [2019]. In finance, this
approach has been used to calibrate the market price of risk in a jump-diffusion PIDE for catastrophe bonds,
from synthetic prices [Raeisi-Makiani et al., 2026]. The network and the parameters are found by a single
nonconvex training run, which must be repeated whenever the market data change.
• Parametric PINNs and physics-informed operator networks take the model parameters as additional inputs
and are trained once, offline, over a box of parameter values. After training, a price at any parameter vector
in the box is a fast evaluation, and its derivative with respect to the parameters is available by automatic
differentiation. Hainaut and Casas [2024] take this approach for Heston, and Chakhoyan et al. [2025] use a
physics-informed DeepONet with the aim of real-time Heston calibration. The accuracy at a given parameter
vector is fixed by the offline training and cannot be improved at that vector without retraining.
The present method takes a third route. At each trial parameter vector the pricing problem is solved directly,
by a single convex least-squares problem, and its exact derivative with respect to the parameters is obtained
from the same factorisation. Table 1 summarises the differences.

but with a fixed polynomial basis: polynomial bases converge very fast for smooth targets, but their jump
integrals are not available in closed form.
3 Models and pricing equations
This section sets out the four models and the equations their prices satisfy. All four equations have the same
structure, which the method of Section 4 exploits: they are linear in the unknown price function, and their
coefficients are explicit functions of the state.

4.2 Fitting by least squares
The pricing equations of Section 3 are posed on unbounded domains. We solve them on a bounded box, for
example x ∈ [xmin, xmax] for the jump models, and impose boundary data ψ on some of its faces. The faces and

there by the equation and the payoff.

matrix, are done once.

4.5 Subtracting the Black–Scholes price
The call payoff f (x) = (ex − 1)+ has a kink at the strike x = 0. At short maturities the price is still close to
this kink, and a finite sum of smooth features cannot reproduce it well. The error of the plain solve is then
concentrated near the strike at small τ , and it stops decreasing as features are added (Section 6.2). For the jump
models we remove the difficulty by subtracting a known function that already contains the kink, and solving
for the remainder. This is the classical technique of singularity subtraction [Fix et al., 1973]; in option pricing
the kink is more often handled by smoothing the payoff or by special start-up steps of a time-stepping scheme
[Pooley et al., 2003].

J h. Writing V = h + w and using M[V ] = 0 and the linearity of M, the remainder w solves
M[w] = −M[h], w(·, 0) = f − h(·, 0), w = ψ − h on the boundary. (4.14)

than V .

5 Sensitivities and calibration
The fitted price is an explicit function of the state variables and, through the output weights, of the model
parameters. Differentiating it with respect to the state gives the Greeks (Section 5.1). Differentiating it with
respect to the model parameters gives the Jacobian needed for calibration (Sections 5.2 to 5.4). Section 5.5
describes the calibration procedure, and Section 5.6 discusses which parameters a calibration can recover.

5.2 Parameter sensitivities
Calibration needs the derivative of the fitted price with respect to each model parameter θp. Unlike the state, a
model parameter does not enter the features: it enters the least-squares problem, because it changes the pricing
operator and hence the matrix A and, when the Black–Scholes price is subtracted, the vector b. The output
weights y∗ therefore depend on θp, and their derivative is obtained by differentiating the normal equations.

Formula (5.1) is the classical derivative of a regularised least-squares solution with respect to parameters on

• The matrix is already factorised. The matrix H in (5.1) is the same matrix whose Cholesky factorisation

which would cost O(m3). Forming the right-hand side costs O(N m) operations.

(Section 4.2), so the initial and boundary rows of A and b are the same for every θ. The rows of Ap for
the interior points are the parameter derivatives of the explicit expressions of Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3, and are
