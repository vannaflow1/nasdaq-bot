Abstract 
Financial markets are among the most complex systems to model. The theoretical 
framework developed to understand how prices form, how risk is priced, and how capital is 
allocated represents decades of formal work, and it rests on assumptions about how prices evolve, 
distributional forms, and market structure that are chosen not because they are always accurate but 
because they make the problem tractable. When machine learning arrived as a practical 
computational tool, the natural response was to treat it as an upgrade: free of those assumptions, 
trained directly on th e data, it was expected to outperform. The evidence on that has been 
instructive. Models that predict well in controlled settings (see Gu, Kelly and Xiu, 2020) routinely 
fail in live markets (see Bartram et al., 2021), and the gap between the two has been attributed to 
overfitting, to regime change, to data -snooping - but rarely to the framing itself. Much of this 
literature evaluates ML on whether it produces better forecasts than existing models, treating 
predictive accuracy as the measure of its contribution. 
This thesis asks a different question. If the value of ML in financial economics lies not in 
outperforming traditional models but in addressing limitations that those models cannot resolve by 
design, then the architecture of the ML framework matters as muc h as the data it learns from. 
Rather than applying general -purpose models to financial data, this thesis adopts the Adaptive 
Markets Hypothesis (AMH) as a theoretical account of how markets behave and designs Machine 
learning (ML) frameworks whose structur e encodes its core properties directly. Theory provides 
the economic logic: what relationships to look for, what quantities matter, what a result means. 
ML provides the estimation flexibility and the capacity to learn those relationships from data 
without constraints that the data does not satisfy. Used together, in either direction, they can reach 
parts of the problem that neither can reach alone. This thesis examines that complementarity across 
three studies adopting the AMH as the theoretical account of market behavior most consistent with 
the methods it employs. 
In the first study, I present a novel framework to remove market microstructure noise that 
is caused by the trading process itself and recover the underlying signal from high-frequency data. 
My framework integrates classical state-space filtering algorithms with the adaptability of machine 
learning networks, allowing the filter to learn both the dynamics of price evolution and the 
structure of the noise from the data rather than assume them. Using NASDAQ 100 stocks sampled 
at one-minute frequency, I show that the recovery of the signal is not uniform. For stocks with the 
highest noise levels, filtering produces statistically significant improvements in both prediction 
accuracy and classification performance. For stocks with the lowest noise levels, it has negligible 
or negative effects, removing informative variation rather than distortion. 
The second study is a methodological contribution to the literature. I propose a multi-agent 
reinforcement learning algorithm (MARL), with an architecture modeled after the principles of the 
AMH, for portfolio management. I derive a novel reward function by extending the Differential 
Sharpe Ratio (DSR) to the multi-agent setting, providing each agent with a real-time signal of its 
marginal contribution to the portfolio’s risk -adjusted return and addressing the credit assignment 
problem, which has limited prior multi-agent applications in finance. Evaluated across five out-of-
sample periods from 2016 to 2021, covering distinct market conditions, the framework 
consistently either outperforms the S&P 500 in risk -adjusted terms or preserves capita l more 
effectively when the benchmark declines. 
The third study extends this framework to the market proxy problem: the true market 
portfolio required by equilibrium models is unobservable, and every empirical test relies on a 
proxy whose composition is fixed by construction. A distinguishing property o f the MARL 
framework is that the policy each agent learns captures general market relationships through the 
shared feature structure, making the trained architecture transferable across problems. I repurpose 
it for market proxy construction by recalibrating portfolio inclusion thresholds and restructuring 
the data pipeline to support a twelve-month rolling inference window. 
The resulting proxy is, to my knowledge, the first to ground index construction in the 
principles of the AMH. I evaluate its information content against the S&P 500 and the CRSP value-
weighted index using the structural variance decomposition of Brogaard et al. (2022). The adaptive 
proxy captures a consistently higher share of market-wide variance across the sample period, with 
the gain in systematic information matched by a reduction in variance attributed to firm -specific 
sources. The proxy reduces average annualized pricing errors substantially under both the Capital 
Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) and the Fama -French 5-Factor model, and the results are nearly 
identical across two alternative weighting schemes, confirming that the advantage derives from 
asset selection rather than weight construction. The results provide empirical support for the view 
that a proxy grounded in how markets actually adapt more accurately captures the market factor 
than the alternatives. 

Keywords: machine learning, market microstructure noise, particle filter, long short-term memory, 
multi-agent reinforcement learning, adaptive markets hypothesis, portfolio optimization, market 
proxy problem, CAPM, variance decomposition, Differential Sharpe Ratio, price efficiency, asset 
pricing, systematic risk. 

1. Introduction 
1.1. A summary of the thesis 
Machine learning methods have demonstrated that they can capture non-linear structure in 
financial data ever since neural networks were established as universal function approximators 
(Hornik, Stinchcombe and White, 1989). These methods do not require the functional form of the 
relationship between inputs and outputs to be specified in advance; they learn it directly from the 
data. This does not mean they are assumption -free; architectural choices, regularization, and the 
quality of the training data all shape what the model can learn. But they are free of the specific 
structural assumptions, such as linearity, stationarity, and known distributional forms, that 
traditional models in financial economics depend on. Much of wh at is now known about asset 
pricing, portfolio construction, and market efficiency was developed within these frameworks, 
which, despite their limitations, remain central to both academic research and industry practice. 
Over the past three decades, the evolution of technology in financial markets has made the 
environment that these models attempt to explain considerably more complex. Trading that once 
occurred over minutes and hours now executes in milliseconds, and high -frequency activity 
accounts for over half of U.S. equity trading volume (Zaharudin, Young and Hsu, 2022). The data 
generated offer a far richer picture of how prices form and how participants interact, but they also 
carry distortions introduced by the mechanics of trading itself: bid-ask bounce, price discreteness, 
and order flow imbalances that are now embe dded in the prices every subsequent model and 
investment decision relies on (Brogaard, Hendershott and Riordan, 2014) . At the same time, the 
evidence that return dynamics, risk compensation, and informational efficiency are not fixed but 
shift over time has accumulated, challenging the stationarity assumptions on which much of the 
classical toolkit depends. 
The models that the field relies on were built on the understanding of markets that prevailed 
when they were developed. They remain the right starting point, but the gap between what they 
assume and what the data now contains has widened. ML, with the flex ibility to learn from the 
data without imposing the assumptions that create this gap, offers a way to bridge it. This thesis 
investigates three settings in which the assumptions of the standard models come into direct 
contact with the properties of modern financial data, and demonstrates that adaptive, data -driven 
methods can reconcile the two by improving the quality of the data these models rely on, the 
flexibility of the allocation decisions they produce, and the accuracy of the benchmarks against 
which they are tested. 
Despite the extensive research on the effectiveness of ML in financial economics, there has 
been a disconnect between the academic findings and the outcomes observed in practice (see 
Bartram et al., 2021). Much of the existing literature frames ML as a prediction problem (see, as 
examples, Henrique, Sobreiro and Kimura, 2019; Hambly, Xu and Yang, 2023) and on that 
criterion, the results have been mixed. 
Models that perform well in controlled settings often fail to deliver in live markets (Bailey 
et al. , 2016) , where the patterns identified in training do not persist (Arnott, Harvey and 
Markowitz, 2019) . But the framing itself may be the problem. ML models are, at their core, 
prediction models. They learn patterns in the data to estimate the most likely outcome. In contrast, 
financial econometrics is the study of the economic relationships embedded in financial data: how 
risk is compensated, how information is incorporated into prices, and how market participants 
interact. It uses models not only to forecast but to understand. Perhaps judging ML by whether it 
produces better forecasts asks the wrong question, and its value in financial economics lies not in 
outperforming traditional models with higher returns, but in addressing limitations that those 
models cannot resolve by design. Motivated by this, I conduct three studies examining settings 
where ML methods and financial economic theory inform and benefit one another. Specifically, I 
investigate the filtering of market microstructure noise from high-frequency price data to produce 
higher-quality features, the development of a reinforcement learning (RL) framework for portfolio 
management grounded in the AMH, and whether a portfolio constructed by the aggregated 
conviction of reinforcement learning agents can serve as a more informative proxy for the market 
portfolio. I directly contribute to the literature through the investigations described below. The 
contributions and research questions of the thesis are briefly discussed in the following paragraphs. 
In Chapter 3, I propose a noise filtering model that combines the non -linear filtering 
capabilities of sequential Monte Carlo methods with the adaptive learning of a Long Short -Term 
Memory (LSTM) network to reduce market microstructure noise in stock price data. Each particle 
in the filter represents a candidate trajectory of the true underlying price, with the LSTM providing 
learned adjustments based on historical patterns. The chapter examines whether this integration 
can separate price signals from noise without relying on the predetermined assumptions about 
price evolution and noise distribution that characterize traditional filters. 
Applied to NASDAQ 100 stock data, the model reveals that the effectiveness of noise 
filtering varies across instruments and depends on the level of microstructure noise in the 
underlying price series. For stocks with the highest noise levels, filtering del ivers statistically 
significant improvements in both prediction accuracy and classification performance. However, 
for stocks with low noise levels, filtering produces negligible or even negative effects, removing 
informative price variation rather than noise. 
This chapter contributes to the literature in two ways. First, existing methods for filtering 
microstructure noise rely on linear filters that require the researcher to specify how efficient prices 
evolve and how noise is distributed; if either is wrong, the filter misattributes signal and noise. The 
proposed model removes this dependence by learning the dynamics of efficient prices directly 
from historical patterns through an LSTM network. Second, t o the best of my knowledge, this 
study is among the first to provide evidence that noise filtration is not universally beneficial for all 
price series. Specifically, the improvement from filtering depends on the characteristics of the price 
series, and a p reliminary assessment of noise intensity is necessary before applying denoising 
techniques. 
In Chapter 4, I propose a MARL framework for portfolio optimization motivated by the 
AMH (A. Lo, 2004a). If markets are shaped by the competition and adaptation of heterogeneous 
participants, then a framework that explicitly models these dynamics should be better suited to 
allocating capital. I operationalize this idea by assigning each financial asset in t he investment 
universe to an individual agent that learns, through continuous interaction with market data, 
whether and how much of that asset belongs in the portfolio. The result is a system in which 
thousands of agents simultaneously compete for capital allocation, each developing expertise in a 
single asset. This structure functions as a large -scale simulation of the adaptive, competitive 
dynamics described by the AMH. 
I develop a novel reward function that extends the DSR (Moody and Saffell, 1998) to the 
multi-agent setting. This allows the algorithm to provide every agent with a real -time signal that 
captures not only its contribution to returns but also its impact on portfolio risk. I evaluate the 
framework using CRSP U.S. equity data across five out-of-sample periods from 2016 to 2021, 
each corresponding to different market conditions. Across all periods, the framework either 
outperforms the S&P 500 benchmark on a risk-adjusted basis or preserves capital more effectively 
when the benchmark declines. It does so without any explicit risk-management mechanisms, such 
as stop-losses, suggesting that risk control emerges from the learning process itself. 
A further finding is that across multiple training iterations, the leading models 
independently converge on significant allocations to the same high -quality assets, purely from 
price and market signals, without any knowledge of the underlying firms’ identities. 
This chapter contributes to the literature in two ways. First, existing MARL approaches to 
portfolio optimization assign agents to functional roles within the investment process, such as 
return maximization and risk control (Li, Tam and Yeung, 2024), with the number of agents fixed 
by the number of roles rather than the number of assets. The proposed framework instead assigns 
each agent to a single asset and trains all agents under a shared policy, allowing the system to scale 
to the full universe of U.S. equities. Second, the novel reward function derived by extending DSR 
to the multi-agent setting provides each agent with a precise measure of its marginal contribution 
to the portfolio’s risk-adjusted return, thereby addressing the credit assignment problem that has 
limited prior MARL applications in finance. Agents whose assets contribute positively to risk -
adjusted performance gain larger allocations, while those that do not are gradually removed. 
Chapter 5 builds upon the framework developed in Chapter 4 and asks whether a portfolio 
constructed by adaptive learning agents can serve as a better proxy for the unobservable market 
portfolio than static capitalization -weighted indices. I address this by treating the aggregated 
conviction of the MARL agents not as a trading strategy designed to generate alpha, but as a 
candidate definition of the market factor itself. 
I evaluate this adaptive proxy using two approaches. First, employing the structural 
variance decomposition framework of Brogaard et al. (2022), I find that the RL-generated portfolio 
captures a consistently higher share of market -wide information than the S&P 500 across the 
sample period. The gain in market -wide information is mirrored by a reduction in variance 
attributed to firm -specific components, implying that the static index misclassifies a portion of 
systematic co-movement as idiosyncratic noise. Second, I test the pr icing efficiency of the proxy 
using the CAPM and the Fama -French 5-Factor model and find that the RL proxy substantially 
reduces average annualized pricing errors under both specifications relative to the S&P 500. I 
further compare two weight-compilation methods and find that the results are nearly identical. This 
contrasts with Plyakha, Uppal and Vilkov (2021) who show that the choice between equal and 
value weighting can substantially alter asset pricing test conclusions when composition is held 
fixed, and suggests that it is the adaptive composition of the proxy rather than how they are 
weighted that drives the improvement. 
This study differs from the existing RL portfolio literature in both its design and its 
evaluation. The framework is modeled after the principles of the AMH, with agents that specialize, 
compete, and adapt to changing conditions, and is evaluated not on it s ability to generate returns 
but on its ability to capture systematic information and reduce pricing errors. The results show that 
the adaptive proxy more accurately captures the market factor than the S&P 500, providing 
empirical support for the view tha t the composition of systematic risk shifts over time and that a 
valid proxy must be able to adapt alongside it. 

2. Background 
In this section, I discuss key concepts that should be understood in advance of reading the 
studies presented in this thesis. 
2.1. Market efficiency and equilibrium pricing 
According to Fama’s (1970) Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH), prices in a competitive 
market fully reflect all available information at all times. Under this condition, no investor can 
systematically earn returns above those justified by the risks accepted, because any information 
capable of generating such returns would already be embedded in the price. In practice, however, 
the evidence presents a more complicated picture. Return predictability varies with economic 
conditions, risk premia are not stable over time, and the same str ategy can generate profits in one 
period and fail in another. These observations are difficult to reconcile with a theory built on the 
premise that markets settle into and maintain a stable, informationally efficient equilibrium. 
Understanding why requires examining the theoretical structure of the EMH and the pricing 
framework it depends on, the limitations the empirical record exposes, and the alternative 
theoretical account those limitations motivate. 
The informational foundation of the EMH rests on the premise that if a market is populated 
by rational participants who act on all available information, any forecastable component of future 
prices will already be reflected in today’s price (Samuelson, 196 5). Price changes are therefore 
random, not despite rational behavior but because of it. Fama (1970) built on this foundation, 
translating the theoretical insight into a testable hypothesis and distinguishing three forms of 
market efficiency according to the scope of the information set prices are assumed to reflect. Weak-
form efficiency holds that curre nt prices incorporate all historical price and return data, so no 
trading strategy based on past prices can generate systematic excess returns. Under semi-strong 
efficiency, prices also incorporate all publicly available information, so that strategies based on 
financial statements, earnings announcements, or other public disclosures offer no systematic 
advantage over the market. Strong-form efficiency makes the most demanding claim: that prices 
reflect private information as well, leaving even those with access to non -public information 
unable to earn abnormal returns by trading on it. This form serves primarily as a theoretical limit 
case; the evidence shows that corporate insiders do earn abnor mal returns ahead of material 
announcements, confirming that prices do not fully incorporate non -public information (Seyhun, 
1986). 
Testing any of these forms requires knowing what return a given asset should earn under 
the assumption of efficiency. Since expected returns vary across assets according to the risks they 
carry, any test of whether prices correctly reflect information must also specify how those risks 
are priced. That specification comes from a model of market equilibrium: a theoretical account of 
how rational investors, interacting in competitive markets, collectively determine the 
compensation required for bearing each type of risk. Without such a model, there is no benchmark 
against which to measure whether an observed return is normal or anomalous. 
The CAPM (Sharpe, 1964; Lintner, 1965; Mossin, 1966) is the first formal model of market 
equilibrium. It builds on Markowitz’s (1952) theory that, for a diversified investor, the relevant 
measure of an asset’s risk is not its own variance but its covariance with the returns of the broader 
portfolio. Tobin (1958) extended this, showing that in the presence of a risk-free asset, all investors 
hold the same risky portfolio regardless of their risk tolerance, differing only in how much they 
allocate to it. The CAPM establishes that if all investors share the same inf ormation and face the 
same investment opportunities, the risky portfolio they all hold must equal the value -weighted 
market portfolio of all traded risky assets. Each asset's expected return is then determined solely 
by its beta, the ratio of its covariance with the market portfolio to the market's variance, since a 
well-diversified investor bears no idiosyncratic risk and therefore demands no compensation for 
it. 
In the original form, CAPM assumes frictionless markets and a single-period investment 
horizon. Neither holds in practice, and subsequent theoretical work extended the framework to 
accommodate both. Merton’s (1975) Intertemporal CAPM (ICAPM) extended the single-period 
framework to a continuous-time setting and showed that assets are priced not only for market risk 
but also for their exposure to shifts in state variables , such as interest rates or volatility, that alter 
future investment conditions. The consumption CAPM (Lucas Jr, 1978; Breeden, 1979) tied 
expected returns to how an asset's payoffs co -move with investors' consumption, grounding the 
pricing kernel directly in marginal utility rather than in a market portfolio. These extensions share 
the same logical architecture as the original CAPM: expected returns are linear functions of 
exposure to a small set of priced risk factors derived from equilibrium conditions under rational 
expectations. 
A further limitation is that the original model and the extensions assume that trading itself 
is costless and markets are frictionless. When an asset is costly to trade, investors demand 
compensation for bearing those costs, and illiquidity therefore becomes a priced characteristic in 
its own right . Amihud and Mendelson (1986) established this empirically, showing that stocks 
with wider bid-ask spreads earn higher average returns , and Amihud (2002) showed that periods 
of high aggregate market illiquidity are followed by higher returns, consistent with investors 
demanding greater compensation when trading becomes more costly. Acharya and Pedersen 
(2005) formalized these effects in a liquidity -adjusted CAPM, showing that a security’s required 
return depends not only on its expected level of illiquidity but on how that security’s returns and 
liquidity co-move with market returns and market liquidity. The premium for liquidity risk is not 
fixed but varies with the state of the market, highest when assets become difficult to trade, precisely 
as broader market conditions deteriorate. 
Despite these extensions, each broadening the equilibrium framework to account for more 
of the variation in expected returns across assets , the evidence against the CAPM’s pricing 
predictions, labeled anomalies, surfaced early. Black, Jensen and Scholes (1972) showed that the 
observed relationship between beta and average returns is weaker than the theory implies, with 
low-beta portfolios earning more and high -beta portfolios earning less than their systematic risk 
exposure would warrant . Ball (1978) identified why the problem runs deeper than beta. If the 
model is incomplete, the stock price itself absorbs whatever expected return variation beta misses, 
so any variable constructed from the price will pick up that variation mechanically. Firm 
characteristics such as size, earnings -to-price ratios, and leverage were found to predict returns 
that beta cannot account for (Banz, 1981; Basu, 1983; Bhandari, 1988) . Fama and French (1992) 
brought these findings together in a single cross-sectional test and found that once size and book-
to-market are included, beta loses almost all its explanatory power for average returns. Each 
violation might be attributed to model misspecification in isolation, but collectively, the evidence 
in Fama (1992) was clear: beta alone does not explain the cross-section of expected returns. 
In response Fama and French (1993) proposed the three -factor model to address these 
anomalies, supplementing the market factor with a size factor (SMB) and a value factor (HML) 
designed to capture the cross-sectional patterns beta misses. They framed the model as an empirical 
implementation of Merton’s (1973) ICAPM, in which the additional factors proxy for state 
variables that carry risk premia beyond what the market factor captures. Rather than deriving 
pricing predictions from equilibrium conditions, it identifies patterns in average returns and 
constructs portfolios to replicate them. Subsequent extensions added momentum (Carhart, 1997), 
profitability, and investment (Fama and French, 2015). 
The information assumption is deeply embedded in asset pricing models and implies that 
no investor holds an advantage over any other, and that prices therefore already reflect all available 
information, which is a strong form of market efficiency. Tests of the CAPM or the extensions 
consequently test that informational condition alongside the model’s own pricing predictions. 
Fama (1970, 1991) formalized this dependency as the joint hypothesis problem : because 
expected returns are defined by an equilibrium model, evidence of abnormal returns may indicate 
either that prices are inefficient or that the model of normal returns is wrong, and no test can 
separate the two. Roll (1977) showed that for the CAPM, specifically, the problem runs deeper. 
His critique rests on two arguments. The first is that the linear beta -return relationship is a 
mathematical property of any mean-variance efficient portfolio, making a CAPM test merely a test 
of that portfolio’s efficiency. The second is that the theoretical market portfolio encompasses all 
investable and non -investable wealth, from publicly traded securities to real estate, private 
businesses, and human capital, making it impossible to observe or construct in practice . Any 
empirical test, therefore, jointly tests the CAPM’s pricing logic, the proxy standing in for the 
market portfolio, and the assumption that prices reflect available information. Fama and French 
(2004) pointed out that this is not only an academic concern: the same proxies used in empirical 
tests are used in cost -of-capital estimation, so the problem carries directly into corporate finance 
practice. 

2.2. The effects of misspecifying the market portfolio 
The sensitivity of empirical results to proxy choice has been receiving a lot of attention 
over the years. Stambaugh (1982) expanded the proxy to incorporate fixed income, real estate, and 
consumer durables alongside equities, and found that CAPM inferences were virtually identical to 
those from a stocks-only proxy, largely because stock returns dominate the volatility of the broader 
index, though the results were sensitive to the choice of test assets. 
Subsequent work provided contrasting results. Kandel and Stambaugh (1987) 
demonstrated that even when a proxy correlates strongly with the true tangent portfolio, test 
outcomes remain unreliable; using the NYSE -AMEX value-weighted index, they found that a 
proxy correlated as high as 0.76 with the tangent portfolio could produce the wrong conclusion 
about the CAPM's validity . Green (1986) showed that two benchmarks nearly identical in their 
risk-return position can produce opposite conclusions about which securities are mispriced - a 
stock that appears to offer excess returns under one proxy can appear to underperform under the 
other. Roll and Ross (1994) demonstrated that even small departures from efficiency in the proxy 
can eliminate the expected positive beta -return relationship entirely. Shanken (1987) proposed a 
test that accounts for how closely the proxy may track the unobservable market portfolio. Applying 
this to an equal-weighted equity index combined with a government bond index, he concluded that 
if the CAPM is correct, the proxy captures no more than two -thirds of the true market return's 
variation, and perhaps as little as half. 
The inclusion of bonds alongside equities made no material difference to this conclusion . 
Hwang and Satchell (2002) investigated the misspecification in beta that arises from using a proxy, 
finding that standard value -weighted and equally weighted indices both sit well away from the 
efficient portfolio and produce substantially different betas from those implied by the Markowitz 
solution, but are roughly interchangeable with each other, suggesting that the distance from 
efficiency is a property of the equity universe itself rather than the weighting scheme. 
To address the problem, there have been various attempts to expand the asset universe of 
the market portfolio rather than changing the weighting scheme. Ibbotson and Siegel (1983) were 
among the first to estimate a global market wealth portfolio spanning equities, bonds, real estate, 
and metals across countries. Doeswijk, Lam and Swinkels (2014) constructed a comprehensive 
version, covering eight asset classes for 1959 -2012 and estimating total invested market 
capitalization at approximately $90.6 trillion, with equities representing only 36.3%. Their follow-
up study (Doeswijk, Lam and Swinkels, 2020) provided return data for this portfolio, reporting a 
compounded real return of 4.45% with 11.2% standard deviation. Gadzinski, Schuller and 
Vacchino (2018) extended the asset universe to include non -financial assets, estimating the total 
global capital stock at approximately $684 trillion, with non-financial assets comprising over forty 
percent. 
Estimating the effects of excluding assets from the market portfolio Ferguson and Shockley 
(2003) showed that omitting corporate debt from the market portfolio systematically understates 
betas for small and highly leveraged firms, because the covariance of equity returns with the 
missing debt component is not captured. This means the size and value patterns that motivated the 
Fama-French factors may partly reflect the equity -only proxy’s omission of debt rather than the 
presence of independent risk factors. Jagannathan and Wang (1996) addressed a different 
omission: human capital. They allowed betas and the risk premium to vary over time and used 
labor income growth as a proxy for human capital returns when building a broader measure of 
overall wealth. This conditional, expanded model explained more than half of the variation in 
average returns across different individuals, while the static CAPM with an equity -only proxy 
showed almost no explanatory power. The implication is that the CAPM's weak empirical 
performance is more due to the inadequate proxy than to a fundamental flaw in the pricing model 
itself. Kamara and Young (2018) expanded the proxy to include Treasury securities, real estate, 
corporate debt, and international equities, finding that both beta estimates and the implied market 
risk premium shifted materially, leading them to argue that proxy selection matters as much as 
model specification when estimating the cost of equity. 
Beyond the exclusion of assets, the argument of whether the weighting scheme itself 
introduces systematic distortion, even when holding the asset universe fixed, has raised concerns. 
Siegel (2006) argued through the Noisy Market Hypothesis that if prices contain a noise 
component, capitalization weighting mechanically overweigh s overpriced securities and 
underweights underpriced ones, creating a systematic drag on index returns. Hsu (2004) 
formalized this, showing the drag is approximately equal to the variance of pricing noise across 
stocks. Treynor (2005) demonstrated that what he called “market-valuation-indifferent” indexing 
tends to outperform because mispricings are roughly symmetrical and cancel out when weights are 
decoupled from prices. The implication for the proxy problem is that even if the asset universe 
were correct, a capitalization-weighted index would still be a biased representation of the efficient 
frontier whenever prices deviate from fundamental value. Arnott, Hsu and Moore (2005) proposed 
Fundamental Indexation as a concrete alternative - weighting by book value, revenue, earnings, 
and dividends - and documented outperformance of approximately 200 basis points annually over 
1962-2004. Whether this constitutes a better proxy or a repackaged value tilt remains debated. Jun 
and Malkiel (2008) attributed the outperformance to Fama-French factor exposures. But the debate 
itself illustrates the difficulty; if a simple change in the weighting scheme produces loadings on 
factors that generate excess returns, it is unclear whether the problem lies with the alternative index 
or with the capitalization-weighted benchmark that fails to price those factors. 
The proxies used in practice differ from one another in ways that matter for asset pricing 
conclusions. The CRSP value -weighted index covers all listed U.S. equities; the Fama -French 
market factor applies a narrower filter, keeping only ordinary common equity and excluding 
REITs, closed-end funds, and ADRs (Fama and French, 1993); the S&P 500 is narrower still and 
uses committee discretion for constituent selection. Each of these is treated as “the market” in 
different studies, yet they do not represent the same portfolio. Canina et al. (1998) demonstrated 
that even the choice between daily and monthly compounding of the CRSP equal-weighted index 
produces a bias of approximately 0.43% per month - a difference large enough to alter conclusions 
about long-run excess returns. Plyakha, Uppal and Vilkov (2021) showed that switching between 
equal and value weighting alters the outcome of GRS efficiency tests, stochastic discount factor 
spanning tests, and the estimated relationship between firm characteristics and returns. This 
indicates that asset pricing conclu sions are partly sensitive to how the benchmark is weighted. 
Cremers, Petajisto and Zitzewitz (2012) found that standard factor models assign economically 
significant non -zero alphas to passive benchmark indices themselves - the Russell 2000, for 
instance, carries a persistent negative alpha, which implies that the models fail to price correctly 
the very indices they use as factors. 
Whether existing proxies are genuinely inefficient or merely appear so due to estimation 
error is unresolved. Levy and Roll (2010) showed that only small perturbations within estimation-
error bounds are needed to render common proxies mean-variance efficient, suggesting that CAPM 
rejections may reflect sampling noise rather than true inefficiency. Brière et al. (2013) reached the 
opposite conclusion using a test designed for the case where all assets are risky, finding that the 
market portfolio is not efficient. Lewellen, Nagel and Shanken (2010) raised a separate concern: 
the strong factor structure of size and book-to-market sorted portfolios makes it easy for virtually 
any factor to appear significant in cross-sectional tests, so apparent support for a given proxy may 
say more about the test po rtfolios than about the proxy itself. Low and Nayak (2009) used 
simulations to argue that Fama -MacBeth test conclusions are largely unaffected even when the 
proxy has low correlation with the true market, suggesting Roll’s critique may be forgiving in 
practice, though this view has not been widely accepted, and their simulations assume conditions 
that may not hold in real markets. 
More recent work has sought to address the proxy problem with modern tools rather than 
simply broadening the index or changing the weights. Giglio and Xiu (2021) developed a three-
pass estimator that recovers risk premia even when priced factors are omitted from the model, 
which is analogous to the situation created by an imperfect market proxy - if the proxy does not 
span the true market factor, it is effectively an omitted-factor problem. Prono (2015) generalized 
Shanken’s (1987) framework by recognizing that the error from using a proxy is not independent 
of the true market return - it is endogenous - and developed a measure of how much this distorts 
asset pricing tests, finding that all models he tested performed better once thi s distortion was 
accounted for. Simmet and Pohlmeier (2020) modeled the two sources of proxy misspecification, 
inaccurate weights and omission of asset classes, directly, and proposed a minimum distance 
estimator that exploits the common measurement error across test assets to identify CAPM 
parameters even with an imperfect proxy. Desban et al. (2023) took a computational approach,
