Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) is a promising way to improve the clinical reason-
ing abilities of large language models (LLMs), but current pipelines often rely
on expert-labeled questions or costly synthetic-data generation. Meanwhile, the
medical literature offers a wealth of high-quality unlabeled documents, including
clinical case reports that trace the full clinical course from initial presentation to
final treatment decisions. We study whether document-grounded self-play can
convert these reports into effective RL training signals. We identify two challenges
that limit existing self-play methods in this setting:knowledge-point fixation, where
the proposer repeatedly asks about a narrow set of salient clues in a source re-
port, anddifficulty-quality mismatch, where appropriately difficult questions may
still be poorly formed or shallow, limiting their value for reasoner training. To
address these challenges, we propose CasePlay, a self-play framework with two
key components: (a) aKnowledge-Conditioned Proposer, which anchors ques-
tion generation to case-specific knowledge points so that questions draw on a
broader range of evidence from the source report; and (b) aRubric-Judge Reasoner,
which augments answer-accuracy feedback with rubric-based quality feedback on
clue integration, reasoning depth, and option quality, guiding the proposer toward
reasoning-intensive questions that better support reasoner training. Extensive ex-
periments across seven medically related benchmarks demonstrate that CasePlay
outperforms existing self-play baselines and improves the SFT warm-start model by
3.1 points on average, highlighted by a 6.3-point gain on LiveClin. This approach
offers an effective path toward continually improving medical reasoning ability
through self-play RL.
1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have shown growing promise in medical reasoning, including
differential diagnosis [1–5], clinical decision support [6–9], and health question answering [10–12].
Many recent advances in medical LLMs have followed the common post-training paradigm of
supervised fine-tuning (SFT) followed by reinforcement learning (RL), which has proven effective
for improving reasoning ability. However, scaling this recipe remains costly as it depends on expert
annotations and synthetic data pipelines, a bottleneck that becomes increasingly severe as models
improve. In contrast, medicine already offers a continually growing collection of unlabeled documents
(e.g., case reports) that contain rich real-world medical knowledge. This raises a fundamental question:
can we continually improve medical reasoning by learning directly from unlabeled clinical corpora?

A promising direction is self-play RL over text corpora [ 13–21]. In this paradigm, aProposer
generates questions from either its internal knowledge or external documents, and aReasonerlearns
to solve them. The reasoner’s performance provides feedback for updating the proposer, while the
generated questions provide training data for improving the reasoner, forming an automated loop
for deriving learning signals from unlabeled corpora. Although this paradigm has shown promising
results in domains such as mathematics [22, 23] and code [24], directly applying it to the medical
domain raises two challenges: (a)knowledge-point fixation: medical case reports are information-
dense and often contain multiple interdependent clinical clues, yet a naive proposer tends to anchor
on the most salient evidence and repeatedly generate questions from the same narrow perspective; and
(b)difficulty-quality mismatch: existing self-play methods often use the reasoner’s answer accuracy as
the main feedback signal, treating intermediate-difficulty questions as useful training data. However,
such questions may still be poorly constructed or require only shallow reasoning, making them poor
candidates for improving medical reasoning through RL.
To address these challenges, we proposeCasePlay, a self-play learning framework consisting of two
key components: (a) aKnowledge-Conditioned Proposer, which conditions question generation
on case-specific knowledge points to broaden coverage of each source report and reduce redundant
questions around the same salient clue; and (b) aRubric-Judge Reasoner, which supplements answer-
accuracy feedback with rubric-based quality feedback along three dimensions: clue integration,
reasoning depth, and option quality. This quality-aware feedback guides the proposer toward higher-
quality questions that provide more useful training examples for the reasoner. Experiments on seven
medically related benchmarks show that CasePlay improves the warm-start model by3.1 pointson
average and outperforms existing self-play baselines, with the largest gain on LiveClin [25] (+6.3
points). Together, these results position CasePlay as a new way to leverage unlabeled case reports for
improving medical reasoning through self-play RL. In summary, our contributions are threefold:
• We study self-play RL over clinical case reports for improving medical reasoning, and
empirically identify two characteristic failure modes of existing methods:knowledge-point
fixationanddifficulty-quality mismatch.
• We propose CasePlay, a medical self-play framework where a Knowledge-Conditioned
Proposer uses case-specific knowledge points to encourage broader document coverage,
while a Rubric-Judge Reasoner leverages rubric-based feedback as an additional quality

signal for proposer optimization, helping generate less redundant and higher-quality training
questions for improving medical reasoning.
• We validate CasePlay through extensive experiments on 7 medically related benchmarks,
where it improves the warm-start model by3.1 pointson average, outperforms medical
reasoning models and self-play baselines, and remains effective across ablations, hyperpa-
rameter settings, and model scales.

Knowledge-Point Fixation.Clinical case reports are information-dense, often containing multiple
clinical clues about symptoms, treatments, outcomes, and disease mechanisms. Ideally, document-
grounded self-play should turn these diverse clues into diverse training questions. However, when the
proposer is conditioned only on the full document, it tends to anchor on the most salient evidence
and repeatedly generate questions from the same narrow subset of the report. Figure 2(a) shows
that SPICE’s source-document coverage quickly saturates at 0.33, compared with 0.42 for CasePlay,
indicating that later rollouts mostly reuse already-covered content rather than explore new case
evidence. The resulting questions are also less diverse: in Figure 2(b), SPICE forms a tighter cluster
and occupies a smaller region than CasePlay in the projection space. We refer to this failure mode
asknowledge-point fixation, where an information-rich case report is reduced to redundant training
3
questions from a narrow subset of clinical evidence. This observation indicates that the proposer
needs an explicit mechanism to control which part of the case report each rollout should explore.

reasoner accuracy of 0.5 and evaluate their quality using the rubrics introduced in Section 3.2. As
shown in Figure 2(c), even within this intermediate-difficulty set, SPICE produces many low-quality

63.5 and reduces the average response length. This highlights the need for quality-aware feedback
that goes beyond difficulty and favors well-formed, reasoning-intensive questions.
3 Proposed Solution: CasePlay
In this paper, we proposeCasePlay, a self-play framework for improving medical reasoning from case
reports. The central idea of CasePlay is to turn two implicit choices in document-grounded self-play
into explicit controls:which clinical focusin a case report the proposer should explore, andhowto
evaluate the quality of generated questions for training. CasePlay implements these controls with a
Knowledge-Conditioned Proposer, which guides question generation with case-specific knowledge
points, and aRubric-Judge Reasoner, which provides quality-aware feedback for proposer learning.
3.1 Knowledge-Conditioned Proposer
Prior document-grounded self-play methods typically condition the proposer on the entire doc-
ument [17, 18], leaving the clinical focus implicit during the generation process. Conceptually,

Chunking offers a crude focus proxy, but can remove the temporal or treatment context needed for
faithful medical questions. We therefore make z explicit as a document-grounded knowledge point: a
discrete clinical focus in the report that guides what the proposer should ask about, while the full
report remains available as context.
Knowledge-Point Extraction.Before self-play, for each document d∈ D, we use the proposer
to extract candidate knowledge points from the source report, remove redundant or overlapping
candidates, and retain at most M of them, denoted as K(d) ={k 1, . . . , kM }. Each knowledge point
km summarizes a clinically meaningful aspect of the case, such as a symptom progression, diagnostic
clue, or outcome pattern, and is supported by explicit evidence in the source document, as illustrated
in Appendix Figure 6. We keep K(d) fixed throughout self-play to provide stable clinical focuses
and avoid introducing additional non-stationarity into proposer optimization. Details of the extraction
procedure are provided in Appendix C.3.

3.2 Rubric-Based Feedback from the Reasoner
As discussed in Section 2, answer accuracy mainly reflects a question’s difficulty for the current
reasoner, but does not necessarily capture whether the question is useful for RL training. Therefore,
CasePlay evaluates the generated question with two reward components:learnability, estimated
from the reasoner’s answer accuracy without access to the source report, andquality, judged by
rubric-based criteria using the target answer and proposer-provided reasoning chain.
Learnability Reward.Given a proposer-generated multiple-choice question q with target answer y,
the reasoner samples Nacc answers to q without access to the source case report, yielding an estimated
accuracyacc(q). Following [14], the learnability reward is defined as:
rlearn = 1−2|acc(q)−0.5|,(5)
which is maximized when acc(q) = 0.5 , encouraging the proposer to generate questions that are
neither too easy nor too hard for the current reasoner.

in the reasoning they elicit. To assess question quality, we ask the reasoner to judge the generated
question together with the target answer y and proposer-provided reasoning chain c. With the
proposer-provided answer and reasoning chain, the reasoner can more directly assess whether the
intended reasoning path is coherent and whether the options are discriminative, without inferring the
rationale from the question alone. Formally, the quality reward is calculated as:

4 Experiments
4.1 Setup
Models and Baselines.We evaluate CasePlay on two model families and multiple scales, including
Qwen3-4B-Base [28], Qwen3-8B-Base [28], and OctoThinker-3B-Hybrid-Base [29]. We compare
against two categories of baselines. The first category consists of recent medical reasoning models,
including HuatuoGPT-o1-8B [2], AlphaMed-8B [30], and Fleming-R1-7B [31]. The second category
consists of self-play methods, including R-Zero [14], SPICE [17], and DARC [18]. Details of baseline
implementations are provided in Appendix D.1.
Training Data.For training data collection, we collect open-access case reports from PubMed
Central (PMC [32]) published between 2020 and 2025. We apply a strict filtering pipeline to remove
incomplete reports, image-dominant articles, and documents with potential benchmark leakage,
yielding a final corpus of 4,281 case reports. For each filtered document, we extract up to M= 4
knowledge points to support knowledge-conditioned question generation. To ensure stable instruction
following and output formatting, we construct a small SFT warm-start dataset from the training case
reports. Both CasePlay and all self-play baselines use this dataset for warm-start initialization to
ensure a fair comparison. Additional details of training data collection are provided in Appendix C.2.
Evaluation.We evaluate CasePlay on seven benchmarks across three categories. First, we test
whether our method improves performance on expert-level medical reasoning benchmarks, including
MedXpert-Text [33], MedBullets-OP4/OP5 [34], and MedQA-USMLE [35]. Second, we evaluate
clinical case reasoning with LiveClin [25], which focuses on patient narratives and requires integrating
case-specific clues from clinical context. Third, we evaluate transfer to scientific QA using MMLU-
Pro [36] (Health and Biology subsets) and GPQA [37] (Genetics and Molecular Biology subset). For
each benchmark question, we sample 8 responses and report the average accuracy as the final metric.
Detailed benchmark descriptions are provided in Appendix D.2.
Implementation Details.We train CasePlay for 15 iterations, with each iteration consisting of
10 proposer-update steps and 10 reasoner-update steps. The rubric weight λ is set to 0.2. For both
the proposer and the reasoner, we use a training batch size of 128 and a learning rate of 1×10 −6.
During training rollouts, we use temperature 1.0 and top- p 0.95, with a maximum input length of
4,096 tokens and a maximum output length of 8,192 tokens. During evaluation, we set the sampling
temperature to 0.6 and top-p to 0.95, while keeping the other generation settings unchanged. Full
experimental hyperparameters are listed in Appendix D.3.

4.2 Performance Comparison
As shown in Table 2, CasePlay achieves the best performance on all seven benchmarks. Compared
with the SFT warm-start model, CasePlay improves the average score from 63.1% to 66.2%, yielding
a +3.1 point gain. The largest gain appears on LiveClin [25] (+6.3 points), because its patient-case
narratives require integrating symptoms, diagnostic clues, and treatment context, which matches
the reasoning ability improved by self-play over clinical case reports. Among self-play baselines,
R-Zero does not use external documents during question generation and slightly underperforms the
warm-start model on average. Compared with document-grounded baselines such as SPICE and
DARC, CasePlay achieves stronger gains, indicating that medical self-play benefits from broader
coverage of case-specific knowledge points and quality-aware proposer feedback.

Knowledge-Conditioned Generation Diversifies Within-Case Questions.As shown in Fig-
ure 3(a), we randomly sample 10% of the training documents and visualize the reasoner’s accuracy

by 1.3 point on average, improving from 64.9 to 66.2 as shown in Table 2.

than CasePlay, and also underperforms other document-grounded self-play methods. This result

4.4 Hyperparameter Sensitivity
Impact of the Number of Knowledge Points.The number of extracted knowledge points controls
the granularity of clinical focus during question generation. We vary the maximum number of
knowledge points per document, including the no-knowledge-point setting, and report the results
in Figure 5(a). With too few knowledge points, the proposer conditions generation on a limited set
of clinical focuses, leading to lower document coverage and weaker performance. As M increases,
document coverage ratio improves, rising from 27% at M= 1 to 43% at M= 8 . However, when M
becomes larger than 4, document coverage improves only marginally while downstream accuracy
begins to decline, indicating that too many knowledge points can make the clinical focuses overly

5 Conclusion
In this paper, we show that directly applying document-grounded self-play RL to medical corpora is
limited by two failure modes:knowledge-point fixationanddifficulty-quality mismatch. To address
these issues, we introduce CasePlay, which makes the clinical focus of question generation explicit
through knowledge-point conditioning and augments proposer optimization with rubric-based quality
feedback from the reasoner. Across seven medically related benchmarks, CasePlay consistently
improves the warm-start model and outperforms existing self-play baselines, with especially strong
gains on clinical case reasoning. Further ablations and generalization experiments support the
robustness of these gains. These results demonstrate that CasePlay provides a scalable way to turn
clinical case reports into continual training signals for improving medical reasoning.

A Related Work
RL for LLM Post-Training.RL has become increasingly important in LLM post-training, es-
pecially for improving reasoning in domains where rewards can be automatically verified [39–43].
Recent representative works, such as OpenAI-o1 [44] and DeepSeek-R1 [45], rely on large amounts
of synthesized or curated training data with verifiable rewards, enabling effective RL training but
often requiring substantial effort for data construction, verification, and filtering. Another line of work
explores more weakly supervised or unsupervised alternatives, deriving training signals either from
the model’s own prior, such as majority voting [46] and semantic similarity [47], or from unlabeled
text [48–50]. The former can be brittle when model confidence is misaligned with correctness [51],
while the latter seeks to extract supervision directly from raw text and thereby provides an alternative
source of training signal. For example, Golden Goose [49] extends the fill-in-the-middle paradigm to
construct supervision from document context, while Quiet-STaR [50] and RLPT [52] optimize future
tokens as supervision targets. These developments motivate our setting: medicine contains abundant
high-quality unlabeled clinical text, making it a natural domain for studying whether self-play RL
can convert real clinical corpora into useful supervision for medical reasoning.
RL for Medical Reasoning Models.A growing body of work applies RL post-training to medical
reasoning and reports promising gains on medical benchmarks [ 2, 11, 53]. HuatuoGPT-o1 [ 2]
is among the earliest efforts in this direction, using exam-style multiple-choice questions such
as MedQA-USMLE [ 35] and MedMCQA [ 54] as verifiable training data. Fleming-R1 [ 31] and
MedReason [53] further synthesize QA-style training instances by extracting medical entities and
relations to construct medical knowledge graphs. AlphaMed [30] builds a carefully curated medical
RL dataset and highlights the importance of informative questions and diverse difficulty levels for
effective training. MedCaseReasoning [55] identifies clinical case reports as a valuable source of
medical reasoning data, but converts them into a static supervised dataset through extensive offline
processing. Despite their encouraging progress, these methods predominantly rely on fixed offline
supervision, including exam-style QA pairs, curated benchmarks, and synthesized reasoning datasets.
In contrast, CasePlay treats clinical case reports not as a source of static annotations, but as grounding
documents in an adaptive self-play loop that dynamically generates training questions for the current
reasoner.
Self-Play RL.Self-play has long been a central paradigm in RL, with early successes in games
such as AlphaZero [56], where models improve by repeatedly competing against themselves and
refining their policies over iterations. Recent work extends this idea to LLM post-training. Absolute
Zero [13] and R-Zero [14] are among the earliest attempts to cast post-training as a co-evolutionary
process between a problem proposer drawing on internal knowledge and a solver, enabling continual
improvement without manually curated datasets. Subsequent methods introduce additional compo-
nents into this loop. SPICE [ 17] grounds problem generation in unlabeled text, while WIST [ 21]
explores tree-structured self-play for acquiring domain-relevant knowledge from the open web. De-
spite this progress, existing self-play methods leave two limitations that are especially important in
document-grounded medical reasoning. First, they typically do not explicitly control coverage over
distinct knowledge points within a source document, which can cause the proposer to repeatedly
anchor on a small subset of salient clues. Second, proposer optimization is still driven primarily by
solver performance, which reflects difficulty but provides limited supervision over whether a ques-
tion is clinically well-formed and useful for RL training. Motivated by these limitations, CasePlay
addresses document-grounded medical self-play through knowledge-conditioned question generation
and rubric-based quality feedback.

C Implementation Details
C.1 Overview
This section provides implementation details for CasePlay. We begin by describing the construction
of the unlabeled clinical case-report corpus from the internet, including document-level filtering and
preprocessing (Section C.2). We then describe how we extract a fixed set of document-grounded

C.2 Data Preparation
We collect open-access clinical case reports from the internet published between 2020 and 2025.
We apply a three-stage filtering pipeline to ensure that each retained document contains sufficient
text-grounded clinical evidence for question generation.First, we parse the raw XML files and discard
reports that cannot be parsed, lack full text, have incomplete formatting, or are too short (fewer than
500 tokens).Second, we perform corpus-level filtering and deduplication. We first remove duplicate
