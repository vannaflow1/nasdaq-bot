From Black-Scholes to Jump-Diffusion: A Narrative 
Review of Option pricing Model Evolution 

Guangyu Wang* 
Institute of Economics, Wuhan University of Technology, Wuhan, China 
*Corresponding author: Guangyu Wang. 

Abstract 
Option pricing theory is a cornerstone of modern financial engineering, but the classical Black -Scholes 
framework relies on continuous price trajectories, constant volatility, and lognormally distributed returns. 
These assumptions are difficult to reconcile with empirical features such as volatility smiles, fat-tailed returns, 
volatility clustering, and abrupt price movements. This review examines the evolution of Option pricing 
models from the Black-Scholes benchmark to stochastic-volatility, jump-diffusion, stochastic-volatility-jump, 
and affine jump-diffusion specifications. It shows that richer models generally improve cross-sectional pricing 
accuracy, especially for short -maturity and out -of-the-money options, but that this improvement is 
accompanied by higher parameter uncertainty, calibration complexity, and hedging instability. The review 
further emphasizes that evidence from emerging markets, particularly China’s domestic derivatives market, 
remains comparatively limited and may require models that a ccount for liquidity constraints, retail 
participation, and clustered jump behavior. 
Keywords 
option pricing, jump-diffusion models, stochastic volatility, affine processes, emerging market options 

1. Introduction 
Option pricing theory transformed modern finance by providing a rigorous mechanism for valuing 
derivative securities and managing nonlinear risk. The Black-Scholes (B-S) model [1], together with Merton's 
rational Option pricing framework [2], established t he no -arbitrage benchmark under continuous trading, 
constant volatility, and lognormally distributed returns. However, market evidence soon showed that these 
assumptions were too restrictive. Empirical studies found systematic pricing errors and implied -volatility 
patterns inconsistent with constant volatility, particularly for out-of-the-money options and after major market 
stress episodes [3,4]. 
The purpose of this review is therefore not to restate the standard chronology of Option pricing models, but 
to synthesize why each structural extension emerged and where it remains limited. Existing reviews and 
empirical summaries have tended to focus on mature U.S. and European option markets, while the institutional 
and microstructure features of emerging markets have received less integrated attention. This gap matters

because jump clustering, liquidity frictions, and retail-driven trading may affect option prices differently from 
the patterns observed in highly liquid index -option markets. Accordingly, this paper connects the main 
theoretical line from jump -diffusion [5], stochastic volatility [6], stochastic -volatility-jump models [7], and 
affine jump-diffusion frameworks [8] with transform -based valuation methods [9] and recent evidence from 
China's option markets [10]. 
This review addresses three research questions: (1) how have Option pricing models evolved structurally 
in response to the limitations of the B-S model? (2) What empirical evidence supports the superiority of jump-
diffusion or stochastic-volatility-jump models over simpler alternatives? (3) What practical gaps remain when 
these models are applied to emerging markets, particularly China? 
The research value of this paper lies in linking model design to market applicability. By comparing pricing 
accuracy, hedging stability, parameter risk, and emerging -market suitability within one review, the paper 
clarifies why more complex models are not automatically better in practice and identifies concrete directions 
for China-based empirical testing.

2.2 Beyond Black-Scholes: Jumps and Stochastic Volatility 
To bring theory closer to observed market behavior, later models relaxed the B -S assumptions along two 
main dimensions: discontinuous price jumps and time -varying volatility. These two extensions address 
different empirical failures. Jump models focus on r are but large price movements and hence are directly 
related to tail risk. Stochastic-volatility models focus on persistent changes in conditional variance and hence 
are better suited to volatility clustering. Neither extension is complete on its own, whic h explains why later 
research moved toward integrated specifications. 
Jump-diffusion models. Merton [5] augmented the diffusion process with a Poisson -driven jump 
component to capture discontinuous price changes caused by earnings surprises, macroeconomic 
announcements, or geopolitical shocks. The dynamics can be expressed as follows: 
 𝑑𝑆𝑡 = 𝜇𝑆𝑡𝑑𝑡 + 𝜎𝑆𝑡𝑑𝑊𝑡 + 𝑆𝑡−𝑑𝐽𝑡 (3) 
where the jump component is governed by a Poisson process with intensity, and the log -jump size is 
commonly assumed to follow a normal distribution. The advantage of this specification is its direct treatment 
of tail events; its limitation is that standard normally distributed jumps may not fully capture asymmetry or 
clustered jump arrivals. Later double -exponential jump specifications partially address this asymmetry by 
allowing different probabilities for upward and downward jumps [11]. 
Stochastic volatility. Heston [6] addressed the constant -volatility assumption by modeling variance as a 
mean-reverting square-root process, building on the broader stochastic-volatility approach to option valuation 
[12]. The process can be written as follows:

2.3 Limitations of the B-S Framework 
The need for these extensions becomes evident when B-S assumptions are compared with empirical market 
data. First, realized returns display leptokurtosis, meaning that the empirical distribution has fatter tails and a 
sharper peak than the normal distribution implied by the B-S model. Second, implied volatility is not constant 
across strike prices; instead, it often forms a U-shaped volatility smile or a downward-sloping volatility smirk. 
Deep in-the-money and out-of-the-money options therefore command high er implied volatilities than at-the-
money options. Third, the same model can perform differently across markets: a specification calibrated to a 
liquid U.S. index-option market may be less reliable in an emerging market where trading activity is thinner, 
investor composition differs, and jumps may occur in clusters. These deviations show that model choice should 
be judged not only by theoretical elegance but also by empirical fit, parameter stability, and market setting. 
2.4 The Affine Jump-Diffusion (AJD) Unification 
Duffie, Pan, and Singleton [8] unified the above ideas through the affine jump -diffusion framework. The 
core innovation is that state variables such as volatility, interest rates, or jump intensity can be specified in an 
affine form, so the conditional cha racteristic function of the asset price remains exponentially affine in those 
state variables. This structure makes it possible to combine stochastic volatility, jumps, and sometimes 
stochastic interest rates in one internally consistent model without losing analytical tractability. 
The importance of the AJD framework lies in its balance between flexibility and computability. Fourier 
inversion and related transform methods can recover option prices from the characteristic function much faster 
than repeated simulation, while still allo wing the model to fit volatility smiles and term structures [9]. 
Subsequent cosine-expansion methods further improved numerical efficiency for European-style options [13]. 
This is why AJD-type models became a mainstream framework in derivatives research: they provide a practical 
bridge between rich economic dynamics and feasible calibration. 
3. Literature Review 
The literature can be organized into four stages, but the main issue is not chronology alone. Each stage 
reflects a specific trade -off between empirical realism, analytical tractability, and implementation risk. The 
following review therefore combines hist orical development with evaluative comments on what each stage 
solved and what it left unresolved. 
3.1 The Foundational Era (1973–1980S) 
The decade following the B-S model focused on establishing the no-arbitrage pricing paradigm. Black and 
Scholes [1] and Merton [2] provided the theoretical foundation under continuous trading and constant volatility. 
Although this framework was revolutiona ry, empirical tests by Rubinstein [3] and Jackwerth and Rubinstein 
[4] showed that the B-S model systematically mispriced out-of-the-money options. These errors were closely 
related to implied-volatility smirks, which suggested that the market was pricing crash risk and time -varying 
volatility even when the basic model did not allow for them. 
This foundational era is best understood as a benchmark -building stage rather than a complete empirical 
explanation. Its main contribution was to define the no -arbitrage logic and replicating -portfolio method that 
later models retained. Its main weakness w as that the assumptions required for a closed -form solution also 
removed the very features--fat tails, skewness, and changing volatility--that are central to real option prices.

3.2 Single-Factor Extensions (1980S–1990S) 
Researchers first addressed B -S failures through single -factor extensions. Merton [5] introduced a jump -
diffusion model and derived a tractable solution for European options with discontinuous price movements. 
Kou [11] later improved the jump specification by allowing asymmetric double -exponential jumps, which 
better reflect the different market responses to upward and downward shocks. In parallel, Heston [6] developed 
a stochastic-volatility model that relaxed the constant -volatility assumption and used ch aracteristic functions 
to obtain a semi-analytical solution. Hull and White [12] also showed that stochastic volatility can materially 
affect option values when volatility risk is not constant. 
The contribution of this stage was to identify two separate sources of B -S misspecification: discontinuous 
returns and random variance. However, single-factor extensions remained incomplete. A pure jump-diffusion 
model can capture crash risk but often trea ts volatility too rigidly, whereas a pure stochastic -volatility model 
can capture volatility clustering but may miss sudden discontinuities. This complementarity created the 
theoretical motivation for multi-factor integration. 
3.3 Multi-Factor Integration and Analytical Tractability (1990S–2000S) 
As neither jumps nor stochastic volatility alone fully explained market dynamics, researchers began to 
integrate both features. Bates [7] combined Heston's stochastic -volatility framework with Merton-type jumps 
to form an SVJ model and showed that this hyb rid specification explained exchange-rate option smiles more 
effectively than single -factor alternatives. Computational barriers were reduced when Duffie, Pan, and 
Singleton [8] formalized the AJD framework, which allowed researchers to apply Fourier -transform methods 
[9] and cosine expansions [13]. These techniques substantially reduced the computational burden associated 
with option valuation, especially relative to simulation-intensive methods. 
The key contribution of multi -factor integration was synthesis: it recognized that tail risk and volatility 
clustering are not competing explanations but interacting channels. The limitation is that every added factor 
increases calibration difficulty. SVJ and AJD models may fit option surfaces better, but they also require more 
parameters and stronger assumptions about jump intensity, jump -size distribution, and volatility dynamics. 
This makes their out-of-sample and hedging performance less certain than their in-sample pricing performance. 
3.4 Empirical Validation and High-Frequency Advancements (2000S–Present) 
Once tractable models became available, empirical validation expanded rapidly. Bakshi, Cao, and Chen 
[14] and Broadie, Chernov, and Johannes [15] found that models incorporating stochastic volatility and jumps 
can substantially reduce pricing errors relati ve to simpler diffusion models, especially for options away from 
the money. Eraker [16] further showed that jumps are important for reconciling evidence from spot and option 
prices. More recent research has shifted attention toward model features that standard AJD specifications may 
not fully capture. Pan and Ma [10] report self -exciting jump behavior in China's CSI 300 ETF options, while 
rough-volatility research argues that volatility can exhibit rough, persistent behavior at high frequency [17]. 
This recent literature shows that the standard AJD framework is powerful but not final. Its common 
Poisson-jump structure may understate jump clustering, and its Markovian volatility structure may miss rough 
volatility patterns. Hawkes-type jumps and rough-volatility extensions therefore represent more than technical 
refinements; they respond to observable features of modern option markets, particularly markets where 
liquidity shocks and investor herding can make jump arrivals self-reinforcing.

simultaneously. This is why SVJ-type models are particularly useful for short-maturity and out-of-the-money

At the same time, model superiority is conditional. A model that fits the implied -volatility surface more 
closely may not be superior for every purpose. For pricing, richer state dynamics help explain curvature and 
skew. For hedging, however, additional ju mp and volatility-risk factors create exposures that cannot always 
be replicated continuously. The empirical dominance of SVJ and AJD models should therefore be interpreted 
as strongest for pricing and calibration, not as a general guarantee of hedging superiority. 
4.2 Contradictions, Trade-Offs, and Limitations 
The main contradiction in the literature is the trade -off between pricing accuracy and implementation 
reliability. Complex models often reduce in-sample pricing errors, but they require estimates of volatility mean 
reversion, volatility of volatility, jump intensity, jump-size distribution, risk premia, and sometimes stochastic 
interest-rate parameters. These estimates can be unstable when option data are noisy, sparse, or concentrated 
in a small set of maturit ies. Model uncertainty can therefore become economically important even when the 
model is mathematically coherent [18]. 
The mature -market versus emerging -market distinction is central to this trade -off. In mature U.S. and 
European index -option markets, deeper liquidity and broader institutional participation make continuous 
calibration more feasible. In emerging markets, including China, option trading may be more affected by retail 
behavior, policy news, margin constraints, and intermittent liquidity. These features can produce clustered 
jumps and discontinuous changes in implied volatility that standard Gaussian or Poisson assumptions do not 
fully capture [10]. As a result, the most suitable model for China may not simply be the most complex imported 
model, but a specification that balances jump clustering, rough volatility, data availability, and recalibration 
stability. 
4.3 Future Research Directions 
The limitations identified above point to three China-relevant research trajectories that can be developed as 
complete empirical programs rather than as isolated technical extensions. 
First, high-frequency calibration should be used to test whether intraday volatility bursts and jump arrivals 
in Chinese ETF and index options differ from patterns documented in mature markets. Rather than relying 
only on daily close -to-close data, future work can use intraday transaction and quote data to separate 
continuous volatility from discrete jumps and to evaluate whether realized-volatility measures improve short-
maturity option pricing [19]. This direction is especially relevant for China because policy announcements, 
trading halts, and retail order flow may create concentrated intraday risk. 
Second, machine-learning-assisted estimation can support model calibration without replacing economic 
structure. Neural networks or regularized learning methods can be used to identify regime shifts, select stable 
parameters, or approximate pricing functions when direct optimization is computationally expensive [20]. For 
China's option market, a useful application would be to combine no-arbitrage model restrictions with machine-
learning tools that detect liquidity regimes and adjust calibration windows accordingly.

Third, rough-volatility and Hawkes-type jump extensions should be tested jointly. Rough-volatility models 
address persistent high -frequency volatility behavior, while Hawkes -type processes allow one jump to raise 
the likelihood of subsequent jumps [10,17]. A promising China-focused study could compare standard AJD, 
rough-volatility, and self -exciting-jump specifications using the same option panel, then evaluate not only 
pricing errors but also hedging performance and parameter stability. 
5. Conclusion 
This review has traced the evolution of option pricing theory from the elegant but restrictive Black-Scholes 
model to more flexible stochastic-volatility, jump-diffusion, SVJ, and AJD frameworks. The central pattern is 
clear: each new generation of models emerged because the previous generation failed to capture an empirically 
important market feature. Jumps address tail events, stochastic volatility addresses volatility clustering, and 
affine transform methods make richer models computationally feasible. 
Nevertheless, greater model richness does not eliminate practical risk. The same features that improve 
pricing accuracy can also increase parameter instability, overfitting risk, and hedging difficulty. This tension 
is especially important in emerging markets, where liquidity conditions, retail participation, policy shocks, and 
clustered jump behavior may differ from those in mature markets. Future research should therefore move 
beyond importing mature-market specifications and should test China-specific combinations of high-frequency 
calibration, machine-learning-assisted estimation, rough volatility, and self-exciting jumps. Such work would 
better connect theoretical elegance with real-world market frictions.
